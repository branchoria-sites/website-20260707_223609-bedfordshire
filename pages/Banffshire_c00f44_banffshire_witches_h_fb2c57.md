## Introduction

Witches, healers and old cures in [Banffshire]({{ 'what-mysteries-shaped-banffshires/' | relative_url }}) belong to a tradition where illness, misfortune and unusual events were often explained through a mixture of religion, local knowledge, herbal remedies and supernatural belief. The county’s stories do not simply divide people into “witches” and “victims”: older communities could distinguish between harmful witchcraft, protective charms and the work of respected local healers or “canny” people who were asked to help with sickness in people or animals. Evidence for Banffshire’s traditions survives mainly through folklore collections, local histories and wider Scottish records of belief rather than through a single famous witch legend.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://banffshirefieldclub.org.uk/PDFs/BOOK_3/Banffshire_Field_Club_Transactions_1887-1893_-_1890_The_Horse_in_Scottish_Folklore_WM.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: banffshirefieldclub.org.uk">[Banffshire Field Club]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">banffshirefieldclub.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Banffshire Field Club Transactions</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Banffshire Field ClubTransactionsFebruary 11, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: February 11, 2026</span></span></span>

For readers exploring Banffshire folklore, the important question is not whether these practices worked in a modern medical sense, but what they reveal about how communities understood danger, illness and care. A charm, a whispered cure or a story about a witch often reflected real fears: losing livestock, unexplained pain, childhood illness, failed crops or tensions between neighbours. Across Scotland, the boundary between a healer and a suspected witch could be fragile, especially when unusual knowledge or unexplained success attracted suspicion.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www-staging.witches.hss.ed.ac.uk/database" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: www-staging.witches.hss.ed.ac.uk">[The Survey of Scottish Witchcraft]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">www-staging.witches.hss.ed.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Survey of Scottish Witchcraft Database &#124; The Survey of Scottish Witchcraft</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Survey of Scottish Witchcraft Database &#124; The Survey of Scottish Witchcraft</span></span></span>

## Witchcraft memories in local communities

### Witches as explanations for trouble and illness

In traditional Banffshire storytelling, witches were often remembered less as figures of organised magical practice and more as explanations for things that went wrong. A sick animal, a sudden weakness, a failed household task or an unexpected illness could be linked to unseen forces. One recorded Banffshire tradition collected by the Banffshire Field Club describes horses believed to have been exhausted by witches riding them at night. In the story, a local “canny woman” was called to restore the animal, showing how ideas about witchcraft and healing could exist side by side within the same community belief system.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://banffshirefieldclub.org.uk/PDFs/BOOK_3/Banffshire_Field_Club_Transactions_1887-1893_-_1890_The_Horse_in_Scottish_Folklore_WM.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: banffshirefieldclub.org.uk">[Banffshire Field Club]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">banffshirefieldclub.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Banffshire Field Club Transactions</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Banffshire Field ClubTransactionsFebruary 11, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: February 11, 2026</span></span></span>

This pattern was common across Scotland. The historical record of Scottish witchcraft accusations shows that communities often used witchcraft accusations to interpret personal conflicts, illness and social problems. The Survey of Scottish Witchcraft, created by historians at the University of Edinburgh, records thousands of accusations between 1563 and 1736 and shows how beliefs about witchcraft were connected to wider social and cultural concerns rather than only to ideas about magic.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www-staging.witches.hss.ed.ac.uk/about" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: www-staging.witches.hss.ed.ac.uk">[The Survey of Scottish Witchcraft]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">www-staging.witches.hss.ed.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Survey of Scottish Witchcraft About &#124; The Survey of Scottish Witchcraft</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Survey of Scottish Witchcraft About &#124; The Survey of Scottish Witchcraft</span></span></span>

Banffshire’s memories should therefore be read carefully. A story about a witch may preserve an old belief, a warning tale, a family memory or a later retelling rather than evidence that a specific event happened exactly as described. Folklore often preserves the fears and values of the people who told it.

### The difference between a witch and a healer

One of the most important distinctions in Scottish folk belief was between someone thought to cause harm and someone believed to remove it. Folk healers, charmers and wise people could be respected members of rural communities because they offered help when official medicine was limited or unavailable. Their knowledge might include herbs, prayers, spoken charms and protective customs.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nms.ac.uk/discover-catalogue/from-amulets-to-elf-bolts-10-scottish-charms" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nms.ac.uk">[National Museums Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nms.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Museums Scotland From amulets to elf bolts: 11 Scottish Charms</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Museums ScotlandFrom amulets to elf bolts: 11 Scottish CharmsJanuary 6, 2025 — We delve into eleven objects and charms that peop...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 6, 2025</span></span></span>

However, the same kinds of practices could create suspicion. Someone who knew unusual remedies, worked with plants, treated livestock or claimed special knowledge might be viewed positively by neighbours but negatively by authorities or rivals. This tension helps explain why the modern image of the “witch” can be misleading when applied to historical rural Scotland. Some people remembered as magical practitioners were not seen locally as evil figures but as people with specialised knowledge.

## Healing charms and folk knowledge

### Remedies between medicine and belief

Traditional healing in Banffshire formed part of a wider Scottish system in which physical remedies and spiritual protection were often combined. People might use plants, household treatments, prayers, objects with protective meaning or spoken formulas. The National Museums Scotland collections highlight how Scottish communities used charms and amulets to protect against illness and misfortune, showing that healing traditions often involved both practical and symbolic actions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nms.ac.uk/discover-catalogue/from-amulets-to-elf-bolts-10-scottish-charms" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nms.ac.uk">[National Museums Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nms.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Museums Scotland From amulets to elf bolts: 11 Scottish Charms</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Museums ScotlandFrom amulets to elf bolts: 11 Scottish CharmsJanuary 6, 2025 — We delve into eleven objects and charms that peop...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 6, 2025</span></span></span>

For rural communities, this made sense within the knowledge available at the time. A person suffering from unexplained pain or a child who repeatedly became ill might be treated with ordinary remedies while also being protected against what people believed could be supernatural causes. Concepts such as harmful influence, the evil eye or attacks by supernatural beings appeared alongside everyday attempts to maintain health and safety.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nms.ac.uk/discover-catalogue/from-amulets-to-elf-bolts-10-scottish-charms" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nms.ac.uk">[National Museums Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nms.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Museums Scotland From amulets to elf bolts: 11 Scottish Charms</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Museums ScotlandFrom amulets to elf bolts: 11 Scottish CharmsJanuary 6, 2025 — We delve into eleven objects and charms that peop...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 6, 2025</span></span></span>

### Charms, animals and protection

Banffshire folklore also preserves links between healing and the protection of animals. Farming communities depended heavily on horses, cattle and other livestock, so illness among animals could threaten a household’s survival. The Banffshire Field Club recorded traditions of protective measures against witchcraft, including the use of rowan branches and crosses placed near buildings. These customs show how folk belief operated as a practical system of reassurance as well as a supernatural explanation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://banffshirefieldclub.org.uk/PDFs/BOOK_3/Banffshire_Field_Club_Transactions_1887-1893_-_1890_The_Horse_in_Scottish_Folklore_WM.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: banffshirefieldclub.org.uk">[Banffshire Field Club]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">banffshirefieldclub.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Banffshire Field Club Transactions</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Banffshire Field ClubTransactionsFebruary 11, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: February 11, 2026</span></span></span>

The use of protective objects was not unique to Banffshire. Across Scotland, charms and amulets were used because people believed certain materials, words or rituals could guard against harm. Today these objects are valuable to historians because they reveal how ordinary people responded to uncertainty before modern medicine and scientific explanations became dominant.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nms.ac.uk/discover-catalogue/from-amulets-to-elf-bolts-10-scottish-charms" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nms.ac.uk">[National Museums Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nms.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Museums Scotland From amulets to elf bolts: 11 Scottish Charms</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Museums ScotlandFrom amulets to elf bolts: 11 Scottish CharmsJanuary 6, 2025 — We delve into eleven objects and charms that peop...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 6, 2025</span></span></span>

## Changing views of witches over time

### From feared figures to folklore characters

The meaning of the witch has changed greatly over the centuries. During periods of witch persecution, accusations could have serious consequences, and suspected witches were investigated through legal and religious systems. Later generations often transformed these figures into characters of storytelling, local colour and historical curiosity.

Modern retellings of Banffshire witch traditions usually focus less on accusation and punishment and more on the beliefs behind the stories: the lonely farm, the mysterious healer, the protective charm or the old woman whose knowledge seemed unusual. These versions are part of a broader interest in Scotland’s local folklore landscapes.

The survival of these traditions also depends on collectors and local historians who recorded memories that might otherwise have disappeared. Organisations such as the Banffshire Field Club have preserved local historical material, including accounts of customs and beliefs that help researchers understand how supernatural ideas were woven into everyday life.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://banffshirefieldclub.org.uk/publications.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: banffshirefieldclub.org.uk">[Banffshire Field Club]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">banffshirefieldclub.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Banffshire Field Club BFC &#124; Publications</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Banffshire Field Club BFC &#124; Publications</span></span></span>

### What Banffshire’s witch traditions reveal

Banffshire’s witch and healing traditions are most valuable as records of human experience. They show how communities explained illness, protected homes, cared for animals and managed fears about things they could not easily understand. They also reveal a more complicated picture than the simple image of the “old witch”: the same society that feared harmful magic could also rely on people who carried healing knowledge.

Seen this way, Banffshire’s witches and healers are not just tales of the supernatural. They are part of the county’s social memory, preserving ideas about trust, survival, landscape and the fragile line between accepted knowledge and feared mystery.
