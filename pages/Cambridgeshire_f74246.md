## Introduction

For this page, Cambridgeshire is treated as the historic county shown in the Wikishire/Wikimedia historic-counties framework. That matters because today’s administrative Cambridgeshire is larger than the historic county: modern boundaries include areas such as Huntingdonshire and the Soke of Peterborough, while the historic county includes the Isle of Ely as its northern fenland division. Folklore does not always respect those lines, but the centre of gravity here is historic Cambridgeshire: Cambridge, Ely, Wisbech, the fen villages, the [Gog Magog]({{ 'gog-magog/' | relative_url }}) Hills and the old rural parishes between them.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://wikishire.co.uk/wiki/Cambridgeshire" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wikishire.co.uk">[wikishire.co.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wikishire.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wikishire.co.uk.</span></span></span>

## Where Cambridgeshire folklore lives

Cambridgeshire’s traditions gather around three kinds of place. First is Cambridge itself, where ghost stories cling to colleges, inns, old streets and former religious houses. Second is the fenland north, especially the Isle of Ely, where flat horizons, waterways and isolated farms helped preserve stories of witches, charms, cures and uncanny disturbances. Third is the chalk country south-east of Cambridge, where the Gog Magog Hills and Wandlebury give the county one of its most striking mythic landscapes.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://wikishire.co.uk/wiki/Isle_of_Ely" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wikishire.co.uk">[wikishire.co.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wikishire.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Isle of Ely</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Isle of Ely</span></span></span>

The historic Isle of Ely is especially important. Wikishire describes it as the northernmost part of Cambridgeshire, taking in the hundreds of Wisbech, Witchford and Ely, and as a landscape distinct from the southern county because of its flatness, wide horizons and drained fenland fields. Those conditions are not just geography: they explain why Cambridgeshire folklore so often concerns water, marsh, hidden routes, disease cures, protective magic and the difference between safe domestic space and dangerous open fen.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://wikishire.co.uk/wiki/Isle_of_Ely" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wikishire.co.uk">[Wikishire]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wikishire.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Isle of Ely</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Isle of Ely</span></span></span>

Modern local-government history can confuse readers. Between 1889 and 1965, Cambridgeshire and the Isle of Ely had separate county councils, though the Isle remained part of the historic county in the older county sense. In 1965 they were merged as Cambridgeshire and Isle of Ely, and in 1974 the administrative county was enlarged again to include Huntingdonshire and the Soke of Peterborough. For folklore, that means a famous modern “Cambridgeshire” tradition such as the Whittlesey Straw Bear sits near historic-county border questions, while older Ely, Wisbech and Cambridge material remains central to historic Cambridgeshire itself.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cambridgeshire_and_Isle_of_Ely_County_Council" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Cambridgeshire and Isle of Ely County Council</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cambridgeshire and Isle of Ely County Council</span></span></span>

## Enid Porter and the county’s memory

The key figure in Cambridgeshire folklore is Enid Porter. She was curator of the Cambridge & County Folk Museum, now the Museum of Cambridge, and is repeatedly described by local heritage bodies as a leading authority on Cambridgeshire customs, stories and beliefs. Capturing Cambridge calls her a pioneer of oral history, while the Museum of Cambridge notes that its collections include regionally significant folklore material covering witchcraft, folk customs, local celebrities and music.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://capturingcambridge.org/online-exhibitions-of-prior-years/herstory/enidporter/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: capturingcambridge.org">[capturingcambridge.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">capturingcambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on capturingcambridge.org.</span></span></span>

Porter’s importance lies in how she collected. The Museum guide says she gathered lore in carpenters’ sheds, farmyards, public houses, fields and public transport, sometimes writing notes by hand and sometimes memorising stories where a pencil might have made an informant self-conscious. That makes her work especially valuable for a county where many traditions were not grand literary myths but practical beliefs: how to ward off witches, cure illness, interpret omens, mark courtship, observe fairs and survive fenland life.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://capturingcambridge.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/05/Folk-Museum-Guide-Book-2012.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: capturingcambridge.org">[Capturing Cambridge]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">capturingcambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Folk Museum Guide Book 2012</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Folk Museum Guide Book 2012</span></span></span>

Her main county work, *Cambridgeshire Customs and Folklore*, was first published in 1969. Modern publisher descriptions emphasise its breadth: courtship, marriage, birth and death, trees and plants, nature lore, food and drink, ghosts, witchcraft and cures for disease. The book’s continuing value is that it records Cambridgeshire as a lived belief-world rather than as a set of isolated “spooky stories”.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/details/cambridgeshirecu0000port_o3b6" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[archive.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">cambridgeshirecu0000port o3b6</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">cambridgeshirecu0000port o3b6</span></span></span>

## Witches, imps and household protection

Witchcraft is one of the strongest strands in Cambridgeshire and fenland folklore. The Museum of Cambridge’s educational material says people in Cambridgeshire and the Fenlands strongly believed that witches possessed familiar spirits: small attendant creatures, locally known in the Fens as “imps”, often imagined as cats, mice or toads. The same museum page links these beliefs with the Witchfinder General’s records of familiars with names such as Pyewacket, Grizzel Greedigut and Vinegar Tom.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.museumofcambridge.org.uk/witches-and-their-familiars/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: museumofcambridge.org.uk">[The Museum of Cambridge]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">museumofcambridge.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Museum of Cambridge Witches and their Familiars</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Museum of Cambridge Witches and their Familiars</span></span></span>

This is where local folklore and early modern legal terror overlap. Matthew Hopkins, the self-styled Witchfinder General, operated mainly in East Anglia during the Civil War period, and his methods helped turn familiar spirits into supposed legal evidence. The National Archives holds teaching material on Hopkins, while research on Ely witchcraft places the city within the turbulent religious politics of the 1640s, when witchcraft accusations, radical religion and local fear could reinforce each other.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nationalarchives.gov.uk/education/resources/early-modern-witch-trials/matthew-hopkins-witch-finder/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nationalarchives.gov.uk">[nationalarchives.gov.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nationalarchives.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nationalarchives.gov.uk.</span></span></span>

Cambridgeshire’s witchcraft traditions were not only about trials. They also lived in objects. The Museum of Cambridge holds witch balls, including a vivid blue example made in 1721 at Gamlingay, south-west of Cambridge. The museum explains the local belief attached to it: hung in a window, the refracted light would dazzle witches and stop them entering the house. That detail is important because it shows folklore as practical household technology: an object beautiful enough to display, but also believed to guard a vulnerable boundary.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.museumofcambridge.org.uk/2024/10/folklore-finds-witch-balls/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: museumofcambridge.org.uk">[The Museum of Cambridge]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">museumofcambridge.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Museum of Cambridge Folklore Finds: Witch Balls</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Museum of Cambridge Folklore Finds: Witch Balls</span></span></span>

Other protective objects in the museum’s folklore collection include witch bottles, holed stones, bones, shoes, pots and charms placed in houses, stables and hearths. The guide explains that these were often put in “weak” parts of the building such as doors, chimneys and windows, where a witch or evil influence might be thought able to enter. In Cambridgeshire, the supernatural was therefore not only out on the fen road; it was also imagined at the threshold of the home.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://capturingcambridge.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/05/Folk-Museum-Guide-Book-2012.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: capturingcambridge.org">[Capturing Cambridge]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">capturingcambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Folk Museum Guide Book 2012</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Folk Museum Guide Book 2012</span></span></span>

## Fenland cures, wise women and everyday magic

Cambridgeshire folklore is full of cures that now sound strange, but which made sense in a world of damp fenland conditions, limited medical access and inherited household practice. The Museum of Cambridge guide notes examples from the folklore collection: carrying a potato for rheumatism, using mole paws for the same complaint, carrying hedgehog jaws against toothache, rubbing boiled hedgehog grease on the head for baldness, and using hedgehog cooking water for whooping cough. These are not presented as effective medicine today, but as evidence of how people tried to manage fear, pain and uncertainty with the materials around them.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://capturingcambridge.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/05/Folk-Museum-Guide-Book-2012.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: capturingcambridge.org">[Capturing Cambridge]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">capturingcambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Folk Museum Guide Book 2012</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Folk Museum Guide Book 2012</span></span></span>

The fenland setting matters. Rheumatism, damp, isolation and hard agricultural labour gave practical urgency to charms and cures. The Museum of Cambridge’s Fenland folklore resources place witches, wise women, traditional cures, potions and local plants together, which reflects how ordinary people might not have separated “medicine”, “magic” and “custom” as sharply as modern readers do.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.museumofcambridge.org.uk/resources/family-fun/fenland-traditions-and-folklore/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: museumofcambridge.org.uk">[The Museum of Cambridge]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">museumofcambridge.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Museum of Cambridge Fenland Traditions and Folklore</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Museum of Cambridge Fenland Traditions and Folklore</span></span></span>

This is one reason Cambridgeshire folklore feels more domestic than heroic. The county has saints and giants, but its most characteristic material often concerns small anxieties: illness, courtship, childbirth, protection of animals, luck in marriage, the safety of a house, or the behaviour of a neighbour thought to have uncanny power. Enid Porter’s work is valuable precisely because it preserves these ordinary beliefs before they vanished from living memory.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.routledge.com/Cambridgeshire-Customs-and-Folklore-Pbdirect/Porter/p/book/9781138845435" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: routledge.com">[Routledge]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">routledge.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Cambridgeshire Customs and Folklore Pbdirect</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cambridgeshire Customs and Folklore Pbdirect</span></span></span>

## Black dogs and the East Anglian edge

The best-known supernatural animal associated with the wider East Anglian region is Black Shuck, the great spectral black dog. Black Shuck is most strongly linked with Norfolk and Suffolk, but the tradition extends into the Cambridgeshire Fens. A nineteenth-century account in *Notes and Queries* described “Shuck the Dog-fiend” as a black shaggy dog with fiery eyes and immense size, and said people in East Norfolk and even Cambridgeshire had described seeing it.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_Shuck" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Black Shuck</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Black Shuck</span></span></span>

For Cambridgeshire, Black Shuck is best understood as a border-crossing fenland tradition rather than a monster confined to one parish. The Fens link Norfolk, Suffolk, Cambridgeshire and Lincolnshire through waterways, drove roads, drainage works and shared seasonal labour. A spectral hound moving through this region fits the landscape: wide, dark, difficult to police, full of causeways, churchyards and lonely routes.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_Shuck" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Black Shuck</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Black Shuck</span></span></span>

Black dog traditions vary across Britain. Sometimes the dog is a death omen; sometimes a guardian; sometimes a churchyard spirit; sometimes a frightening but morally ambiguous presence. That variability matters for Cambridgeshire because modern retellings often flatten Black Shuck into a simple “demon dog”, while older folklore is less tidy. The same creature-type could warn, threaten, guard or simply mark a place as uncanny.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://tetzoo.com/blog/2022/8/13/legend-of-the-black-dog" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: tetzoo.com">[Tetrapod Zoology]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">tetzoo.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">legend of the black dog</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">legend of the black dog</span></span></span>

## Ely: saints, relics and resistance

Ely gives Cambridgeshire its strongest sacred landscape. St Etheldreda, the seventh-century East Anglian princess and abbess associated with Ely, became one of the best-known early English saints. Ely Cathedral states that pilgrims came to Ely seeking miracles from her relics, which were believed to have healing powers, especially for throat and neck diseases.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.elycathedral.org/about/history-heritage/st-etheldreda" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: elycathedral.org">[Ely Cathedral]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">elycathedral.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Ely Cathedral St. Etheldreda</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Ely Cathedral St. Etheldreda</span></span></span>

The saint’s story belongs partly to church history and partly to popular religious culture. Pilgrimage, relics, healing and named wells all sit at the meeting point of official devotion and folk practice. Modern Ely still remembers Etheldreda through cathedral heritage and the Catholic shrine, while local well traditions also connect her sanctity with water, healing and place.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.elycathedral.org/about/history-heritage/st-etheldreda" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: elycathedral.org">[elycathedral.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">elycathedral.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Ely Cathedral St. Etheldreda</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Ely Cathedral St. Etheldreda</span></span></span>

Ely is also tied to Hereward the Wake, the legendary Anglo-Saxon resistance figure after the Norman Conquest. Historic UK describes Ely as Hereward’s stronghold, using the natural defences of the Isle of Ely as a base for resistance to William the Conqueror. In folklore terms, Hereward is not simply a military figure: he becomes a fenland hero, someone whose story depends on marsh, water, local knowledge and the idea of an island community resisting outside power.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.historic-uk.com/HistoryMagazine/DestinationsUK/Ely/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: historic-uk.com">[Historic UK]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">historic-uk.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on historic-uk.com.</span></span></span>

This is one of Cambridgeshire’s clearest examples of history turning into legend. There was real post-Conquest resistance around Ely, but later storytelling enlarged Hereward into a figure of cunning, endurance and local defiance. His legend works because Ely itself feels made for it: a high sacred place rising out of the fen, both refuge and beacon.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://wikishire.co.uk/wiki/Isle_of_Ely" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wikishire.co.uk">[Wikishire]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wikishire.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Isle of Ely</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Isle of Ely</span></span></span>

## Gog Magog Hills and the lost giant

South-east of Cambridge, the Gog Magog Hills give Cambridgeshire a very different mythic atmosphere. Wandlebury, or Gogmagog, is a flat-topped hill about 8 km south-east of Cambridge, with an Iron Age hillfort and a long history of antiquarian speculation. Antiquity’s project gallery summarises the key issue: there were reported chalk figures at Wandlebury at different times, including giant figures and a later supposed goddess figure traced by T. C. Lethbridge in the 1950s, but these are now covered, lost or disputed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://antiquity.ac.uk/projgall/meadows343" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: antiquity.ac.uk">[antiquity.ac.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">antiquity.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on antiquity.ac.uk.</span></span></span>

The giant tradition is older than Lethbridge. Accounts refer to a giant figure connected with the Gog Magog Hills, and later local tradition said a single giant was visible from Sawston until the nineteenth century. The evidence is fragmentary, but the persistence of the name and the hill’s association with giants make Wandlebury one of Cambridgeshire’s most important legendary landscapes.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.mythicalbritain.co.uk/post/t-c-lethbridge-and-the-lost-gogmagog-giants-of-wandlebury" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: mythicalbritain.co.uk">[mythicalbritain.co.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">mythicalbritain.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">mythical-britain T C Lethbridge and the lost Gogmagog giant of Wandlebury</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">mythical-britain T C Lethbridge and the lost Gogmagog giant of Wandlebury</span></span></span>

The safest reading is not that a prehistoric giant has been proven, but that Wandlebury became a place where archaeology, student custom, local story, chalk geology and national giant legends met. The hillfort is real; the hill figures are uncertain; the name “Gog Magog” links the site to the wider British giant tradition. For readers, that distinction is crucial: the folklore matters even where the archaeology remains contested.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://antiquity.ac.uk/projgall/meadows343" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: antiquity.ac.uk">[antiquity.ac.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">antiquity.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on antiquity.ac.uk.</span></span></span>

## Ghosts in Cambridge and the villages

Cambridge is one of the county’s richest ghost-story settings, partly because its colleges and old religious sites provide the perfect architecture for haunting traditions. Local ghost stories attach to college rooms, chapels, old inns and former monastic places, and they often blur verifiable history with rumour. A Stapleford History Society account, reporting a talk by ghost-story researcher Robert Halliday, notes tales at Corpus Christi College alongside the real 1632 death of Henry Butts, Master of Corpus, who hanged himself during a plague period.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://historysociety.staplefordvillage.org.uk/cambridge-ghosts/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: historysociety.staplefordvillage.org.uk">[Stapleford History Society]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">historysociety.staplefordvillage.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Stapleford History Society Cambridge Ghosts</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Stapleford History Society Cambridge Ghosts</span></span></span>

The Museum of Cambridge has also reflected publicly on how ghosts should be handled by a serious museum. Its 2024 article gives Abbey House as one of Cambridge’s most haunted houses, with stories of a hairy creature nicknamed “Wolfie” or “Furrey” and a nun-like apparition. The same article discusses the Gorefield disturbances of 1923, one of the strangest well-documented supernatural claims in Cambridgeshire, where objects reportedly flew and broke in a farmhouse, press interest followed, and a local wise woman was brought in to perform ritual spell-breaking.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.museumofcambridge.org.uk/2024/10/ghosts-should-we-embrace-them/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: museumofcambridge.org.uk">[The Museum of Cambridge]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">museumofcambridge.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Museum of Cambridge Ghosts – Should we embrace them?</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Museum of Cambridge Ghosts – Should we embrace them?</span></span></span>

Village ghost traditions are often more compact and place-specific. At Harston, Enid Porter recorded Women’s Institute testimony from 1935 about a White Lady seen walking from the Queen’s Head towards Mill Road, and another story of a woman seen throwing herself into the river near the bridge towards Haslingfield. These are typical of local ghost lore: a repeated route, a named landmark, a tragic gesture and community memory preserved through local recorders.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://capturingcambridge.org/places-in-south-cambridgeshire/harston/queens-head-harston/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: capturingcambridge.org">[Capturing Cambridge]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">capturingcambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">queens head harston</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">queens head harston</span></span></span>

## Plough Monday and the Straw Bear

Cambridgeshire’s calendar customs are as important as its supernatural tales. Plough Monday, the first Monday after Twelfth Night, marked the return to agricultural work after Christmas. A useful national context is that the earliest known source to name Plough Monday comes from Cambridgeshire in 1529, making the county significant in the documented history of the custom.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plough_Monday" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Plough Monday</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Plough Monday</span></span></span>

In Cambridgeshire records, seasonal customs were not minor entertainments. A preview of Porter’s *Cambridgeshire Customs and Folklore* notes that school log books recorded children absenting themselves on St Valentine’s Day, May Day, Plough Monday and Guy Fawkes’ Day, as well as during Midsummer and Stourbridge Fairs. That detail shows custom as a social force strong enough to interrupt school attendance and village routine.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://api.pageplace.de/preview/DT0400.9781000112917_A40133157/preview-9781000112917_A40133157.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: api.pageplace.de">[PagePlace]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">api.pageplace.de</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Page Place Cambridgeshire Customs and Folklore</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Page Place Cambridgeshire Customs and Folklore</span></span></span>

The most visible modern survival is the Whittlesey Straw Bear. The festival’s own history says the custom was revived in 1980 by the Whittlesea Society, bringing a straw-clad “Bear” back to the streets with musicians and dancers after roughly seventy years. Accounts of the tradition connect it with Plough Monday or Plough Tuesday, when agricultural labourers dressed one of their number in straw and took him from house to house to dance for gifts of money, food, beer or tobacco.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://strawbear.org.uk/history" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: strawbear.org.uk">[strawbear.org.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">strawbear.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on strawbear.org.uk.</span></span></span>

Strictly, Whittlesey sits in a complicated border-world of Isle of Ely, Huntingdonshire and modern Cambridgeshire identity. That is exactly why the Straw Bear is useful for this county page: it shows how fenland customs move across administrative lines, while still feeling strongly Cambridgeshire to many modern visitors.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ramsey-and-district.ccan.co.uk/content/catalogue_item/history-of-ramsey-straw-bear" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ramsey-and-district.ccan.co.uk">[ramsey-and-district.ccan.co.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ramsey-and-district.ccan.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">history of ramsey straw bear</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">history of ramsey straw bear</span></span></span>

## What is old tradition, and what is modern retelling?

Cambridgeshire folklore is strongest when treated as layers rather than as a single ancient inheritance. Some material is medieval or early modern in origin, such as St Etheldreda’s cult, Hereward’s legend, Plough Monday and witchcraft belief. Some is nineteenth- and twentieth-century collection, especially the material preserved by Women’s Institutes, local historians and Enid Porter. Some is modern heritage revival, such as the Straw Bear Festival’s 1980 revival.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.elycathedral.org/about/history-heritage/st-etheldreda" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: elycathedral.org">[elycathedral.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">elycathedral.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Ely Cathedral St. Etheldreda</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Ely Cathedral St. Etheldreda</span></span></span>

The same distinction applies to places. Wandlebury is an ancient hillfort, but the lost giant figures are uncertain and heavily mediated by antiquarian and twentieth-century interpretation. Cambridge college ghosts often attach to real people and buildings, but the ghostly details may be later narrative embroidery. Witch balls and charms are physical objects, but the beliefs attached to them varied from place to place and household to household.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://antiquity.ac.uk/projgall/meadows343" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: antiquity.ac.uk">[antiquity.ac.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">antiquity.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on antiquity.ac.uk.</span></span></span>

This does not make the folklore less valuable. On the contrary, it makes Cambridgeshire especially interesting. The county’s folklore is not a museum case of “survivals” from a single ancient past; it is a record of how people kept interpreting their landscape, illnesses, homes, saints, roads, hills and fears across changing centuries. The truth of the tradition lies in its use: what people repeated, where they repeated it, and what anxieties or hopes it helped them express.

## Why Cambridgeshire’s folklore matters

Cambridgeshire’s folklore gives the county a deeper identity than the familiar images of punting, colleges and flat fields. It shows Cambridge as a haunted and storied city, Ely as a sacred fenland stronghold, the Isle of Ely as a landscape of water, resistance and charms, and Wandlebury as a chalk hill where archaeology and giant-lore meet. It also keeps ordinary people in view: schoolchildren missing class for fairs, fen families using charms and cures, women preserving ghost stories through local institutes, and museum collectors recording speech before it disappeared.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://api.pageplace.de/preview/DT0400.9781000112917_A40133157/preview-9781000112917_A40133157.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: api.pageplace.de">[pageplace.de]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">api.pageplace.de</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Page Place Cambridgeshire Customs and Folklore</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Page Place Cambridgeshire Customs and Folklore</span></span></span>

The county’s best-known folklore is therefore not one tidy legend but a pattern. Black dogs cross the fen edge. Witches and imps haunt household imagination. Saints heal throats and necks at Ely. A rebel hero uses marshland as defence. A lost giant lies somewhere between chalk, memory and speculation. A straw bear dances through winter streets. Together, these traditions make Cambridgeshire a county where landscape and belief are inseparable.

    if (window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation && window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation.inferMarket) {
      marketId = window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation.inferMarket(available, {
        storageKey: 'phoenix-ebay-market',
        defaultMarket: defaultMarket
      });
    } else if (available.indexOf(defaultMarket)< 0) {
      marketId = available[0] || defaultMarket;
    }
    var select = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-select]');
    if (select) {
      select.addEventListener('change', function () {
        applyMarket(section, select.value, true);
      });
    }
    var trigger = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-trigger]');
    var menu = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-menu]');
    if (trigger && menu) {
      trigger.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
        event.stopPropagation();
        var expanded = trigger.getAttribute('aria-expanded') === 'true';
        menu.hidden = expanded;
        trigger.setAttribute('aria-expanded', expanded ? 'false' : 'true');
        var picker = trigger.closest('.fr-ebay-market-picker');
        if (picker) picker.classList.toggle('fr-ebay-market-picker--open', !expanded);
      });
      Array.prototype.slice.call(menu.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-option]')).forEach(function (button) {
        button.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
          event.stopPropagation();
          applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-option'), true);
        });
      });
      document.addEventListener('click', function () { closeMenu(section); });
    }
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      button.addEventListener('click', function () {
        applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button'), true);
      });
    });
    applyMarket(section, marketId, false);
  });
