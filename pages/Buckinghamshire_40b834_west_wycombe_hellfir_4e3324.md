## Introduction

West Wycombe became “Hellfire country” because its caves are not just holes in a Chiltern hillside. They are a deliberately theatrical landscape: chalk tunnels cut for Sir Francis Dashwood in the mid-eighteenth century, linked in memory with elite drinking clubs, mock ritual, scandal, ghost stories and modern paranormal tourism. The most useful way to read the Hellfire Caves is not as proof of satanic rites, nor as a simple country-house curiosity, but as a place where architecture, gossip and visitor storytelling have repeatedly fed one another. Historic England lists the cave entrance as part of Dashwood’s eighteenth-century landscape scheme, with man-made caves running for about a quarter of a mile under the hill and a “supposed” association with Hell-Fire Club meetings.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://historicengland.org.uk/listing/the-list/list-entry/1332393" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: historicengland.org.uk">[Historic England]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">historicengland.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Historic England The Cave, West Wycombe</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Historic EnglandThe Cave, West Wycombe - 1332393 &#124; Historic England...</span></span></span>



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/Buckinghamshire_40b834_west_wycombe_hellfir_4e3324-overview.webp" | relative_url }}" alt="Overview image for Hellfire Caves" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

That careful word “supposed” matters. West Wycombe’s folklore is powerful precisely because it sits on the edge between evidence and performance. The caves were real. Dashwood’s circle was real. The secrecy, political satire and anti-clerical play were real enough to generate scandal. But many of the darker tales were amplified later by guides, tourists, newspapers, ghost writers and the attraction economy. In [Buckinghamshire]({{ 'where-buckinghamshire-keeps-its-strange/' | relative_url }}) folklore, the Hellfire Caves show how a built landscape can become a haunted landscape without needing to begin as an old village oral tradition.

## Where the Hellfire Caves fit in Buckinghamshire

The Hellfire Caves are at West Wycombe, near High Wycombe, on the southern edge of the Chiltern Hills. They sit in the historic Buckinghamshire landscape of chalk, beechwoods, coaching roads, country estates and villages close enough to London to attract both fashionable eighteenth-century visitors and modern day-trippers. The caves’ present visitor address is Church Lane, West Wycombe, and show-cave guides place them halfway up West Wycombe Hill, below the church with its striking golden ball.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.showcaves.com/english/gb/subterranea/WestWycombe.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: showcaves.com">[Show Caves of the World]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">showcaves.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Show Caves of the World Subterranea of Great Britain: West Wycombe Caves</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Show Caves of the World Subterranea of Great Britain: West Wycombe Caves</span></span></span>

This setting is essential to the legend. West Wycombe is not a remote ruin where a ghost story floated free of visible evidence. It is a highly staged estate village and hill. The National Trust describes West Wycombe Park as one of the most theatrical eighteenth-century English country houses, shaped by the flamboyant tastes of Francis Dashwood, 2nd Baronet. Dashwood’s remodelling extended beyond the house into the village, the church, the mausoleum and the caves, so the underground attraction belongs to a wider landscape of deliberate spectacle.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nationaltrust.org.uk/visit/oxfordshire-buckinghamshire-berkshire/west-wycombe-park-village-and-hill/history-of-west-wycombe-park-village-and-hill" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nationaltrust.org.uk">[National Trust]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nationaltrust.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Trust West Wycombe&#x27;s history &#124; Buckinghamshire &#124; National Trust</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Trust West Wycombe&#x27;s history &#124; Buckinghamshire &#124; National Trust</span></span></span>

For Buckinghamshire folklore, this makes West Wycombe different from a [black dog]({{ 'black-dog/' | relative_url }}) lane, a holy well or a Shrove Tuesday custom. Its legends are not simply inherited from anonymous rural memory. They are attached to a landscape built by a named aristocrat, then repeatedly reinterpreted by visitors. The county’s “strange history” here is partly about what happened underground, and partly about how a place designed to impress became a machine for producing rumours.

## Chalk tunnels, secrecy and performance

The caves began with chalk. The official history of the attraction says there had long been open-cast quarrying on the hillside for building foundations and roads, and that Dashwood extended the quarry after three successive harvest failures in 1748, 1749 and 1750, both to relieve local unemployment and to provide material for a new road between West Wycombe and High Wycombe.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.hellfirecaves.co.uk/history" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: hellfirecaves.co.uk">[The Hellfire Caves]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">hellfirecaves.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Hellfire Caves History — The Hellfire Caves</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Hellfire Caves History — The Hellfire Caves</span></span></span> West Wycombe Parish Council similarly describes impoverished villagers being paid to tunnel underground to mine chalk after harvest failures.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://westwycombeparishcouncil.gov.uk/history-of-west-wycombe.php" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: westwycombeparishcouncil.gov.uk">[West Wycombe Parish Council]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">westwycombeparishcouncil.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on westwycombeparishcouncil.gov.uk.</span></span></span>

That origin is worth keeping in view because it resists the simplest spooky version of the story. The caves were not an ancient underworld shrine. They were hand-cut chalk workings turned into an artificial grotto, landscape feature and elite meeting place. The British Geological Survey, which surveyed the caves with terrestrial LiDAR in 2014, describes them as artificial chalk and flint caverns extending 500 metres underground, above West Wycombe at the southern edge of the Chilterns.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.bgs.ac.uk/geology-projects/geomatic-surveys/hellfire-caves-case-study/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: bgs.ac.uk">[British Geological Survey]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">bgs.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">British Geological Survey Hellfire Caves case study</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">British Geological Survey Hellfire Caves case study</span></span></span>

The performance came from what Dashwood did with the space. The official history argues that artificial caves and follies were fashionable in the eighteenth century, but that Dashwood’s cave was unusually large and curious. The entrance was dressed with a tall flint façade and an open courtyard, intended to be seen from the house across the valley as another landscape feature, almost like a Gothic church.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.hellfirecaves.co.uk/history" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: hellfirecaves.co.uk">[The Hellfire Caves]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">hellfirecaves.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Hellfire Caves History — The Hellfire Caves</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Hellfire Caves History — The Hellfire Caves</span></span></span>

This is where the folklore begins to thicken. The caves were underground, secretive and deliberately arranged. Their later route through named chambers, towards the so-called River Styx and Inner Temple, gives visitors a journey that feels symbolically loaded: down into the hill, through darkness, towards a hidden inner space. Even without accepting every lurid tale, the design invites an underworld reading. It turns quarrying into theatre.

## From Hellfire Club scandal to ghost tourism

Dashwood’s circle is often called the Hellfire Club, but the historical picture is more complicated than the popular label suggests. The National Trust says Dashwood founded the Order of the Knights of St Francis, also known as the Franciscans of Medmenham, in 1745; it was one of several exclusive eighteenth-century societies later grouped under the “Hellfire Clubs” name.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nationaltrust.org.uk/visit/oxfordshire-buckinghamshire-berkshire/west-wycombe-park-village-and-hill/history-of-west-wycombe-park-village-and-hill" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nationaltrust.org.uk">[National Trust]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nationaltrust.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Trust West Wycombe&#x27;s history &#124; Buckinghamshire &#124; National Trust</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Trust West Wycombe&#x27;s history &#124; Buckinghamshire &#124; National Trust</span></span></span> The Hellfire Caves’ own history lists members and associates from politics, literature, medicine and art, including Paul Whitehead, the Earl of Sandwich, William Hogarth and John Wilkes.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.hellfirecaves.co.uk/history" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: hellfirecaves.co.uk">[The Hellfire Caves]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">hellfirecaves.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Hellfire Caves History — The Hellfire Caves</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Hellfire Caves History — The Hellfire Caves</span></span></span>

The scandal was not invented from nothing. The group used mock-monastic language, enjoyed drink, satire and ribald performance, and attracted rumours of blasphemy and sexual licence. Yet the most extreme popular versions are often hard to pin down as firm evidence. The attraction’s own history includes a famous story of John Wilkes frightening Lord Sandwich with a dressed-up baboon, a tale that already sounds like a theatrical prank translated into devilish gossip.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.hellfirecaves.co.uk/history" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: hellfirecaves.co.uk">[The Hellfire Caves]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">hellfirecaves.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Hellfire Caves History — The Hellfire Caves</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Hellfire Caves History — The Hellfire Caves</span></span></span>

Modern haunted tourism draws on this ambiguity. The caves now sell the experience of entering a place associated with secrecy, transgression and darkness. The official paranormal page invites visitors to explore the tunnels after dark and presents the site as a place of shadows, restless spirits and “Paranormal Nights”, run with a ghost-hunt company.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.hellfirecaves.co.uk/paranormal-investigation" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: hellfirecaves.co.uk">[The Hellfire Caves]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">hellfirecaves.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Hellfire Caves Paranormal Investigation — The Hellfire Caves</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Hellfire Caves Paranormal Investigation — The Hellfire Caves</span></span></span> Subterranea Britannica also notes that the caves have been open to the public again since 1951 and often run Halloween and paranormal-themed events.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.subbrit.org.uk/sites/hellfire-caves/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: subbrit.org.uk">[SubBrit]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">subbrit.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Sub Brit Hellfire Caves – Subterranea Britannica</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Sub Brit Hellfire Caves – Subterranea Britannica</span></span></span>

This is not unusual in heritage tourism, but West Wycombe is a particularly clear example. The attraction does not merely preserve a legend; it stages the conditions in which the legend feels plausible. Low light, chalk walls, narrow tunnels, named chambers, the story of aristocratic secrecy and the visitor’s own expectation all work together. The “haunting” becomes a performance in which the tourist is invited to test the story by walking through it.

## Sukie, Paul Whitehead and the making of local ghosts

Two ghost traditions dominate modern retellings of the caves: Sukie, sometimes spelt Suki, and Paul Whitehead. They feel old because they are now so firmly attached to the site, but their value as folklore lies in how they have been repeated, reshaped and commercialised.

Sukie is usually presented as a young woman connected with the George and Dragon Inn, deceived or humiliated in a romantic trick and killed in or near the caves. Modern ghost-hunt companies describe her as a woman in white, often linked to the Banqueting Hall.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.hauntedhappenings.co.uk/hellfire-caves/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: hauntedhappenings.co.uk">[hauntedhappenings.co.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">hauntedhappenings.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on hauntedhappenings.co.uk.</span></span></span> These retellings should be treated as legend rather than verified local history. They work because they give the caves a human victim: not just aristocratic misbehaviour, but a wronged young woman whose story can be felt by visitors in the dark.

Paul Whitehead is more securely tied to the historical record, though his ghost story is another matter. Whitehead was a poet and steward of Dashwood’s circle. The caves’ official history records his unusual bequest: he left his heart to Lord le Despencer, along with £50 for a marble urn in which it could be deposited in the mausoleum as a memorial of his attachment to Dashwood.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.hellfirecaves.co.uk/history" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: hellfirecaves.co.uk">[The Hellfire Caves]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">hellfirecaves.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Hellfire Caves History — The Hellfire Caves</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Hellfire Caves History — The Hellfire Caves</span></span></span> The same history notes that by the mid-twentieth century many original busts and urns in the mausoleum had been removed or stolen.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.hellfirecaves.co.uk/history" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: hellfirecaves.co.uk">[The Hellfire Caves]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">hellfirecaves.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Hellfire Caves History — The Hellfire Caves</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Hellfire Caves History — The Hellfire Caves</span></span></span>

The ghost version turns this macabre fact into a haunting: Whitehead is said to search for his missing heart. Historian Jason M. Kelly traces West Wycombe ghost stories back at least to 1781, when Dashwood and servants reportedly claimed to have seen Whitehead’s ghost in the house and gardens. Kelly also notes that local guides soon told tourists stories of the Medmenham Monks, the church ball and the caves, and that visitors were even said to have been allowed to hold Whitehead’s heart until it was stolen in 1829.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://hellfiresecrets.wordpress.com/tag/paul-whitehead/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: hellfiresecrets.wordpress.com">[Secrets of the Hellfire Club]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">hellfiresecrets.wordpress.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Secrets of the Hellfire Club Paul Whitehead &#124; Secrets of the Hellfire Club</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Secrets of the Hellfire Club Paul Whitehead &#124; Secrets of the Hellfire Club</span></span></span>

That is a crucial piece of folklore history. It suggests that West Wycombe’s ghost tourism is not just a late twentieth-century invention. Local guiding, paid access, scandal and ghost narrative were already intertwined in the nineteenth century. What changes over time is the tone: from curious Georgian and Victorian scandal to darker modern paranormal branding.

## Why modern legends attach so strongly to built landscapes

The Hellfire Caves show why some built places become more legend-rich than older natural sites. They offer the visitor a ready-made story machine.

First, the caves have **physical drama**. A chalk tunnel does not need much embellishment to feel uncanny. It narrows the senses, changes the temperature, muffles sound and makes direction uncertain. The British Geological Survey’s modern survey reminds us that this is a real, complex underground structure, not just a decorative room with a gloomy name.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.bgs.ac.uk/geology-projects/geomatic-surveys/hellfire-caves-case-study/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: bgs.ac.uk">[British Geological Survey]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">bgs.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">British Geological Survey Hellfire Caves case study</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">British Geological Survey Hellfire Caves case study</span></span></span>

Second, the caves have **moral drama**. Their association with Dashwood’s club gives visitors the feeling that something hidden, elite and possibly forbidden happened there. The attraction’s own history is careful to discuss clubs, toasts, mock ceremonies and pranks, while also acknowledging long-standing ghost tales.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.hellfirecaves.co.uk/history" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: hellfirecaves.co.uk">[The Hellfire Caves]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">hellfirecaves.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Hellfire Caves History — The Hellfire Caves</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Hellfire Caves History — The Hellfire Caves</span></span></span> That mixture is more potent than certainty. If the evidence proved only ordinary dinners, the legend would shrink; if it proved a single clear crime, the story would become true crime rather than folklore. The power lies in the half-lit zone between secrecy and rumour.

Third, the caves are part of **a visible symbolic landscape**. St Lawrence’s Church and the Dashwood Mausoleum stand above; the caves run below. Historic England identifies the cave entrance as one feature in Dashwood’s wider eighteenth-century scheme across park and hillside.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://historicengland.org.uk/listing/the-list/list-entry/1332393" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: historicengland.org.uk">[Historic England]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">historicengland.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Historic England The Cave, West Wycombe</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Historic EnglandThe Cave, West Wycombe - 1332393 &#124; Historic England...</span></span></span> The popular heaven-and-hell reading almost writes itself: church on the hill, “hellfire” under the ground, mausoleum nearby, golden ball above, village and inn below.

Fourth, the site has **continuing performance**. The caves were formally reopened in 1951, after deterioration and wartime damage. The official history says Sir Francis Dashwood, the 11th Baronet, reopened them at one shilling admission with candles provided, after which publicity and controversy helped visitors “roll in”; by the end of the first summer nearly 10,000 people had paid to enter.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.hellfirecaves.co.uk/history" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: hellfirecaves.co.uk">[The Hellfire Caves]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">hellfirecaves.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Hellfire Caves History — The Hellfire Caves</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Hellfire Caves History — The Hellfire Caves</span></span></span> Show-cave records likewise note the 1951 opening and nearly 10,000 visitors, followed by engineering works and later visitor milestones.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.showcaves.com/english/gb/subterranea/WestWycombe.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: showcaves.com">[Show Caves of the World]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">showcaves.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Show Caves of the World Subterranea of Great Britain: West Wycombe Caves</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Show Caves of the World Subterranea of Great Britain: West Wycombe Caves</span></span></span>

The result is a landscape where every generation adds a layer: eighteenth-century theatre, nineteenth-century guide stories, mid-twentieth-century heritage reopening, television ghost hunts, Halloween events and online paranormal accounts. This is modern folklore in action, not folklore as a fossil.

## What to believe, and what to treat as performance

A grounded reading of West Wycombe does not require stripping away the mystery. It means separating different kinds of claim.

The strongest evidence supports the caves as eighteenth-century man-made chalk workings, expanded under Dashwood, incorporated into a theatrical estate landscape, associated with his Knights of St Francis circle, and reopened as a visitor attraction in the twentieth century. These points are supported by Historic England, the National Trust, the site’s own history and specialist underground-site sources.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://historicengland.org.uk/listing/the-list/list-entry/1332393" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: historicengland.org.uk">[historicengland.org.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">historicengland.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Historic England The Cave, West Wycombe</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Historic EnglandThe Cave, West Wycombe - 1332393 &#124; Historic England...</span></span></span>

The middle ground is the club’s behaviour. There is credible evidence of elite sociability, satire, mock-religious language, drinking, sexual reputation and deliberate secrecy. But many later claims about satanism, ritual evil or extreme occult practice are better understood as rumour, political attack, moral panic or tourist amplification unless tied to a specific reliable source. Kelly’s account is especially useful here because it shows how the West Wycombe story became darker over time, with more threatening versions appearing around the turn of the twentieth century and “local mystery-mongers” helping to circulate them.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://hellfiresecrets.wordpress.com/tag/paul-whitehead/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: hellfiresecrets.wordpress.com">[Secrets of the Hellfire Club]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">hellfiresecrets.wordpress.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Secrets of the Hellfire Club Paul Whitehead &#124; Secrets of the Hellfire Club</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Secrets of the Hellfire Club Paul Whitehead &#124; Secrets of the Hellfire Club</span></span></span>

The weakest evidence is for particular apparitions as factual events. Sukie’s ghost, Whitehead searching for his heart, phantom monks and paranormal experiences are important as living legends, not as confirmed supernatural occurrences. Their value is cultural: they show what later visitors wanted the caves to mean. In a Buckinghamshire folklore context, that is not a disappointment. It is the point.

## Why West Wycombe matters to Buckinghamshire folklore

West Wycombe matters because it gives Buckinghamshire a rare example of elite scandal becoming local supernatural tradition. Many county legends are attached to rural lanes, wells, churches or seasonal customs. The Hellfire Caves are different: they are a designed mythic environment, created by wealth and taste, then turned into popular folklore through rumour, guiding, tourism and media.

They also show how modern haunted tourism can preserve and distort at the same time. Without the visitor economy, the caves might have remained an eccentric estate feature known mainly to architectural historians and local people. Tourism kept the site visible, funded interpretation and made the stories memorable. But tourism also rewards the most atmospheric version of events: darker, simpler, more ghostly and more marketable.

For readers exploring Buckinghamshire’s strange history, the best approach is to hold both truths together. The Hellfire Caves are not merely a Halloween attraction pasted onto a historic site. Nor are they reliable evidence of every lurid tale told about them. They are a Buckinghamshire underworld made from chalk, class performance, scandal, guidebook tradition and the continuing pleasure of being frightened somewhere that already looks as though it ought to be haunted.
