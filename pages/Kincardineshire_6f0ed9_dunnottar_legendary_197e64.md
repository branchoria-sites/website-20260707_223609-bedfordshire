## Introduction

Dunnottar Castle feels more than historical because its stories are built into its shape. The ruin stands on a sea-girt rock south of Stonehaven, in the old county of [Kincardineshire]({{ 'where-kincardineshire-turns-history-into/' | relative_url }}) or the Mearns, where cliffs, narrow approaches, hidden chambers and dangerous water turn documented events into the texture of legend. Its best-known traditions are not fairy tales in the strict sense. They are remembered episodes of treasure-saving, siege, imprisonment, martyrdom, sacred ground and cliff-edge violence, retold because the place itself makes them feel almost mythic. Dunnottar is therefore one of Kincardineshire’s great legendary landscapes: a site where national history, local memory and modern visitor storytelling overlap without becoming the same thing.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://abcounties.com/counties/county-profiles/kincardineshire/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: abcounties.com">[abcounties.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">abcounties.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Association of British Counties Kincardineshire</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Association of British Counties Kincardineshire</span></span></span>

The strongest evidence for Dunnottar’s legendary importance lies in three linked layers. First, the castle’s land-and-sea setting makes it read as a natural fortress. Secondly, the saving of the Honours of Scotland in the 1650s gives it a true treasure legend with national stakes. Thirdly, the Whigs’ Vault and older stories of chapel, siege and violence give the ruin a darker memory that visitors still encounter on the ground.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.dunnottarcastle.co.uk/history" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: dunnottarcastle.co.uk">[dunnottarcastle.co.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">dunnottarcastle.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Dunnottar Castle History</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Dunnottar Castle History</span></span></span>

## The castle between land and sea

Dunnottar’s folklore power begins before any single story is told. It occupies a rocky outcrop on the north-east coast, just south of Stonehaven, and modern visitor material still leads with the sense of height, isolation and exposure: the castle is described as perched 160 feet above the waves and surrounded on three sides by the North Sea. That geography matters because legendary landscapes often depend on thresholds. Dunnottar is not simply “a castle”; it is a place between land and sea, safety and danger, authority and exile.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.dunnottarcastle.co.uk/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: dunnottarcastle.co.uk">[Dunnottar Castle]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">dunnottarcastle.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on dunnottarcastle.co.uk.</span></span></span>

The approach reinforces that impression. Aberdeenshire’s Historic Environment Record describes the entrance as reached by a steep path and defended by a solid wall set into a cleft in the rock, while the wider site includes a huge water system, chapel, gatehouse, courtyard buildings and cliff-edge defences. These details explain why Dunnottar’s stories keep returning to enclosure and escape: people are shut in, jewels are smuggled out, soldiers are said to be driven to the cliffs, and the sea becomes both barrier and possible route.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://her.aberdeenshire.gov.uk/Monument/MAB42414/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: her.aberdeenshire.gov.uk">[her.aberdeenshire.gov.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">her.aberdeenshire.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on aberdeenshire.gov.uk.</span></span></span>

For a Kincardineshire folklore page, this is the key point. Dunnottar’s legendary quality is not separate from its historic fabric. The same physical features that made it defensible also make it memorable: a narrow neck of land, a high rock, water below, a vault inside, a chapel on contested sacred ground, and a coastline that seems to cut the place off from ordinary life. This is why modern accounts can call it a world-renowned attraction and a place that looks “straight out of a fantasy novel” without inventing a new myth from nothing. The romance comes from the site’s actual form.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.dunnottarcastle.co.uk/visit" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: dunnottarcastle.co.uk">[Dunnottar Castle]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">dunnottarcastle.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on dunnottarcastle.co.uk.</span></span></span>

Dunnottar also sits inside an older Mearns geography. Kincardineshire is a maritime historic county on the North Sea coast, and Stonehaven is strongly associated with that county identity. In present-day public wording, Dunnottar is often placed in Aberdeenshire; in older county and heritage frames, it belongs to Kincardineshire and the Mearns. That double labelling is not a contradiction. It reflects the difference between modern administration and historic county memory, which is exactly the kind of difference that matters when tracing local tradition.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://abcounties.com/counties/county-profiles/kincardineshire/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: abcounties.com">[abcounties.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">abcounties.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Association of British Counties Kincardineshire</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Association of British Counties Kincardineshire</span></span></span>

## The Honours of Scotland as treasure legend

The story that most clearly turns Dunnottar into a legendary landscape is the saving of the Honours of Scotland. These are the Scottish crown jewels — the crown, sceptre and sword of state — and Edinburgh Castle describes them as the oldest Crown jewels in Britain. Their importance is not only decorative. They were used in royal ceremony and became symbols of Scotland’s national continuity.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.edinburghcastle.scot/see-and-do/highlights/honours-of-scotland/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: edinburghcastle.scot">[Edinburgh Castle]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">edinburghcastle.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Edinburgh Castle Honours of Scotland &#124; Edinburgh Castle</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Edinburgh Castle Honours of Scotland &#124; Edinburgh Castle</span></span></span>

During the crisis of the 1650s, the Honours were taken to Dunnottar. Historic Environment Scotland’s statement on the Honours records that after Charles II was crowned at Scone in 1651, the regalia could not be safely returned to Edinburgh Castle and were instead taken to “mighty Dunnottar Castle, Kincardineshire”, the seat of the Earl Marischal. In 1652–60, they were smuggled out and buried under the floor of nearby Kinneff Kirk.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://app-hes-pubs-prod-neu-01.azurewebsites.net/api/file/3f455c7c-60f2-45f6-94a2-aff800aeadbd" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: app-hes-pubs-prod-neu-01.azurewebsites.net">[HES Publications]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">app-hes-pubs-prod-neu-01.azurewebsites.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">HES Publicationsedinburgh castle honours of scotland statement of significance</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">HES Publicationsedinburgh castle honours of scotland statement of significance</span></span></span>

That bare timeline already has the structure of legend: sacred objects, an enemy army, a fortress under pressure, secrecy, concealment and recovery. The power of the tale is strengthened by its local route. The story does not remain at Dunnottar alone. It runs from Scone to Dunnottar, from Dunnottar to Kinneff, and eventually back to Edinburgh. Kinneff Old Church’s own preservation trust presents the church as the place where the Crown Jewels were hidden from Cromwell’s army, tying the national story to a small coastal kirk in the Mearns.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.kinneffoldchurch.co.uk/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: kinneffoldchurch.co.uk">[kinneffoldchurch.co.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">kinneffoldchurch.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Kinneff Old Church – Guardians to the honours of Scotland</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Kinneff Old Church – Guardians to the honours of Scotland</span></span></span>

The most memorable figures in the tradition are not only kings and commanders. The Kinneff account highlights George Ogilvy, the Rev. James Grainger and his wife, while other retellings emphasise Christian Fletcher’s role in removing or concealing the regalia. This matters for folklore because the story becomes locally tellable through human action: a woman carrying precious objects through danger, a minister’s household guarding a secret, and a community landscape protecting what a fortress could not openly keep.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.kinneffoldchurch.co.uk/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: kinneffoldchurch.co.uk">[kinneffoldchurch.co.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">kinneffoldchurch.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Kinneff Old Church – Guardians to the honours of Scotland</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Kinneff Old Church – Guardians to the honours of Scotland</span></span></span>

Dunnottar’s official history also frames the episode as one of the castle’s defining moments, stating that it played a vital role in the safekeeping of the Honours during the mid-seventeenth-century wars. The objects themselves are now displayed at Edinburgh Castle, but Dunnottar and Kinneff preserve the suspenseful middle of the story: the moment when national symbols were not in a royal chamber but hidden in a hard coastal landscape.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.dunnottarcastle.co.uk/history" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: dunnottarcastle.co.uk">[Dunnottar Castle]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">dunnottarcastle.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Dunnottar Castle History</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Dunnottar Castle History</span></span></span>

As legend, the Honours story is unusually strong because its core is well attested. The romantic details may vary in retelling, but the main sequence — removal to Dunnottar, smuggling out, concealment at Kinneff, later return — is supported by official heritage accounts. That makes it different from a ghost tale or heroic literary episode. It is a historical event that has become legendary because the circumstances are so vivid and because the landscape still lets visitors imagine the danger.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://app-hes-pubs-prod-neu-01.azurewebsites.net/api/file/3f455c7c-60f2-45f6-94a2-aff800aeadbd" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: app-hes-pubs-prod-neu-01.azurewebsites.net">[HES Publications]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">app-hes-pubs-prod-neu-01.azurewebsites.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">HES Publicationsedinburgh castle honours of scotland statement of significance</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">HES Publicationsedinburgh castle honours of scotland statement of significance</span></span></span>

## Dark memories around water and imprisonment

Dunnottar’s legendary pull is not only heroic. The Whigs’ Vault gives the castle one of the darkest remembered episodes in Kincardineshire. The castle’s own history states that more than 167 Covenanters and Whigs were imprisoned in a cellar in terrible conditions in 1685 because they refused to acknowledge religious reforms imposed by King Charles II. Aberdeenshire’s Historic Environment Record similarly records that 167 Covenanters were packed into a small vault, adding that nine died because of the conditions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.dunnottarcastle.co.uk/history" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: dunnottarcastle.co.uk">[Dunnottar Castle]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">dunnottarcastle.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Dunnottar Castle History</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Dunnottar Castle History</span></span></span>

This is not a vague haunted-room tradition. It is a named place within the ruin, attached to a specific year, a political-religious conflict and a remembered group of prisoners. The vault turns the castle’s architecture into moral memory. A visitor does not merely hear that people suffered “somewhere” at Dunnottar; the story is fixed to a confined chamber, beneath the later domestic ranges, within the same cliff-top site that also carries the treasure narrative.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.dunnottarcastle.co.uk/history" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: dunnottarcastle.co.uk">[Dunnottar Castle]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">dunnottarcastle.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Dunnottar Castle History</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Dunnottar Castle History</span></span></span>

Water sharpens the story’s emotional force. Dunnottar’s setting above the sea creates a constant contrast between open air and confinement, escape and fatal exposure. Some accounts of the 1685 imprisonment include escape attempts and deaths by falling from the cliffs, but even without leaning on every dramatic detail, the geography helps explain why the Whigs’ Vault has become such a powerful memory-place. A cellar on an isolated headland is not just a prison room; it is a chamber cut into a landscape already associated with siege, drop, tide and danger.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dunnottar_Castle" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Dunnottar Castle</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Dunnottar Castle</span></span></span>

For readers of folklore, the Whigs’ Vault is a good example of how “haunting” does not always require a ghost. A site can be haunted by remembered suffering, by repeated visitor narration and by the way a story is attached to stone. Dunnottar’s modern supernatural reputation, including Green Lady stories in tourism and ghost-writing, is much less securely evidenced than the Covenanter imprisonment. It is best treated as modern haunted-castle folklore layered over a better-documented historic trauma, rather than as the central tradition of the site.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.thecourier.co.uk/fp/lifestyle/1680127/sensational-castle-visit/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: thecourier.co.uk">[thecourier.co.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">thecourier.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">sensational castle visit</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">sensational castle visit</span></span></span>

## Older stories: chapel, shield and Wallace

Dunnottar’s legendary atmosphere is also deepened by older and more uncertain traditions. Aberdeenshire’s Historic Environment Record says the castle probably occupies the site of a prehistoric fort, that St Ninian was said to have established a church there near the beginning of the fifth century, and that the site may be the “Dunfoithir” besieged in 681. The Atlas of Hillforts is more cautious, noting documentary suggestions of an early medieval stronghold beneath the medieval castle but rating the dating evidence as weak.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://her.aberdeenshire.gov.uk/Monument/MAB42414/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: her.aberdeenshire.gov.uk">[her.aberdeenshire.gov.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">her.aberdeenshire.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on aberdeenshire.gov.uk.</span></span></span>

That caution is important. These early layers are part of Dunnottar’s legendary landscape, but they are not all equally secure. The idea of a saintly chapel, an early fortress and a Pictish or early medieval stronghold gives the rock an ancient aura, yet the evidence is thinner than for the seventeenth-century events. A grounded retelling should therefore present these as traditions, identifications and possibilities, not as simple facts.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://her.aberdeenshire.gov.uk/Monument/MAB42414/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: her.aberdeenshire.gov.uk">[her.aberdeenshire.gov.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">her.aberdeenshire.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on aberdeenshire.gov.uk.</span></span></span>

The literary layer is equally revealing. Dunnottar is associated with the early thirteenth-century romance known as the Roman de Fergus, in which the hero Fergus travels to Dunnottar to retrieve a magic shield. This is one of the clearest reasons to call Dunnottar “legendary” in a literal sense: the place enters medieval romance as a destination for a marvellous object. Even though modern visitors usually come for ruins and views rather than Arthurian literature, the old story shows that Dunnottar’s dramatic location was imaginatively charged long before modern tourism.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dunnottar_Castle" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Dunnottar Castle</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Dunnottar Castle</span></span></span>

The William Wallace tradition is more famous but more difficult. Dunnottar’s official history presents the story as legend: Wallace is said to have attacked an English garrison and burned the chapel where soldiers had taken refuge, with others driven over the cliffs. Other summaries trace the episode to Blind Harry, whose Wallace narrative was written long after the events it describes. The result is a powerful local legend, but not a secure battle report.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.dunnottarcastle.co.uk/history" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: dunnottarcastle.co.uk">[Dunnottar Castle]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">dunnottarcastle.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Dunnottar Castle History</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Dunnottar Castle History</span></span></span>

That distinction makes the Wallace story more useful, not less. It shows how Dunnottar gathers heroic violence into itself. The elements are familiar: a national hero, an enemy garrison, sacred ground, fire and cliff-edge death. Whether or not the event happened as later tradition says, the story fits the landscape so closely that it has become part of how people imagine the place.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.dunnottarcastle.co.uk/history" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: dunnottarcastle.co.uk">[Dunnottar Castle]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">dunnottarcastle.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Dunnottar Castle History</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Dunnottar Castle History</span></span></span>

## How Dunnottar is retold today

Modern Dunnottar is a visitor attraction, but its public appeal still depends on the same legendary ingredients: height, danger, secrecy, treasure and memory. The castle’s visitor pages describe it as a dramatic cliff-top ruin, an iconic destination and a place famous for its geology, history and setting. That language is promotional, but it works because the historic and physical foundations are real.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.dunnottarcastle.co.uk/visit" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: dunnottarcastle.co.uk">[Dunnottar Castle]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">dunnottarcastle.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on dunnottarcastle.co.uk.</span></span></span>

The site is also protected heritage. Historic Environment Scotland records Dunnottar Castle as a scheduled monument, and Aberdeenshire’s Historic Environment Record links that protection to a complex site of castle remains, possible earlier occupation, chapel, vault, water system and defensive structures. This matters because legendary landscapes can be damaged by careless exaggeration. At Dunnottar, the stones are not just scenery for stories; they are nationally important remains that hold the stories in place.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://portal.historicenvironment.scot/apex/f?p=1505%3A300%3A%3A%3A%3A%3AVIEWTYPE%2CVIEWREF%3Adesignation%2CSM986" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: portal.historicenvironment.scot">[Historic Environment Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">portal.historicenvironment.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Historic Environment Scotland Dunnottar Castle (SM986</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Historic Environment Scotland Dunnottar Castle (SM986</span></span></span>

Visual culture has helped turn Dunnottar into an emblem. John Slezer’s Theatrum Scotiae, first published in 1693, presented Scottish castles, towns and other places as part of a printed national landscape, and Dunnottar appeared in that tradition of picturing power and place. Long before Instagram and travel blogs, Dunnottar was already the kind of site that people wanted to frame, engrave, circulate and remember.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nls.uk/collections/stories/maps-and-places/john-slezer/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nls.uk">[National Library of Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nls.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">john slezer</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">john slezer</span></span></span>

Today’s retellings often blend categories that should be kept distinct. The Honours of Scotland episode is a well-supported historical treasure story. The Whigs’ Vault is a documented memory of imprisonment and suffering. The Wallace burning story is a later heroic legend attached to the Wars of Independence. The Green Lady and other ghostly motifs are part of modern haunted-castle folklore, more lightly evidenced but still relevant to visitor imagination. Dunnottar’s strength is that all of these layers gather around one coherent place rather than floating as disconnected tales.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://app-hes-pubs-prod-neu-01.azurewebsites.net/api/file/3f455c7c-60f2-45f6-94a2-aff800aeadbd" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: app-hes-pubs-prod-neu-01.azurewebsites.net">[azurewebsites.net]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">app-hes-pubs-prod-neu-01.azurewebsites.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">HES Publicationsedinburgh castle honours of scotland statement of significance</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">HES Publicationsedinburgh castle honours of scotland statement of significance</span></span></span>

## Why Dunnottar matters to Kincardineshire folklore

Dunnottar matters because it gives Kincardineshire a nationally recognisable legendary landmark without needing to invent a monster or a fairy kingdom. Its folklore is the folklore of a charged historic landscape: a sea-rock fortress where real political crises, sacred objects, prisoners, chapels and cliff paths have become stories people retell because the place makes them unforgettable.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://her.aberdeenshire.gov.uk/Monument/MAB42414/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: her.aberdeenshire.gov.uk">[her.aberdeenshire.gov.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">her.aberdeenshire.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on aberdeenshire.gov.uk.</span></span></span>

Within the wider county pattern, Dunnottar sits beside Stonehaven’s coastal identity, the Mearns’ older parish histories, Kinneff’s hidden-regalia tradition and other Kincardineshire sites where belief and memory attach to wells, kirks, stones and seasonal customs. It is not merely a backdrop to national history. It is one of the old county’s clearest examples of how a particular landscape can turn history into legend: the Honours hidden from Cromwell, Covenanters shut in the vault, Wallace imagined at the burning chapel, and the sea always below.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://abcounties.com/counties/county-profiles/kincardineshire/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: abcounties.com">[abcounties.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">abcounties.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Association of British Counties Kincardineshire</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Association of British Counties Kincardineshire</span></span></span>
