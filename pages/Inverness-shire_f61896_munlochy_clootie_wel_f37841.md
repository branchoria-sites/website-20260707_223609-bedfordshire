## Introduction

Munlochy Clootie Well is one of the clearest surviving examples of a Highland healing custom in which water, cloth, prayer, illness and landscape are all bound together. The rite is simple: a small rag, or “cloot”, is dipped in the well, tied to a nearby tree, and left to decay; as the cloth wastes away, the illness or trouble is traditionally believed to fade with it. Forestry and Land Scotland now presents the site as a living woodland tradition, while asking visitors to use only small biodegradable offerings, because modern synthetic cloth and plastic objects can damage the place people have come to honour.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://forestryandland.gov.scot/visit/destinations/munlochy-clootie-well" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: forestryandland.gov.scot">[Forestry and Land Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">forestryandland.gov.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Forestry and Land Scotland Munlochy Clootie Well Forest Visitor Guide &#124; FLS</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Forestry and Land Scotland Munlochy Clootie Well Forest Visitor Guide &#124; FLS</span></span></span>

For an [Inverness-shire]({{ 'inverness-shire/' | relative_url }}) folklore route, Munlochy needs careful placing. The well is on the Black Isle, just across the Beauly Firth from Inverness; strict historic-county mapping places Munlochy in Ross-shire, not Inverness-shire, while Inverness-shire lies immediately to the south and west.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://wikishire.co.uk/wiki/Black_Isle" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wikishire.co.uk">[wikishire.co.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wikishire.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wikishire.co.uk.</span></span></span> Its value here is as a near-neighbouring Black Isle case that helps explain the holy wells, healing customs and sacred-water beliefs that readers encounter around the Inverness area, including the related clootie well in Culloden Forest near Inverness.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://forestryandland.gov.scot/visit/destinations/munlochy-clootie-well" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: forestryandland.gov.scot">[Forestry and Land Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">forestryandland.gov.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Forestry and Land Scotland Munlochy Clootie Well Forest Visitor Guide &#124; FLS</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Forestry and Land Scotland Munlochy Clootie Well Forest Visitor Guide &#124; FLS</span></span></span>

## How clootie well rituals work

The Munlochy custom turns illness into something visible. In the best-known form of the rite, a piece of cloth connected with the sick person is dipped in the spring water and tied to a branch near the well. The cloth is not meant to be a permanent decoration. Its slow rotting is the point: the ailment is imagined as leaving the person as the rag breaks down. Forestry and Land Scotland summarises the same core belief for visitors today, explaining that the sickness or ailment is thought to fade as the rag disintegrates.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://forestryandland.gov.scot/visit/destinations/munlochy-clootie-well" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: forestryandland.gov.scot">[Forestry and Land Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">forestryandland.gov.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Forestry and Land Scotland Munlochy Clootie Well Forest Visitor Guide &#124; FLS</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Forestry and Land Scotland Munlochy Clootie Well Forest Visitor Guide &#124; FLS</span></span></span>

Older descriptions add more ceremonial detail. Undiscovered Scotland records a tradition in which pilgrims circled the Munlochy well sunwise three times, splashed some of the water on the ground, made a prayer, and then tied the cloot to a nearby tree.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.undiscoveredscotland.co.uk/munlochy/clootiewell/index.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: undiscoveredscotland.co.uk">[Undiscovered Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">undiscoveredscotland.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Undiscovered Scotland The Clootie Well Feature Page on Undiscovered Scotland</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Undiscovered Scotland The Clootie Well Feature Page on Undiscovered Scotland</span></span></span> The Guardian’s account of the 2022 controversy repeats the same basic pattern: a cloth associated with the ill person, water from the well, a prayer or entreaty, and a healing hope attached to the rag’s decay.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.theguardian.com/uk-news/2022/jan/30/upset-a-lot-of-people-outrage-tidy-up-of-scottish-sacred-well-clootie-offerings" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theguardian.com">[The Guardian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theguardian.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on theguardian.com.</span></span></span>

The ritual is easy to misunderstand because, to a modern passer-by, the site can look like litter hanging from trees. In the traditional logic of the practice, however, the rag is not simply a souvenir. It is a carrier. It may represent the illness, the person’s need, the prayer made at the spring, or the offering left in exchange for help. This is why the condition of the material matters so much. A cotton or wool scrap can rot into the landscape; nylon, polyester, plastic toys, face masks and synthetic garments can linger long after the symbolic “fading” should have happened.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://forestryandland.gov.scot/visit/destinations/munlochy-clootie-well" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: forestryandland.gov.scot">[Forestry and Land Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">forestryandland.gov.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Forestry and Land Scotland Munlochy Clootie Well Forest Visitor Guide &#124; FLS</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Forestry and Land Scotland Munlochy Clootie Well Forest Visitor Guide &#124; FLS</span></span></span>

## Healing, blessing and misfortune

Munlochy’s well is usually presented as a healing place rather than as a site of spectacle. The associated belief is not that the cloth itself cures by medicine, but that water, prayer, saintly or spiritual presence, and symbolic decay together move misfortune away from the sufferer. Modern accounts often describe the recipient as a sick person, while some local retellings specify sick children; the broader point is that the well belongs to a Highland pattern of sacred springs where distress is transferred, blessed, washed away or left behind.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clootie_well" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Clootie well</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Clootie well</span></span></span>

The saintly layer matters. Munlochy’s healing well is commonly linked with St Boniface or St Curitan, missionary figures associated in tradition with early medieval Christianity in northern Scotland. Undiscovered Scotland says the well is said to date back to, and perhaps beyond, the time of St Boniface or St Curitan, while The Guardian describes the site as an example of older sacred-water practice being absorbed into Christian tradition.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.undiscoveredscotland.co.uk/munlochy/clootiewell/index.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: undiscoveredscotland.co.uk">[Undiscovered Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">undiscoveredscotland.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Undiscovered Scotland The Clootie Well Feature Page on Undiscovered Scotland</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Undiscovered Scotland The Clootie Well Feature Page on Undiscovered Scotland</span></span></span>

That does not mean every detail can be proved back to the seventh century. The safer reading is layered: the spring is remembered through saintly dedication, antiquarian and local description, modern visitor interpretation, and continuing practice. This is exactly what makes it valuable as folklore. It is not just an “ancient custom” frozen in the past, nor merely a modern tourist curiosity. It is an active place where older ideas of healing, saintly intercession, water spirits, votive offerings and personal grief still meet on the same trees.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://forestryandland.gov.scot/visit/destinations/munlochy-clootie-well" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: forestryandland.gov.scot">[Forestry and Land Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">forestryandland.gov.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Forestry and Land Scotland Munlochy Clootie Well Forest Visitor Guide &#124; FLS</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Forestry and Land Scotland Munlochy Clootie Well Forest Visitor Guide &#124; FLS</span></span></span>

Misfortune is also part of the story because many clootie customs depend on removal. The rag may stand for a pain, a disease, a worry, or a body part in need of healing. Modern observers have noted socks, masks, underwear and other personal objects at the site, which can be read in different ways: some may be careless dumping, some may be literal attempts to connect the offering with a particular ailment, and some may be memorial or emotional tokens rather than old-style healing cloots.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://insearchofholywellsandhealingsprings.wordpress.com/2020/01/19/in-search-of-rag-wells-the-clootie-or-st-bonifaces-well-munlochy-scotland-a-photo-archive/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: insearchofholywellsandhealingsprings.wordpress.com">[holyandhealingwells]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">insearchofholywellsandhealingsprings.wordpress.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wordpress.com.</span></span></span> The ambiguity is part of the modern custom’s tension: the same object can look sacred to one visitor, unhygienic to another, and ecologically damaging to a land manager.

## Why Munlochy became a modern test case

Munlochy is not the only clootie well in Scotland, but it has become one of the best-known because the practice is still visible there on a large scale. Forestry and Land Scotland’s visitor page openly invites people to visit the well and make a wish, while also giving practical guidance on how to do so without harming the woodland.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://forestryandland.gov.scot/visit/destinations/munlochy-clootie-well" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: forestryandland.gov.scot">[Forestry and Land Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">forestryandland.gov.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Forestry and Land Scotland Munlochy Clootie Well Forest Visitor Guide &#124; FLS</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Forestry and Land Scotland Munlochy Clootie Well Forest Visitor Guide &#124; FLS</span></span></span> The official visitor information places the site just off the A832 before Munlochy, with the rags visible near the car park entrance, which helps explain why it receives casual visitors as well as people making deliberate offerings.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://forestryandland.gov.scot/visit/destinations/munlochy-clootie-well/visitor-information" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: forestryandland.gov.scot">[Forestry and Land Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">forestryandland.gov.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on gov.scot.</span></span></span>

Its accessibility has changed the custom. A hidden local spring can be cared for informally by people who know the place; a well signposted from a road and circulated online becomes a destination. That brings more offerings, more photography, more curiosity, and more mistakes. The holy well becomes a governance problem without ceasing to be a sacred or folkloric place. Who decides what is a valid cloot? Who removes a plastic garment? When does care become desecration? Munlochy now answers those questions in public.

The 2022 clearance made that tension visible. After a stranger removed many offerings from the site, local concern and press coverage framed the well as both an environmental problem and a place of strong personal attachment. The Guardian reported that Forestry and Land Scotland already had signage asking for small biodegradable offerings, that rangers regularly tidied inappropriate items, and that local people were interested in a more organised community approach to caring for the site.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.theguardian.com/uk-news/2022/jan/30/upset-a-lot-of-people-outrage-tidy-up-of-scottish-sacred-well-clootie-offerings" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theguardian.com">[The Guardian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theguardian.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on theguardian.com.</span></span></span>

This is why Munlochy is best understood not only as a survival of Highland belief, but as a policy intervention in miniature. The living custom now depends on practical rules: biodegradable cloth, small offerings, respect for the spring, and community-led care rather than unilateral “clean-ups”. The belief asks for decay; environmental care asks for the same thing. The ideal cloot is therefore not the brightest, biggest or most dramatic object, but the one that can disappear without poisoning the place.

## Biodegradable offerings and sacred landscape care

Forestry and Land Scotland’s current guidance is unusually clear: visitors may bring a cloot, but it should be small, appropriate and biodegradable; pure wool or pure cotton are named as the best options for the environment.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://forestryandland.gov.scot/visit/destinations/munlochy-clootie-well" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: forestryandland.gov.scot">[Forestry and Land Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">forestryandland.gov.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Forestry and Land Scotland Munlochy Clootie Well Forest Visitor Guide &#124; FLS</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Forestry and Land Scotland Munlochy Clootie Well Forest Visitor Guide &#124; FLS</span></span></span> That advice is not a modern add-on that destroys the tradition. It actually fits the ritual logic. If the healing depends on the cloth decaying, then synthetic material that does not rot properly interrupts both the symbolism and the ecology.

Modern clothing makes this harder than it sounds. A visitor may think they are leaving “cloth”, but many garments contain polyester, elastane, nylon, dyes, plastic prints or metal fastenings. The Guardian’s reporting on Munlochy noted that the build-up of debris highlighted the amount of plastic in modern clothing, while Anne Whitehead’s discussion of clootie sites points out that older scraps of cotton or wool would disintegrate in a way that many man-made fibres do not.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.theguardian.com/uk-news/2022/jan/30/upset-a-lot-of-people-outrage-tidy-up-of-scottish-sacred-well-clootie-offerings" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theguardian.com">[The Guardian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theguardian.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on theguardian.com.</span></span></span>

A respectful modern clootie practice around Munlochy therefore has three simple tests:<span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://allthatsinteresting.com/munlochy-clootie-well" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: allthatsinteresting.com">[allthatsinteresting.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">allthatsinteresting.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">munlochy clootie well</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">munlochy clootie well</span></span></span>


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **Will it rot?** Pure cotton or wool is closest to the older ritual logic; plastic, synthetic fabric, laminated objects and elasticated clothing are poor fits.
* **Is it modest in scale?** A small strip of cloth carries the meaning without turning a tree into a dumping place.
* **Does it respect other visitors?** A holy well is shared ground. The offering should not make the spring unpleasant, unsafe or inaccessible for the next person.

</div>

This is where folklore and land management meet. The aim is not to sanitise the site until it becomes a museum exhibit, but to keep the custom legible and sustainable. A clootie well with no cloots loses much of its meaning; a clootie well buried under plastic loses another kind of meaning. Munlochy’s current challenge is to hold the middle ground.

## How old and well-attested is the Munlochy tradition?

The Munlochy well is well attested as a modern and recent-traditional site, but the exact age of every claimed detail is harder to prove. The saintly association with St Boniface or St Curitan is widely repeated in local and visitor accounts, and the spring is presented as an old healing well rather than a recently invented attraction.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.undiscoveredscotland.co.uk/munlochy/clootiewell/index.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: undiscoveredscotland.co.uk">[Undiscovered Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">undiscoveredscotland.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Undiscovered Scotland The Clootie Well Feature Page on Undiscovered Scotland</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Undiscovered Scotland The Clootie Well Feature Page on Undiscovered Scotland</span></span></span> Forestry and Land Scotland treats the site as part of a wider family of Celtic clootie wells linked to ancient healing traditions, while also presenting the practice in cautious belief-language rather than as a medical fact.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://forestryandland.gov.scot/visit/destinations/munlochy-clootie-well" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: forestryandland.gov.scot">[Forestry and Land Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">forestryandland.gov.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Forestry and Land Scotland Munlochy Clootie Well Forest Visitor Guide &#124; FLS</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Forestry and Land Scotland Munlochy Clootie Well Forest Visitor Guide &#124; FLS</span></span></span>

The broader rag-well custom is much better documented than any single origin story. Antiquarian and folklore writing records holy wells “tapestried” with rags in Scotland, and modern summaries connect clootie wells with older votive-water traditions found in Celtic regions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.undiscoveredscotland.co.uk/munlochy/clootiewell/index.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: undiscoveredscotland.co.uk">[Undiscovered Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">undiscoveredscotland.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Undiscovered Scotland The Clootie Well Feature Page on Undiscovered Scotland</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Undiscovered Scotland The Clootie Well Feature Page on Undiscovered Scotland</span></span></span> The key point is not that the exact Munlochy rite can be dated neatly to one founding moment, but that it belongs to a durable British and Irish pattern: sacred water, a tree, a personal token, and a request for healing or protection.

There is also evidence of a living, changing practice. The holy-wells blog that photographed Munlochy offerings in 2017 treated the site as the country’s most famous example and recorded a wide variety of objects left there, from conventional cloth strips to more puzzling personal items.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://insearchofholywellsandhealingsprings.wordpress.com/2020/01/19/in-search-of-rag-wells-the-clootie-or-st-bonifaces-well-munlochy-scotland-a-photo-archive/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: insearchofholywellsandhealingsprings.wordpress.com">[holyandhealingwells]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">insearchofholywellsandhealingsprings.wordpress.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wordpress.com.</span></span></span> That variety shows the custom adapting beyond a narrow inherited form. Some visitors still follow the healing logic closely; others bring memorial, tourist, artistic or improvised meanings to the same place.

## Why it matters for Inverness-shire folklore

Munlochy is just outside historic Inverness-shire, but it belongs naturally in the folklore geography of the Inverness-facing Highlands. The Black Isle is visible and reachable from Inverness, and the well sits within a wider local circuit of sacred, strange and story-rich places: Culloden’s clootie well near Inverness, Black Isle fairy and prophetic traditions, Rosemarkie and Fortrose church landscapes, and the older saintly networks around the Moray and Beauly Firths. Forestry and Land Scotland itself links Munlochy with another clootie well in Culloden Forest.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://forestryandland.gov.scot/visit/destinations/munlochy-clootie-well" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: forestryandland.gov.scot">[Forestry and Land Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">forestryandland.gov.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Forestry and Land Scotland Munlochy Clootie Well Forest Visitor Guide &#124; FLS</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Forestry and Land Scotland Munlochy Clootie Well Forest Visitor Guide &#124; FLS</span></span></span>

For readers following Inverness-shire folklore, the well is useful because it changes the subject from famous monsters and dramatic legends to intimate ritual behaviour. [Loch Ness]({{ 'loch-ness/' | relative_url }}) stories are often about sightings, media and mystery; clootie wells are about what people do when they are worried, ill, grieving or hopeful. The supernatural element is quieter. It lives in an action repeated by ordinary visitors: wet the cloth, tie it, leave it, trust the place.

The historic-county distinction also adds value rather than confusion. Munlochy reminds us that folklore does not always obey administrative borders. Customs travel along roads, firths, parish ties, saintly dedications and family memory. A strict county map places the Black Isle mainly in Ross-shire and Cromartyshire, with Inverness-shire nearby; a folklore map shows a shared Highland belief landscape around Inverness, the Beauly Firth and the Moray Firth.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://wikishire.co.uk/wiki/Black_Isle" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wikishire.co.uk">[wikishire.co.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wikishire.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wikishire.co.uk.</span></span></span>

## How the custom is retold today

Today, Munlochy Clootie Well is retold in three overlapping ways. First, it remains a practical visitor site managed by Forestry and Land Scotland, with access information, parking guidance and environmental instructions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://forestryandland.gov.scot/visit/destinations/munlochy-clootie-well/visitor-information" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: forestryandland.gov.scot">[Forestry and Land Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">forestryandland.gov.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on gov.scot.</span></span></span> Secondly, it is a local sacred or semi-sacred place where people continue to leave offerings connected with healing, memory or hope. Thirdly, it is a modern folklore image: a striking woodland scene of coloured cloth, personal objects and uneasy questions about belief.

The 2022 clearance sharpened the modern story. Some saw the removal of offerings as tidying; others experienced it as an attack on a place of memory, prayer and local identity. The aftermath pushed the conversation towards community stewardship, education and better environmental practice rather than simply more offerings or total removal.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.theguardian.com/uk-news/2022/jan/30/upset-a-lot-of-people-outrage-tidy-up-of-scottish-sacred-well-clootie-offerings" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theguardian.com">[The Guardian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theguardian.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on theguardian.com.</span></span></span>

That makes Munlochy unusually revealing. A clootie well is not just an old custom preserved under glass. It is a negotiation between the living and the landscape: between illness and hope, ritual and rubbish, private grief and public access, old cloth and modern plastic. Around Munlochy, the most important lesson is that Highland belief survives not only in stories told about the past, but in small acts still being argued over, repaired and relearnt in the present.

    if (menu) menu.hidden = true;
    if (trigger) trigger.setAttribute('aria-expanded', 'false');
    if (picker) picker.classList.remove('fr-ebay-market-picker--open');
  }
  function availableMarkets(section) {
    var select = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-select]');
    if (select) {
      return Array.prototype.slice.call(select.options)
        .map(function (option) { return option.value; })
        .filter(function (marketId) { return !!markets[marketId]; });
    }
    return Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]'))
      .map(function (button) { return button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button'); })
      .filter(function (marketId) { return !!markets[marketId]; });
  }
  function applyMarket(section, marketId, persist) {
    var available = availableMarkets(section);
    if (available.indexOf(marketId)< 0) marketId = available[0] || defaultMarket;
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-localized-link]')).forEach(function (link) {
      var query = link.getAttribute('data-ebay-query') || '';
      var reference = link.getAttribute('data-ebay-reference') || '';
      link.setAttribute('href', buildUrl(query, reference, marketId));
    });
    var select = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-select]');
    if (select && available.indexOf(marketId) >= 0) select.value = marketId;
    var selectedText = marketId;
    if (select && select.selectedIndex >= 0) selectedText = select.options[select.selectedIndex].text;
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-selected-market-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = selectedText;
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-selected-market-flag], [data-ebay-trigger-market-flag]')).forEach(function (flag) {
      flag.className = flagClass(marketId);
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-trigger-market-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = selectedText;
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-domain-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = domainLabel(marketId);
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-option]')).forEach(function (button) {
      button.setAttribute('aria-selected', button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-option') === marketId ? 'true' : 'false');
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      var active = button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button') === marketId;
      button.setAttribute('aria-pressed', active ? 'true' : 'false');
    });
    section.setAttribute('data-ebay-selected-market', marketId);
    if (persist) {
      try { if (window.localStorage) localStorage.setItem('phoenix-ebay-market', marketId); } catch (err) {}
    }
    closeMenu(section);
  }
  sections.forEach(function (section) {
    if (section.getAttribute('data-ebay-localized-init') === '1') return;
    section.setAttribute('data-ebay-localized-init', '1');
    var available = availableMarkets(section);
    var marketId = defaultMarket;
    if (window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation && window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation.inferMarket) {
      marketId = window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation.inferMarket(available, {
        storageKey: 'phoenix-ebay-market',
        defaultMarket: defaultMarket
      });
    } else if (available.indexOf(defaultMarket)< 0) {
      marketId = available[0] || defaultMarket;
    }
    var select = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-select]');
    if (select) {
      select.addEventListener('change', function () {
        applyMarket(section, select.value, true);
      });
    }
    var trigger = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-trigger]');
    var menu = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-menu]');
    if (trigger && menu) {
      trigger.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
        event.stopPropagation();
        var expanded = trigger.getAttribute('aria-expanded') === 'true';
        menu.hidden = expanded;
        trigger.setAttribute('aria-expanded', expanded ? 'false' : 'true');
        var picker = trigger.closest('.fr-ebay-market-picker');
        if (picker) picker.classList.toggle('fr-ebay-market-picker--open', !expanded);
      });
      Array.prototype.slice.call(menu.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-option]')).forEach(function (button) {
        button.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
          event.stopPropagation();
          applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-option'), true);
        });
      });
      document.addEventListener('click', function () { closeMenu(section); });
    }
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      button.addEventListener('click', function () {
        applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button'), true);
      });
    });
    applyMarket(section, marketId, false);
  });
})();
</script>
