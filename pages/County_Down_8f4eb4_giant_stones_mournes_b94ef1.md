## Introduction

[County Down]({{ 'where-county-down-turns-landscape-into/' | relative_url }})’s best-known giant stone story is the tale of the Cloughmore Stone above Rostrevor: a huge granite boulder, high on the slopes of Slieve Martin, which local legend says was hurled across Carlingford Lough by Fionn Mac Cumhaill during a battle with a rival giant. The modern geological explanation is different but just as striking: Cloughmore is a glacial erratic, a stone moved and dropped by ice rather than by a giant’s hand. That double reading is exactly why the story matters. In one view, the Mourne landscape is evidence of deep natural history; in the other, it is a stage where old heroic figures left visible marks. The folklore does not simply decorate the scenery. It turns boulders, lough, mountain slopes and distant ridges into a readable local story-map of south County Down.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.visitmournegullionstrangford.com/things-to-do/the-cloughmore-trail-p824971" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: visitmournegullionstrangford.com">[visitmournegullionstrangford.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">visitmournegullionstrangford.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Visit Mourne Gullion Strangford The Cloughmore Trail</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Visit Mourne Gullion Strangford The Cloughmore Trail</span></span></span>

## The Cloughmore above Rostrevor

Cloughmore, often called “the Big Stone”, sits above Rostrevor in Kilbroney Forest Park, overlooking Carlingford Lough and the Cooley Peninsula. Visitor guides now present it as a 50-tonne granite boulder reached by the Cloughmore Trail, a circular walk through Kilbroney’s woods and viewpoints; the official trail description says the route leads to the “gigantic, 50 tonne granite boulder” and links it directly with the local tale of Finn McCool throwing the stone across the lough during a fight with a giant rival.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.visitmournegullionstrangford.com/things-to-do/the-cloughmore-trail-p824971" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: visitmournegullionstrangford.com">[Visit Mourne Gullion Strangford]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">visitmournegullionstrangford.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Visit Mourne Gullion Strangford The Cloughmore Trail</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Visit Mourne Gullion Strangford The Cloughmore Trail</span></span></span>

The stone works so well as folklore because it looks like a problem waiting for a story. It is large, isolated and dramatically placed. A geological field guide to Cooley, Gullion, Mourne and Slieve Croob identifies Cloughmore as a glacial erratic: a granite boulder resting on metasedimentary rocks, not a neat continuation of the rock beneath it. The same guide notes that from Cloughmore a visitor can see almost the whole of Carlingford Lough, Slieve Foy across the water, the eastern Cooley Peninsula, and the hills and valleys of the Warrenpoint and Rostrevor area shaped by ice.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cdn.ringofgullion.org/sites/4/2025/09/A_Geological_Field_Guide_to_Cooley_Gullion_Mourne_Slieve_Croob_.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cdn.ringofgullion.org">[cdn.ringofgullion.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cdn.ringofgullion.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">A Geological Field Guide to Cooley Gullion Mourne Slieve Croob</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">A Geological Field Guide to Cooley Gullion Mourne Slieve Croob</span></span></span>

That view is central to the legend. The story is not just “a giant threw a stone”; it is “a giant threw that stone from over there to here”. The lough, the opposite shore and the exposed boulder all remain visible in one sweep. This makes the tale unusually easy to picture: Fionn stands across the water, the rival giant threatens from the Down side, and the stone’s present position becomes the punchline.

The Mourne Gullion Strangford UNESCO Global Geopark lists Cloughmore as a glacial erratic geosite and describes it as a Scottish-origin granite erratic deposited on a mountain top above Carlingford Lough, associated with local mythology. The same listing says a Geopark-supported research project with Queen’s University Belfast 3-D scanned the erratic and calculated its mass at 50 metric tonnes.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://mournegullionstrangfordgeopark.com/things-to-do/geosites/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: mournegullionstrangfordgeopark.com">[mournegullionstrangfordgeopark.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">mournegullionstrangfordgeopark.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Geopark Geosites</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Geopark Geosites</span></span></span>

## What version of the story is actually attested?

The Cloughmore legend is not only a modern tourism caption. A nineteenth-century printed version appears in John O’Hanlon’s *Irish Local Legends*, published in Dublin in 1896. The Internet Archive catalogue identifies O’Hanlon’s book, its date and publisher, while a text presentation of the Cloughmore tale gives the story as “The Legend of the Origin of the Cloughmore Stone”.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/details/irishlocallegend00ohan/page/n67/mode/2up" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on archive.org.</span></span></span>

In that older printed version, Fionn is challenged by a giant from Scotland. The two giants quarrel over strength and agree to throw large fragments of rock from near Carlingford across the sea. The Scottish giant chooses what he thinks is the lighter stone but fails to clear the water; Fionn throws the larger one and lands it high on the cliffs beyond, where it remains as Cloughmore. O’Hanlon’s version is valuable because it shows the core idea already fixed in print by the late nineteenth century: the stone’s strange position is explained as proof of Fionn’s superior strength.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.mondelegendaire.com/en/articles/temp-1773837156-803434/lang/en/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: mondelegendaire.com">[mondelegendaire.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">mondelegendaire.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Monde légendaire</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Monde légendaire</span></span></span>

Modern local retellings often shift the rival’s identity and add a more dramatic battle. In one current tourism version, Fionn fights Ruscaire, a giant of snow and ice. Fionn has pursued and cooked a wild boar on Slieve Foy, then sees a giant shadow over Carlingford Lough. The giants fight with feet astride mountain and water; Fionn eventually hurls the Cloughmore Stone, crushing Ruscaire and leaving the stone in Kilbroney Park.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.visitmournegullionstrangford.com/blog/read/2026/01/our-story-of-fionn-mac-cumhaill-b284" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: visitmournegullionstrangford.com">[Visit Mourne Gullion Strangford]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">visitmournegullionstrangford.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Visit Mourne Gullion Strangford Our Story of Fionn Mac Cumhaill</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Visit Mourne Gullion Strangford Our Story of Fionn Mac Cumhaill</span></span></span>

Those differences matter. They show a living tale-family rather than a single fixed “original”. The older printed form stresses a contest of strength with a Scottish giant; the modern tourist form stresses seasonal conflict, spectacle and named local places. Both keep the same essential structure: a giant, a stone, a throw across Carlingford Lough, and a visible boulder above Rostrevor.

## Fionn Mac Cumhaill as landscape giant

Fionn Mac Cumhaill is not, in medieval learned tradition, simply a mountain-sized giant. He is the central hero of the Finn or Fenian Cycle, associated with the Fianna, a warrior band whose stories were preserved in Irish and Scottish Gaelic tradition. The wider Fionn tradition is very old: a Dublin Review of Books discussion of recent scholarship notes that the earliest known reference to Fionn may date as far back as the seventh century, while later medieval and early modern material greatly expanded the cycle.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://drb.ie/article/the-high-deeds-of-fionn/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: drb.ie">[Dublin Review of Books]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">drb.ie</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Dublin Review of Books The High Deeds of Fionn</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Dublin Review of Books The High Deeds of Fionn</span></span></span>

The Rostrevor story belongs to a popular place-lore habit in which Fionn becomes large enough to explain dramatic landscape features. This is not unusual in the broader tradition. The Fionn Folklore Database project, led by Prof Natasha Sumner, describes a corpus of about 3,500 stories and songs about Fionn and the Fianna, told for over a millennium and carried wherever Irish and Gaelic speakers went.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://natashasumner.scholars.harvard.edu/fenian-folklore-database" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: natashasumner.scholars.harvard.edu">[Natasha Sumner]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">natashasumner.scholars.harvard.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on harvard.edu.</span></span></span>

For County Down readers, the key point is that Cloughmore localises a much larger heroic figure. Fionn is not introduced here through the full complexity of the Fenian Cycle; he is made legible through the visible landscape. A massive boulder becomes his missile. Carlingford Lough becomes the gap he can throw across. Slieve Foy and the Mourne side of the lough become opposing platforms in a giant drama.

This is why the tale should not be treated as a poor substitute for geology. It answers a different question. Geology asks how a granite boulder came to rest on different bedrock. Folklore asks what such a strange stone means to people who live with it, walk to it, point it out, tell children about it, and use it as a marker of local identity.

## Carlingford Lough as a stage for giant conflict

Carlingford Lough is not just scenery in the Cloughmore legend. It is the stage that gives the tale its shape. The stone looks across to the Cooley side; the throw crosses water; the rival giant is imagined on or near the opposite hills; and the viewer standing at Cloughmore can test the story by looking across the lough themselves. Modern Visit Mourne material makes this viewpoint central, noting that on a clear day the Cooley Peninsula can be seen from the stone and that the supposed throw crosses the water from the far shore to the Down hillside.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.visitmourne.com/discover/cloughmore-stone" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: visitmourne.com">[Visit Mourne]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">visitmourne.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Visit Mourne The Stone a Giant Threw Across the Lough &#124; Discover &#124; Visit Mourne</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Visit Mourne The Stone a Giant Threw Across the Lough &#124; Discover &#124; Visit Mourne</span></span></span>

The lough also helps explain why the legend belongs to County Down while reaching beyond it. The stone is in historic County Down, above Rostrevor, but the imagined action depends on a cross-lough relationship with the Cooley Mountains in County Louth. That does not make the story less County Down. It makes it typical of border and shore folklore, where stories follow sightlines, travel routes and shared landscapes rather than modern administrative lines.

The UNESCO Geopark context reinforces this cross-landscape reading. The geopark includes the Mourne Mountains, Slieve Gullion and surrounding lough landscapes, and UNESCO describes the region as one where glacial landforms, human settlement, mythology and early Christian heritage sit together in a compact area of strong people-place connection.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.unesco.org/en/iggp/mourne-gullion-strangford-unesco-global-geopark" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: unesco.org">[UNESCO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Mourne Gullion Strangford UNESCO Global Geopark</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Mourne Gullion Strangford UNESCO Global Geopark</span></span></span>

In the Cloughmore story, that people-place connection is unusually direct. Stand by the boulder and the tale points your eyes outward: down to Rostrevor, across Carlingford Lough, over to Slieve Foy, and back to the stone under your hand. The landscape is not a backdrop to the legend; it is the legend’s evidence.

## Ice, granite and the usefulness of two explanations

The scientific explanation does not cancel the giant story. It gives the story a sharper edge. Cloughmore is puzzling because it is geologically out of place: a granite boulder sitting on metasedimentary rocks. The field guide calls it a glacial erratic and places it within a wider ice-shaped landscape around Rostrevor, Warrenpoint and Carlingford Lough.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cdn.ringofgullion.org/sites/4/2025/09/A_Geological_Field_Guide_to_Cooley_Gullion_Mourne_Slieve_Croob_.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cdn.ringofgullion.org">[cdn.ringofgullion.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cdn.ringofgullion.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">A Geological Field Guide to Cooley Gullion Mourne Slieve Croob</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">A Geological Field Guide to Cooley Gullion Mourne Slieve Croob</span></span></span>

It describes the boulder as Scottish-origin granite, deposited on a mountain top, locally famous and linked with mythology; it also records the modern 3-D scan and 50-tonne mass calculation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://mournegullionstrangfordgeopark.com/things-to-do/geosites/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: mournegullionstrangfordgeopark.com">[mournegullionstrangfordgeopark.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">mournegullionstrangfordgeopark.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Geopark Geosites</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Geopark Geosites</span></span></span>

This makes Cloughmore a good example of how folklore and earth science can sit side by side without needing to be confused. The natural history explains transport by ice sheets over immense time. The legend compresses the same visual puzzle into human terms: force, rivalry, aim and memory. Both explanations begin from the same observation — this stone looks as if something powerful moved it.

For a public folklore page, that overlap is more useful than a simple “myth versus fact” dismissal. The legend preserves the local astonishment. The geology explains the physical process. Together they make Cloughmore memorable, which is one reason the stone remains a successful visitor landmark rather than just another boulder on a hill.

## Why Cloughmore matters within County Down folklore

Cloughmore matters because it condenses several County Down folklore patterns into one place. It is a named stone with a memorable physical presence; it belongs to the Mourne edge of the county; it looks across a lough that connects Down with Louth; it gives Fionn a local footprint; and it can be read through both oral tradition and geology.

It also shows the difference between layers of tradition. The nineteenth-century O’Hanlon version records a contest with a Scottish giant. Modern local versions often foreground Ruscaire and a more elaborate battle. Geology identifies the stone as a glacial erratic. Tourism turns the combined story into a walkable heritage experience. None of these layers has to erase the others. For County Down, the power of the tale lies in the layering itself.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.mondelegendaire.com/en/articles/temp-1773837156-803434/lang/en/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: mondelegendaire.com">[mondelegendaire.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">mondelegendaire.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Monde légendaire</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Monde légendaire</span></span></span>

The result is a landscape legend with unusual staying power. Cloughmore is not hidden, obscure or purely literary. It can be visited, photographed, climbed to and argued over. Its story is short enough for a child to remember and strong enough to carry older themes: giant strength, rivalry across water, strange stones, visible proof and the shaping of the Mournes by forces larger than ordinary life.
