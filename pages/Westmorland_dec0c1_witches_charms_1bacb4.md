## Introduction

Witches, charms and village beliefs in [Westmorland]({{ 'westmorland/' | relative_url }}) reveal a world where healing, misfortune and mystery were explained through a mixture of practical knowledge, Christian faith, inherited custom and fear of unseen forces. In rural communities, a person known as a wise man, wise woman, healer or conjuror might be asked to treat illness, protect livestock, find lost property or break a suspected spell. These figures were not simply “witches” in the modern sense: local tradition often separated helpful practitioners from people believed to cause harm.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cunning_folk_in_Britain" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Cunning folk in Britain</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cunning folk in Britain</span></span></span>



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/Westmorland_dec0c1_witches_charms_1bacb4-overview.webp" | relative_url }}" alt="Overview image for Witches And Charms" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

The strongest Westmorland examples are linked to the tradition of the “wise folk” of the Lake District, especially figures such as the Lickbarrow family of Longsleddale and the reputed wise man Doctor Lickbarrow. Their stories show how real people with knowledge of herbs, natural phenomena or unusual skills could become surrounded by tales of magic. Modern research into Westmorland’s wise men places these traditions between documented rural history and later folklore, where fact and legend became closely intertwined.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/328655446_Science_Superstition_and_Sorcery_%27wise_men%27_in_Westmorland_during_the_seventeenth_to_nineteenth_centuries" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

## Cunning folk and local healers

### The wise people of Westmorland villages

In Westmorland villages, the boundary between healer, herbalist and magical practitioner was often unclear. A person who knew plants, understood animals, or seemed able to solve problems that ordinary people could not explain might gain a reputation for special powers. Across Britain such people were commonly described as “cunning folk” or wise men and women, providing practical services such as healing, protection, locating lost goods and counteracting suspected witchcraft.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cunning_folk_in_Britain" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Cunning folk in Britain</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cunning folk in Britain</span></span></span>

The Westmorland tradition of Doctor Lickbarrow is one of the clearest examples. Associated with Longsleddale, his reputation grew around stories of herbal medicine, mysterious knowledge and unusual abilities. Although later accounts gave him magical qualities, historians distinguish between the possible historical individuals behind the name and the folklore that developed around them.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doctor_Lickbarrow" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Doctor Lickbarrow</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Doctor Lickbarrow</span></span></span>

Research by Ian D. Hodkinson places the Lickbarrow stories alongside other Westmorland “wise men”, including Isaac Atkinson of the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries. The evidence shows that these figures could combine genuine learning or practical skill with a reputation for sorcery. A knowledge of herbs, astronomy, mathematics or natural events could appear mysterious in a rural society where such expertise was uncommon.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/328655446_Science_Superstition_and_Sorcery_%27wise_men%27_in_Westmorland_during_the_seventeenth_to_nineteenth_centuries" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

### Why villagers turned to them

The importance of these figures was not only supernatural belief. They filled a social role in places where professional medical care was distant or expensive. A farmer worried about sick animals, unexplained bad luck or a failed crop might seek help from someone believed to understand hidden causes.

Stories about wise people also reflect village concerns about trust and reputation. A respected healer could be valued as a protector, while a neighbour accused of using magic to cause harm might become the subject of suspicion. This tension between help and danger is central to British witchcraft traditions: the same community could respect someone’s knowledge while also fearing it.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cunning_folk" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Cunning folk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cunning folk</span></span></span>

## Charms, cures and protection traditions

### Everyday magic for illness and misfortune

Westmorland charms belonged to a wider northern English tradition of small protective actions used in everyday life. These practices were often connected with concerns that mattered immediately to rural families: keeping animals healthy, protecting children, preventing accidents and explaining illnesses that had no obvious cause.

A charm did not necessarily mean a dramatic spell or a formal magical ceremony. It might involve a spoken phrase, a repeated action, a blessed object or a traditional remedy passed through generations. Such practices existed alongside ordinary medicine and Christian devotion rather than always replacing them.

The distinction between “magic” and “medicine” was also less clear in earlier rural communities. Herbal knowledge could be respected as practical skill, while the same person might be credited with powers that went beyond physical remedies. The Lickbarrow traditions, for example, combine accounts of herbal treatments with stories of divination and supernatural ability.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doctor_Lickbarrow" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Doctor Lickbarrow</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Doctor Lickbarrow</span></span></span>

### Protecting homes, farms and animals

Many village beliefs focused on protection from unseen dangers. Livestock were especially important because a sudden illness among animals could threaten a family’s livelihood. A person suspected of witchcraft might be blamed for spoiled milk, failed butter-making or animal sickness, while a cunning person might be asked to undo the damage.

One recorded Westmorland tradition involving Isaac Atkinson tells of a farmer who believed a failure in butter-making had been caused by witchcraft. Atkinson’s supposed magical solution was actually linked to a practical explanation involving warmth and conditions for the process, showing how folk stories could preserve memories of both superstition and everyday knowledge.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/328655446_Science_Superstition_and_Sorcery_%27wise_men%27_in_Westmorland_during_the_seventeenth_to_nineteenth_centuries" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

These stories are valuable because they show how village belief worked in practice. Folklore was not simply a collection of strange tales; it was a way communities explained uncertainty. A charm or a wise person offered reassurance when illness, weather or bad fortune seemed beyond human control.

## From oral belief to modern retelling

### How stories changed over time

Westmorland’s witch and charm traditions survive mainly through oral storytelling, local histories and folklore collections rather than through large bodies of written evidence from the people who originally held these beliefs. This means individual details must be treated carefully. Some stories preserve memories of real people and customs, while others grew through repeated retelling.

The story of Doctor Lickbarrow demonstrates this mixture clearly. Historical traces connect the name with the Lickbarrow family and Longsleddale, but later generations added elements typical of wider British “wizard” and conjuror traditions. The result is neither pure biography nor simple invention, but a folklore portrait shaped by local imagination.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doctor_Lickbarrow" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Doctor Lickbarrow</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Doctor Lickbarrow</span></span></span>

### What these beliefs tell us about Westmorland identity

Witches and charms remain important in Westmorland folklore because they preserve memories of how rural communities understood knowledge, danger and healing. They reveal a landscape where a remote valley, an unusual neighbour or a skilled herbalist could become part of a larger story about hidden forces.

Today these traditions are usually approached as cultural history rather than proof of supernatural events. Their value lies in showing how earlier generations interpreted their surroundings and passed explanations from one generation to another. The old beliefs attached to Westmorland villages continue to shape the county’s folklore identity because they connect ordinary concerns — health, farming, family and survival — with the mystery of the unknown.
