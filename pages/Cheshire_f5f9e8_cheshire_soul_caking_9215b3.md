## Introduction

Soul-caking is [Cheshire]({{ 'where-cheshires-old-stories-still-live/' | relative_url }})’s most distinctive living autumn custom: a local form of souling in which performers visit pubs and villages around All Souls’ tide with a short, rough-edged mumming play, a begging or calling-on song, comic combat, death, revival, and often a snapping horse’s head. It matters because it is not just a remembered custom in a book. In places such as Antrobus, Warburton, Comberbach, Halton and Lymm, the tradition has been kept alive, revived, documented, and re-performed in front of modern audiences.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.lymmhic.co.uk/soulcaking" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: lymmhic.co.uk">[lymmhic.co.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">lymmhic.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">lymmheritagecentresoulcaking &#124; lymmheritagecentre</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">lymmheritagecentresoulcaking &#124; lymmheritagecentre</span></span></span>



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/Cheshire_f5f9e8_cheshire_soul_caking_9215b3-overview.webp" | relative_url }}" alt="Overview image for Soul Caking" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

For Cheshire folklore, soul-caking sits at the point where food, poverty, seasonal religion, village theatre, and the memory of the dead all meet. Older souling involved people going from door to door at Hallowtide or All Souls’ tide, receiving cakes, apples, drink or money in return for songs, prayers, or performance. The Cheshire version became especially memorable because it attached that autumnal begging round to a full dramatic custom: masks, stock characters, a fight, a quack doctor, Beelzebub-like figures, and the Wild Horse.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.winsfordhistorysociety.co.uk/2014/11/12/old-traditional-customs-soul-caking/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: winsfordhistorysociety.co.uk">[winsfordhistorysociety.co.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">winsfordhistorysociety.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">old traditional customs soul caking</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">old traditional customs soul caking</span></span></span>

## What Is Soul-Caking, and When Does It Happen?

Soul-caking belongs to the wider English custom of souling, associated with Allhallowtide: the season of Halloween, All Saints’ Day and All Souls’ Day. In its simplest form, souling meant visiting houses, singing or asking for food, and linking the gift of a cake with remembrance of the dead. English Heritage describes soul cakes as small cakes associated with Halloween and older All Souls’ customs, while local Cheshire accounts preserve the more performative form: groups moving through a village, singing, acting, and collecting food, drink or money.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.english-heritage.org.uk/halloween/soul-cakes/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: english-heritage.org.uk">[English Heritage]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">english-heritage.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on english-heritage.org.uk.</span></span></span>

In Cheshire, the timing is the key to the custom’s identity. The performances cluster around 1 and 2 November, with some groups extending the season across the following weekends. Master Mummers lists the Antrobus Soulcakers as performing “on All Soul’s Eve and the following 2 weekends”, while Calendar Customs places the Antrobus season in early to mid November, usually beginning from All Souls’ Eve on 1 November.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.mastermummers.org/groupslist.php?format=full&amp;oID=11" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: mastermummers.org">[mastermummers.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">mastermummers.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Directory of Folk Play Groups</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Directory of Folk Play Groups</span></span></span>

Warburton follows a similar seasonal pattern. Its souling play is described as taking place in early to mid November, based around the Saracen’s Head and other local pubs, with the name of the custom explicitly linked to All Souls’ Day on 2 November.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://calendarcustoms.com/articles/warburton-souling-play/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: calendarcustoms.com">[Calendar Customs]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">calendarcustoms.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Calendar Customs Warburton Souling Play</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Calendar Customs Warburton Souling Play</span></span></span>

This matters because Cheshire soul-caking is not simply “mumming in autumn”. Mumming plays exist in many parts of Britain at Christmas, Easter, Plough Monday and other calendar points. Cheshire’s special claim is the survival of a mumming-play tradition attached to All Souls’ tide, where the dead, the darkening year, begging, food, and performance remain visibly entangled. Lymm Heritage Centre puts the point strongly: in its local account, these traditional mummers’ plays are performed at this particular time of year in Cheshire, with active groups named at Warburton, Antrobus, Halton and Comberbach.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.lymmhic.co.uk/soulcaking" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: lymmhic.co.uk">[lymmheritagecentre]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">lymmhic.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">lymmheritagecentresoulcaking &#124; lymmheritagecentre</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">lymmheritagecentresoulcaking &#124; lymmheritagecentre</span></span></span>

## Why Cheshire Became the Stronghold

Soul-caking once belonged to a broader English and border-country world of seasonal begging songs, cakes and house-visiting. Cheshire did not invent the whole idea of souling, but it preserved one of the most theatrical and recognisable local forms. That distinction is important: the county’s living custom is not just the cake, nor only the song, but the complete autumn performance.

The strongest modern centres lie in the north and mid-Cheshire village landscape around Antrobus, Comberbach, Warburton, Halton, Lymm and neighbouring pub circuits. These are places of farms, lanes, village inns and older parish networks, which suited a travelling performance custom. A soul-caking gang could move from house to house in the older pattern, and later from pub to pub, carrying the same mix of entertainment, collection, local recognition and seasonal unease.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.lymmhic.co.uk/soulcaking" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: lymmhic.co.uk">[lymmheritagecentre]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">lymmhic.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">lymmheritagecentresoulcaking &#124; lymmheritagecentre</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">lymmheritagecentresoulcaking &#124; lymmheritagecentre</span></span></span>

Antrobus is the best-known example because continuity has become part of its identity. Master Mummers describes the Antrobus Gang as “well established”, with continuity as a distinguishing feature, and records past events across many years in the twenty-first century. The same listing notes that the group is thought to have performances continuously dating back hundreds of years, though that phrase should be read as a tradition claim rather than a precise documentary date.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.mastermummers.org/groupslist.php?format=full&amp;oID=11" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: mastermummers.org">[mastermummers.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">mastermummers.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Directory of Folk Play Groups</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Directory of Folk Play Groups</span></span></span>

The documented revival story is more concrete. Local Antrobus history records that a team performed up to the First World War, that the group was revived in the late 1920s at the instigation of Major A. W. Boyd, and that regular performance continued afterwards.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://antrobushistory.wordpress.com/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: antrobushistory.wordpress.com">[The History of Antrobus]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">antrobushistory.wordpress.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wordpress.com.</span></span></span> This is exactly the kind of layered evidence that makes Cheshire folklore interesting: not a perfectly unbroken medieval survival, but a custom with old roots, local interruption, named revival, and ongoing community practice.

## Masks, Songs, Visits, Food, and Performance

The older soul-caking round began with visiting. Performers, often described in local accounts as soul-cakers, soulers, or soul gangs, went from place to place at a difficult point in the rural year. Farm work was harder to find in the dark months, so the custom had a practical edge as well as a ritual one: food, drink, apples, small coins and hospitality mattered.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.winsfordhistorysociety.co.uk/2014/11/12/old-traditional-customs-soul-caking/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: winsfordhistorysociety.co.uk">[winsfordhistorysociety.co.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">winsfordhistorysociety.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">old traditional customs soul caking</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">old traditional customs soul caking</span></span></span>

The food gave the custom its name. Soul cakes are usually described as small spiced cakes or buns given to visitors at All Souls’ tide, with the gift tied to remembrance, prayer, charity, or good luck. Cheshire accounts often mention cakes and apples together, which helps explain why the custom could also be known in some areas as soul-appling.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.winsfordhistorysociety.co.uk/2014/11/12/old-traditional-customs-soul-caking/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: winsfordhistorysociety.co.uk">[winsfordhistorysociety.co.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">winsfordhistorysociety.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">old traditional customs soul caking</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">old traditional customs soul caking</span></span></span>

The song is another important layer. A Cheshire souling song appears in the Roud Folk Song Index tradition, and the Vaughan Williams Memorial Library has records for “Cheshire Souling Song”, including audio and text entries. The library’s own guidance explains that its song indexes point researchers to songs, dances and tunes held in its collections, including printed, manuscript and sound-recording material.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archives.vwml.org/record/RoudFS/S302977" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.vwml.org">[archives.vwml.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.vwml.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">CHESHIR E SOULING SONG</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">CHESHIR E SOULING SONG</span></span></span>

For a reader, the main point is not to memorise a single “correct” song. Souling songs varied by place, collector and occasion. What survives is a family of recognisable appeals: the performers announce themselves, ask for a cake, apple, drink or coin, and link the request to the season of souls. That makes the song both a door-opener and a ritual frame. It tells the host what kind of night this is.

## The Play: A Fight, a Death, a Doctor, and the Wild Horse

The Cheshire soul-caking play belongs to the wider mumming-play family. The plot is deliberately broad rather than subtle: a hero or soldier enters, a rival challenges him, a sword fight follows, one character is killed, and a comic doctor revives him. The humour lies in the exaggeration, the interruption, the impossible cure, and the rough energy of performance in a crowded room rather than on a formal stage.

Antrobus gives the clearest modern example. Calendar Customs describes the group as performing a traditional hero or combat play involving a sword fight with the Black Prince, who has to be revived by the Doctor, followed by the appearance of Dick the Wild Horse of Antrobus.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://calendarcustoms.com/articles/antrobus-soulcakers/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: calendarcustoms.com">[Calendar Customs]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">calendarcustoms.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Calendar Customs Antrobus Soulcakers</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Calendar Customs Antrobus Soulcakers</span></span></span> Local Antrobus history gives a similar outline: King George fights the Black Prince, the Black Prince is killed and revived by a doctor, and the last characters to enter are the Wild Horse and its Driver.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://antrobushistory.wordpress.com/2023/11/04/but-has-soul-caking-a-future/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: antrobushistory.wordpress.com">[The History of Antrobus]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">antrobushistory.wordpress.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The History of Antrobus But has Soul Caking a future?</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The History of Antrobus But has Soul Caking a future?</span></span></span>

The Wild Horse is the image many people remember. In Antrobus accounts, it is made from a real horse’s skull, painted black, with wired jaws that can snap at the audience, mounted on a pole and carried by a performer bent under a covering.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://antrobushistory.wordpress.com/2023/11/04/but-has-soul-caking-a-future/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: antrobushistory.wordpress.com">[The History of Antrobus]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">antrobushistory.wordpress.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The History of Antrobus But has Soul Caking a future?</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The History of Antrobus But has Soul Caking a future?</span></span></span> It is comic, alarming and uncanny at once: not a polished theatre prop, but a village apparition that can lunge into the room.

## Why the Custom Feels So Old — and Why That Needs Care

Soul-caking is often described as ancient, and in broad terms that is reasonable: All Souls’ customs, almsgiving, prayers for the dead, begging songs and seasonal cakes all reach back into medieval and early modern religious culture. But the exact Cheshire plays as performed today are better understood as layered traditions rather than museum-perfect survivals.

There are at least four layers to keep separate. First is the Christian All Souls’ practice of remembering the dead and giving food or alms. Second is the seasonal begging round, where poorer people, children or working groups asked for food, drink or money. Third is the mumming-play structure of combat, death and revival. Fourth is the modern pub-based performance circuit, where the custom survives through local organisation, charity collections, heritage interest and community pride.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.english-heritage.org.uk/halloween/soul-cakes/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: english-heritage.org.uk">[english-heritage.org.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">english-heritage.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on english-heritage.org.uk.</span></span></span>

That layered view avoids two common mistakes. The first mistake is to flatten soul-caking into “the origin of trick-or-treating”. There are obvious family resemblances — visiting, seasonal gifts, performance, food — but Cheshire soul-caking has its own All Souls setting, songs, adult performance groups and mumming-play structure. The second mistake is to treat every modern performance detail as medieval. Some parts may be old; others were reshaped by revival, local memory and the practical demands of performing in pubs.

The First World War is one of the clearest turning points in local accounts. Lymm Heritage Centre notes that the plays risked dying out, particularly after the war, when many who knew the tradition did not return. Antrobus history similarly presents the modern group as revived in the late 1920s after earlier local performance had continued up to the war.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.lymmhic.co.uk/soulcaking" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: lymmhic.co.uk">[lymmheritagecentre]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">lymmhic.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">lymmheritagecentresoulcaking &#124; lymmheritagecentre</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">lymmheritagecentresoulcaking &#124; lymmheritagecentre</span></span></span>

## The Revival That Kept It Alive

The revival of Cheshire soul-caking was not a vague folk-revival fashion imposed from outside. In Antrobus, the named figure is Major A. W. Boyd of Frandley House, who encouraged the late-1920s revival and helped preserve the local play tradition. Local Antrobus history credits Boyd with instigating the revival after performance had lapsed around the First World War period.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://antrobushistory.wordpress.com/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: antrobushistory.wordpress.com">[The History of Antrobus]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">antrobushistory.wordpress.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wordpress.com.</span></span></span>

This makes Antrobus especially valuable because its modern custom has both community continuity and a traceable preservation story. The performance is traditional, but the survival is also an act of local maintenance. Someone had to remember the words, gather the players, organise the route, keep the horse, negotiate with pubs, and persuade a new generation that this strange autumn drama was worth carrying on.

Warburton shows the revival process in another way. Its souling play is described as a revivalist side dating from the late 1970s, with the Saracen’s Head as its home base.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://calendarcustoms.com/articles/warburton-souling-play/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: calendarcustoms.com">[Calendar Customs]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">calendarcustoms.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Calendar Customs Warburton Souling Play</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Calendar Customs Warburton Souling Play</span></span></span> That does not make it inauthentic. In folklore, revival can be part of the tradition when a community repeatedly performs, adapts and transmits a custom in recognisable form.

The modern pub circuit also changes the meaning of the event. Older soul-cakers went house to house; today’s groups often move through pubs and clubs. Lymm Heritage Centre explicitly contrasts soul-caking with large open-air civic events such as the May Queen, noting that soul-caking is performed as a short playlet in pubs and clubs around All Hallows Day.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.lymmhic.co.uk/soulcaking" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: lymmhic.co.uk">[lymmheritagecentre]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">lymmhic.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">lymmheritagecentresoulcaking &#124; lymmheritagecentre</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">lymmheritagecentresoulcaking &#124; lymmheritagecentre</span></span></span> The shift from doorstep to pub has made the custom more public, more social, and perhaps more sustainable.

## Where to See Cheshire Soul-Caking Today

The main living and revival-linked Cheshire soul-caking groups are associated with Antrobus, Warburton, Comberbach and Halton, with performances also appearing in nearby places such as Lymm depending on the year and route. Lymm Heritage Centre names Warburton, Antrobus, Halton and Comberbach as the active groups in its account, while Master Mummers and Calendar Customs provide event-oriented listings for Antrobus and Warburton.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.lymmhic.co.uk/soulcaking" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: lymmhic.co.uk">[lymmhic.co.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">lymmhic.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">lymmheritagecentresoulcaking &#124; lymmheritagecentre</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">lymmheritagecentresoulcaking &#124; lymmheritagecentre</span></span></span>

Antrobus performances usually centre on the Antrobus Arms and pubs in surrounding villages. Calendar Customs describes the season as early to mid November, with performances from All Souls’ Eve and the following two weekends, commonly at several pubs in one evening.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://calendarcustoms.com/articles/antrobus-soulcakers/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: calendarcustoms.com">[Calendar Customs]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">calendarcustoms.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Calendar Customs Antrobus Soulcakers</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Calendar Customs Antrobus Soulcakers</span></span></span> Master Mummers’ listing records the group’s home town as Antrobus and identifies the play as a traditional Cheshire souling play performed on All Souls’ Eve and the following two weekends.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.mastermummers.org/groupslist.php?format=full&amp;oID=11" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: mastermummers.org">[mastermummers.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">mastermummers.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Directory of Folk Play Groups</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Directory of Folk Play Groups</span></span></span>

## Why Soul-Cakers Still Matter

Soul-caking still matters because it gives Cheshire a living autumn custom that is both local and unusually complete. Many counties have stories of ghosts, black dogs, witches, stones and haunted halls; Cheshire has those too. But soul-caking is different because people still perform it. It is not only a tale about the past. It is a repeated social act.

It also keeps several kinds of memory alive at once. There is religious memory in All Souls’ tide and prayers for the dead. There is rural economic memory in the old exchange of performance for food, drink or money. There is theatrical memory in the mumming-play structure. There is village memory in the named gangs, pubs, routes and families who have carried the custom. And there is material memory in objects such as the Wild Horse, which turns folklore into something you can hear, dodge and laugh at.

The custom also shows how folklore survives honestly. Cheshire soul-caking is not valuable because every detail can be proved ancient. It is valuable because the evidence shows a recognisable custom repeatedly collected, revived, adapted and performed in the same county setting. The Antrobus case has a local pre-war tradition, a named late-1920s revival, repeated modern performances and a distinctive horse figure. Warburton has a revivalist history from the late 1970s and a continuing November route. Lymm preserves local testimony and heritage interpretation. Together they show a county custom that has survived by being performed, not by being frozen.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://antrobushistory.wordpress.com/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: antrobushistory.wordpress.com">[wordpress.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">antrobushistory.wordpress.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wordpress.com.</span></span></span>

For Cheshire’s wider folklore map, soul-caking belongs beside [Alderley Edge]({{ 'alderley-edge/' | relative_url }}), Chester’s medieval performance culture, holy wells, haunted halls and seasonal village customs. It is one of the county’s clearest examples of folklore as a living practice: not simply a legend told about a place, but a place gathering each autumn to let the dead, the comic, the communal and the uncanny back into the room.

        .map(function (option) { return option.value; })
        .filter(function (marketId) { return !!markets[marketId]; });
    }
    return Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]'))
      .map(function (button) { return button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button'); })
      .filter(function (marketId) { return !!markets[marketId]; });
  }
  function applyMarket(section, marketId, persist) {
    var available = availableMarkets(section);
    if (available.indexOf(marketId)< 0) marketId = available[0] || defaultMarket;
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-localized-link]')).forEach(function (link) {
      var query = link.getAttribute('data-ebay-query') || '';
      var reference = link.getAttribute('data-ebay-reference') || '';
      link.setAttribute('href', buildUrl(query, reference, marketId));
    });
    var select = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-select]');
    if (select && available.indexOf(marketId) >= 0) select.value = marketId;
    var selectedText = marketId;
    if (select && select.selectedIndex >= 0) selectedText = select.options[select.selectedIndex].text;
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-selected-market-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = selectedText;
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-selected-market-flag], [data-ebay-trigger-market-flag]')).forEach(function (flag) {
      flag.className = flagClass(marketId);
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-trigger-market-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = selectedText;
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-domain-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = domainLabel(marketId);
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-option]')).forEach(function (button) {
      button.setAttribute('aria-selected', button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-option') === marketId ? 'true' : 'false');
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      var active = button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button') === marketId;
      button.setAttribute('aria-pressed', active ? 'true' : 'false');
    });
    section.setAttribute('data-ebay-selected-market', marketId);
    if (persist) {
      try { if (window.localStorage) localStorage.setItem('phoenix-ebay-market', marketId); } catch (err) {}
    }
    closeMenu(section);
  }
  sections.forEach(function (section) {
    if (section.getAttribute('data-ebay-localized-init') === '1') return;
    section.setAttribute('data-ebay-localized-init', '1');
    var available = availableMarkets(section);
    var marketId = defaultMarket;
    if (window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation && window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation.inferMarket) {
      marketId = window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation.inferMarket(available, {
        storageKey: 'phoenix-ebay-market',
        defaultMarket: defaultMarket
      });
    } else if (available.indexOf(defaultMarket)< 0) {
      marketId = available[0] || defaultMarket;
    }
    var select = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-select]');
    if (select) {
      select.addEventListener('change', function () {
        applyMarket(section, select.value, true);
      });
    }
    var trigger = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-trigger]');
    var menu = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-menu]');
    if (trigger && menu) {
      trigger.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
        event.stopPropagation();
        var expanded = trigger.getAttribute('aria-expanded') === 'true';
        menu.hidden = expanded;
        trigger.setAttribute('aria-expanded', expanded ? 'false' : 'true');
        var picker = trigger.closest('.fr-ebay-market-picker');
        if (picker) picker.classList.toggle('fr-ebay-market-picker--open', !expanded);
      });
      Array.prototype.slice.call(menu.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-option]')).forEach(function (button) {
        button.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
          event.stopPropagation();
          applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-option'), true);
        });
      });
      document.addEventListener('click', function () { closeMenu(section); });
    }
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      button.addEventListener('click', function () {
        applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button'), true);
      });
    });
    applyMarket(section, marketId, false);
  });
})();
