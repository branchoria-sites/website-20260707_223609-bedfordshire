## Introduction

Kinawley roads became “fairy paths” in local memory because this part of south [Fermanagh]({{ 'where-fermanaghs-water-keeps-the-stories/' | relative_url }}) gathered sacred wells, old coach roads, funeral routes, border lanes, graveyard customs and fairy-place warnings into the same everyday landscape. The tradition is not one single tale with one fixed location. It is a cluster of stories around St Naile’s Well at Kinawley graveyard, the old road from Dublin to Enniskillen, the Swanlinbar–Kinawley route, nearby fairy forts and the mountain paths towards Benaughlin. Together they show how rural people read roads and wells as charged places: useful, holy, dangerous, healing, and sometimes crossed by hidden traffic. The best-attested material comes from the Schools’ Collection on Dúchas, the Ireland’s Holy Wells County-by-County database, parish history, and later local-history retellings. The evidence is strongest for the holy well and its rules, and more scattered for “fairy roads”, where the idea survives through route stories, fairy forts, night journeys, music, lights and warnings about not interfering.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ihwcbc.omeka.net/items/show/573" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ihwcbc.omeka.net">[omeka.net]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ihwcbc.omeka.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Ireland&#x27;s Holy Wells St. Naile’s Well · Ireland&#x27;s Holy Wells County-by-County</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Ireland&#x27;s Holy Wells St. Naile’s Well · Ireland&#x27;s Holy Wells County-by-County</span></span></span>

## Where Kinawley Fits in Fermanagh Folklore

Kinawley sits in the southern part of County Fermanagh, close to County Cavan and the wider Swanlinbar area. That border position matters. The stories attached to Kinawley do not stop neatly at the modern UK border: the civil parish and local routes draw in Fermanagh townlands, Cavan townlands, old roads to Enniskillen and Swanlinbar, and upland paths towards Florencecourt and Benaughlin. For a Fermanagh folklore page, the centre of gravity is Kinawley village, St Naile’s church site, the graveyard and the old approach roads, but the oral evidence naturally spills into neighbouring Cavan because local memory followed parish, market and funeral routes rather than present-day administrative lines.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.duchas.ie/en/cbes/5044804/5040208/5083405" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: duchas.ie">[Dúchas]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">duchas.ie</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Dúchas Roads and Fords · Uragh (C.) · The Schools&#x27; Collection &#124; dúchas.ie</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Dúchas Roads and Fords · Uragh (C.) · The Schools&#x27; Collection &#124; dúchas.ie</span></span></span>

The village’s sacred focus is St Naile. The present Kinawley church is dedicated to him, and the parish website describes him as a sixth-century saint and friend of St Columcille who founded a church there. It also notes that the ruins of a medieval church survive, with Tobar Naile, St Naile’s holy well, nearby; a pilgrimage to the well formed part of the traditional Bilberry Sunday celebrations on the last Sunday in July.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://kinawleykillesherparish.co.uk/our-church/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: kinawleykillesherparish.co.uk">[Kinawley Killesher Parish]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">kinawleykillesherparish.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on kinawleykillesherparish.co.uk.</span></span></span>

That combination is the key to the local folklore. Kinawley is not remembered simply as a church village or simply as fairy country. It is a place where Christian devotion, older seasonal timing, healing water, graveyard customs, road memory and fairy caution all overlap. The well offered cure and blessing, but it also had rules. The road carried coaches, funerals, smugglers, walkers and imagined supernatural processions. The surrounding hills and forts supplied the otherworldly pressure that made ordinary travel feel risky after dark.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ihwcbc.omeka.net/items/show/573" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ihwcbc.omeka.net">[omeka.net]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ihwcbc.omeka.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Ireland&#x27;s Holy Wells St. Naile’s Well · Ireland&#x27;s Holy Wells County-by-County</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Ireland&#x27;s Holy Wells St. Naile’s Well · Ireland&#x27;s Holy Wells County-by-County</span></span></span>

## St Naile’s Well and Healing Traditions

St Naile’s Well is the best documented sacred site in this Kinawley cluster. Ireland’s Holy Wells County-by-County places it in the townland of Lismonaghan, in the civil parish of Kinawley, County Fermanagh. The well lies in the graveyard near the parish church of St Naile, on a slope below the ruins of a medieval church, probably of the fourteenth or fifteenth century. It is enclosed by a stone well-house, with access to the water through an iron gate.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ihwcbc.omeka.net/items/show/573" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ihwcbc.omeka.net">[Ireland&#x27;s Holy Wells]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ihwcbc.omeka.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Ireland&#x27;s Holy Wells St. Naile’s Well · Ireland&#x27;s Holy Wells County-by-County</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Ireland&#x27;s Holy Wells St. Naile’s Well · Ireland&#x27;s Holy Wells County-by-County</span></span></span>

The healing claim most clearly attached to the well is a cure for jaundice. The Schools’ Collection entry from Uragh says the water was a cure for jaundice and adds local testimony from Mrs Bella McHugh of Swanlinbar, who was said to have known two cases cured immediately after drinking it. The same Dúchas page also records another holy well in the parish of Kinawley, at Dromod, Swanlinbar, blessed by St Naile and associated with cures for toothache, backache and warts.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.duchas.ie/en/cbes/5044804/5040162/5083301" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: duchas.ie">[Dúchas]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">duchas.ie</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Dúchas Holy Wells · Uragh (C.) · Bailiúchán na Scol &#124; dúchas.ie</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Dúchas Holy Wells · Uragh (C.) · Bailiúchán na Scol &#124; dúchas.ie</span></span></span>

This is important because holy wells in Irish local tradition were rarely vague “magical” places. They often had a specific cure, a saintly origin story, a ritual calendar and local rules about proper use. At Kinawley, the cure for jaundice, the graveyard setting, the vanished penitential stone and the last-Sunday-in-July pattern all show a living devotional landscape rather than a decorative legend. Ireland’s Holy Wells County-by-County notes that although St Naile’s feast day is 27 January, stations were once made at the well on the last Sunday of July, suggesting an association with the late-summer festival season.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ihwcbc.omeka.net/items/show/573" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ihwcbc.omeka.net">[Ireland&#x27;s Holy Wells]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ihwcbc.omeka.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Ireland&#x27;s Holy Wells St. Naile’s Well · Ireland&#x27;s Holy Wells County-by-County</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Ireland&#x27;s Holy Wells St. Naile’s Well · Ireland&#x27;s Holy Wells County-by-County</span></span></span>

The saintly origin stories deepen the place-memory. One version says St Naile and St Ninian both wanted to build on the same spot, so they marked apples and dropped them into an upland pool connected by an underground stream to the lower well; St Naile’s apple emerged first, giving him the site. Another says St Naile struck a rock with his crozier and caused the spring to gush out. The Schools’ Collection gives a related object-memory, saying St Naile threw half his crozier into the well and that St Molaise’s bell was left there for a time. These are not historical proofs in the modern sense, but they explain how the well’s water was imagined as connected to saintly authority, hidden movement underground and sacred objects.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ihwcbc.omeka.net/items/show/573" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ihwcbc.omeka.net">[Ireland&#x27;s Holy Wells]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ihwcbc.omeka.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Ireland&#x27;s Holy Wells St. Naile’s Well · Ireland&#x27;s Holy Wells County-by-County</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Ireland&#x27;s Holy Wells St. Naile’s Well · Ireland&#x27;s Holy Wells County-by-County</span></span></span>

## The Trout Warning and Sacred-Place Rules

The most memorable Kinawley well story is the trout warning. In the Dúchas version, there were once two trout in St Naile’s Well. A man caught one, brought it home, and his wife cooked it. She died soon afterwards, the man went insane, and the second trout disappeared from the well and was never seen again. Ireland’s Holy Wells County-by-County records the same basic legend, showing that this was not an isolated stray detail but a recognisable part of the well’s folklore.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.duchas.ie/en/cbes/5044804/5040162/5083301" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: duchas.ie">[Dúchas]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">duchas.ie</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Dúchas Holy Wells · Uragh (C.) · Bailiúchán na Scol &#124; dúchas.ie</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Dúchas Holy Wells · Uragh (C.) · Bailiúchán na Scol &#124; dúchas.ie</span></span></span>

The point of the story is not really zoological. It is a rule-tale. The trout mark the well as a living sacred place, not a free source of food. Taking from it in the wrong way turns blessing into danger. In plain terms, the tale teaches that some waters are to be approached with respect, and that what belongs to the holy place should not be carried into ordinary domestic use as if it were ownerless.

That logic appears again and again in Irish well and fairy traditions: do not damage the place, do not remove what should remain there, do not treat ritual water as mere property, and do not interfere with beings or signs attached to a charged site. At Kinawley the punishment is severe, but that severity is what makes the story memorable. It turns a local well into a moral landmark: the place heals when approached properly and harms when violated.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.duchas.ie/en/cbes/5044804/5040162/5083301" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: duchas.ie">[Dúchas]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">duchas.ie</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Dúchas Holy Wells · Uragh (C.) · Bailiúchán na Scol &#124; dúchas.ie</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Dúchas Holy Wells · Uragh (C.) · Bailiúchán na Scol &#124; dúchas.ie</span></span></span>

The trout also connect the Christian well to a wider supernatural landscape. Fish in sacred wells often function as guardians, signs or taboo creatures. In Kinawley, the trout are not described as fairies, yet they behave like boundary-figures. They belong to the well’s hidden order. When one is taken, death, madness and disappearance follow. That is why the trout story sits naturally beside fairy-road beliefs: both treat the countryside as crossed by invisible ownership and invisible traffic.

## Old Roads, Funeral Routes and Why Paths Became Charged

The “fairy road” idea around Kinawley is best understood through old route-memory rather than through a single labelled fairy road on a map. Dúchas records that the old coach road from Dublin to Enniskillen passed through Drumbrughas, Drummod and Drumba directly towards Kinawley, about three miles from Swanlinbar, with a branch from Gortoral to Swanlinbar. The same account remembers old milestones built into a bridge and into a bank, showing how pieces of road history survived as local landmarks.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.duchas.ie/en/cbes/5044804/5040208/5083405" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: duchas.ie">[Dúchas]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">duchas.ie</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Dúchas Roads and Fords · Uragh (C.) · The Schools&#x27; Collection &#124; dúchas.ie</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Dúchas Roads and Fords · Uragh (C.) · The Schools&#x27; Collection &#124; dúchas.ie</span></span></span>

Another Dúchas entry on wakes and funeral customs gives a more intimate sense of how roads worked in community memory. It says a coffin might be carried on shoulders or on handspokes if the journey was long, that no work was done in the townland from the death until after the funeral, and that the coffin was left down on the boundary of the deceased person’s townland so the dead could “say farewell” to it. The same account identifies a place called the Whiskey Pass halfway between Swanlinbar village and Kinawley graveyard.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.duchas.ie/en/cbes/5044804/5040266" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: duchas.ie">[Dúchas]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">duchas.ie</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Dúchas Uragh (C.) · The Schools&#x27; Collection &#124; dúchas.ie</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Dúchas Uragh (C.) · The Schools&#x27; Collection &#124; dúchas.ie</span></span></span>

This helps explain why everyday routes became spiritually charged. A road to Kinawley graveyard was not just a transport line. It carried the dead, the mourners, the farewell at the townland boundary, the memory of shared drink, and the passage from home ground into burial ground. Once a route has that role, it is easy for later or parallel stories to imagine other processions moving along it: fairy funerals, night lights, ghostly travellers or strange companies that should not be interrupted.

In local belief, “fairy roads” often express a rule about passage. They are not simply pretty lanes. They are routes reserved, shared or remembered as belonging partly to hidden beings. Kinawley’s evidence points to a landscape in which several kinds of passage overlapped: saintly movement from spring to church, coach-road movement between larger towns, funeral movement to the graveyard, illicit or anxious night movement across border country, and fairy movement across forts, hills and old paths. The road became uncanny because it already carried too much memory to feel ordinary.

## Fairy Forts, Lights and Night Travel Near Kinawley

The fairy material around Kinawley is strongest in the wider parish and neighbouring route network. The Uragh Schools’ Collection records fairy forts in the parish, including one at Drumcondra near Swanlinbar, described as a large round hill sloping to a stream, with whitethorn bushes around it and a lone bush in the centre. Another fort at Drumbarr is described as similar, with lone bushes and a stream running into a spring separating it from the next hill.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.duchas.ie/en/cbes/5044804/5040202/5083401" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: duchas.ie">[Dúchas]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">duchas.ie</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Dúchas Fairy Forts · Uragh (C.) · Bailiúchán na Scol &#124; dúchas.ie</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Dúchas Fairy Forts · Uragh (C.) · Bailiúchán na Scol &#124; dúchas.ie</span></span></span>

Those details matter. Fairy forts, lone bushes, springs and streams are exactly the sort of features that made rural routes feel inhabited by more than human neighbours. The warning was usually practical as well as supernatural: do not cut the lone bush, do not level the fort, do not cross certain places carelessly at night, and do not assume that a field is spiritually empty just because it is farmed.

The Benaughlin and Play-bank stories, close to the Kinawley–Swanlinbar–Florencecourt route world, add the night-travel atmosphere. One Dúchas account says people who stayed late on the mountain heard mournful cries, while others claimed to have come upon the fairy host holding fairs on the Play-bank and buying and selling cattle. Another tells of two men who went rabbit-snaring at about two o’clock in the morning and heard beautiful music and the sound of thousands dancing above them, before fleeing towards Florencecourt Demesne.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.duchas.ie/en/cbes/5044804/5040169/5083327" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: duchas.ie">[Dúchas]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">duchas.ie</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Dúchas(no title) · Uragh (C.) · The Schools&#x27; Collection &#124; dúchas.ie</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Dúchas(no title) · Uragh (C.) · The Schools&#x27; Collection &#124; dúchas.ie</span></span></span>

These are not Kinawley graveyard stories in the narrowest sense, but they belong to the same local route system. They show the mechanism by which a road becomes a fairy path: a person is out late, away from the safe centre of the house; a short cut crosses a known uncanny place; music, cries or a procession appear; the traveller remembers that interference is dangerous; the story is then retold as advice about where not to linger and when not to take liberties.

## Fairy Funerals and Hidden Traffic

The specific phrase “fairy funeral” is not as securely attached to Kinawley as the trout legend is attached to St Naile’s Well. The strongest Kinawley evidence instead shows the ingredients from which a fairy-funeral tradition grows: graveyard roads, coffin customs, townland farewell rituals, fairy forts, night journeys, hidden companies, fairy music and warnings not to interfere.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.duchas.ie/en/cbes/5044804/5040266" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: duchas.ie">[Dúchas]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">duchas.ie</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Dúchas Uragh (C.) · The Schools&#x27; Collection &#124; dúchas.ie</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Dúchas Uragh (C.) · The Schools&#x27; Collection &#124; dúchas.ie</span></span></span>

Dúchas does preserve a nearby fairy abduction tale from the Swanlinbar-Corlough area. In it, a boy believed dead returns and tells his brother that he had not died but had been taken by the fairies, who left a false image in his bed. He says he and his fairy companions are going that night to take away a girl near Swanlinbar, and the brother is told he might recognise him riding a grey horse. The parish priest advises the brother not to interfere, and the brother never sees him again.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.duchas.ie/en/cbes/5044804/5040274/5083696" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: duchas.ie">[Dúchas]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">duchas.ie</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Dúchas Fairy Tales · Uragh (C.) · The Schools&#x27; Collection &#124; dúchas.ie</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Dúchas Fairy Tales · Uragh (C.) · The Schools&#x27; Collection &#124; dúchas.ie</span></span></span>

That story is not a funeral procession, but it belongs to the same belief-world as fairy funerals: the dead may not be simply dead; a body may be a substitute; the fairies travel in organised companies; the living are warned not to step into their traffic. In a district where actual funerals moved along named passes towards Kinawley graveyard, it is easy to see why fairy processions and fairy funerals would feel plausible to storytellers. Human processions gave the roads their emotional pattern; fairy belief supplied a hidden double.

The result is a landscape with two layers of movement. By day, roads connect farms, churches, markets and graveyards. By night, in story, the same routes may belong to others: the fairies crossing from fort to fort, the dead taking leave of a townland, the fairy host holding a fair on the hill, or a traveller meeting music where no human gathering should be.

## Why the Well and the Road Belong Together

At first glance St Naile’s Well and fairy roads might seem like separate topics: one Christian and healing, the other eerie and otherworldly. Around Kinawley, they work better as two sides of the same local grammar. Both are about how to behave in a charged landscape.

The well teaches respect through cure and taboo. Drink rightly and the water may heal; take the trout and disaster follows. The road teaches respect through passage and avoidance. Carry the dead properly, pause at the boundary, know the old pass, keep clear of fairy music, and do not interfere with a hidden company. In both cases the landscape is not inert. It has owners, memories, routes and rules.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.duchas.ie/en/cbes/5044804/5040162/5083301" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: duchas.ie">[Dúchas]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">duchas.ie</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Dúchas Holy Wells · Uragh (C.) · Bailiúchán na Scol &#124; dúchas.ie</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Dúchas Holy Wells · Uragh (C.) · Bailiúchán na Scol &#124; dúchas.ie</span></span></span>

This is why Kinawley is valuable within Fermanagh folklore. It shows a mechanism rather than just a monster or a famous legend. The supernatural attaches to ordinary rural infrastructure: a spring, a graveyard, a bridge, a milestone, a pass, a short cut, a fort-field, a lone bush. The stories make everyday movement meaningful. They tell people which places healed, which places punished, which roads belonged to the dead, and which night sounds were best left alone.

The material also shows how Fermanagh folklore often works across borders. The historic county focus remains Fermanagh, especially Kinawley graveyard and St Naile’s Well, but the oral landscape crosses into Cavan through Swanlinbar, Uragh and Corlough. That is not a weakness in the evidence. It is the point. Folklore followed the people who walked the roads.

## How Kinawley Is Retold Today

Today, the holy well is easier to verify than the fairy road. St Naile’s Well appears in dedicated holy-well databases, parish history, local-history writing and the digitised Schools’ Collection. The parish website still identifies the church’s medieval remains and nearby well, and links the well pilgrimage to Bilberry Sunday. Ireland’s Holy Wells County-by-County gives the site’s physical description, cure tradition, pattern-day memory and key stories.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://kinawleykillesherparish.co.uk/our-church/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: kinawleykillesherparish.co.uk">[Kinawley Killesher Parish]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">kinawleykillesherparish.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on kinawleykillesherparish.co.uk.</span></span></span>

The fairy-road side is less likely to appear today as a signposted tourist claim. The question was not only “Did people believe in fairies?” It was “Which path did you take after dark, what places did you avoid, and what stories told you why?”

## What Makes Kinawley Distinct

Kinawley’s distinctiveness lies in the tightness of the cluster. Many Irish and Fermanagh places have holy wells. Many have fairy forts or old roads. Kinawley brings several strands together in a small, readable landscape: St Naile’s graveyard well, a saintly foundation story, a trout taboo, a healing cure, a late-July pattern, old coach-road memory, funeral customs between Swanlinbar and Kinawley graveyard, and fairy narratives from the surrounding parish routes.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ihwcbc.omeka.net/items/show/573" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ihwcbc.omeka.net">[omeka.net]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ihwcbc.omeka.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Ireland&#x27;s Holy Wells St. Naile’s Well · Ireland&#x27;s Holy Wells County-by-County</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Ireland&#x27;s Holy Wells St. Naile’s Well · Ireland&#x27;s Holy Wells County-by-County</span></span></span>

The evidence should be handled with care. The well traditions are well attested in named folklore and heritage sources. The old road and funeral-route evidence is also clearly recorded. The “fairy roads” interpretation is a synthesis of route-based fairy material rather than proof of one officially named fairy road at Kinawley. That distinction matters, because it keeps the page grounded while still explaining what readers are really looking for: why local people imagined some roads as spiritually dangerous or invisibly occupied.

In the end, Kinawley’s folklore is about shared passage. Saints move through the land and open springs. Water moves unseen from hill-pool to well. Funerals move from townland to graveyard. Coaches move along the old road. Fairies move by night from fort to hill. The living move among all of these routes, guided by stories that say: drink with respect, do not steal from the sacred place, pause for the dead, leave the lone bush alone, and think twice before following music in the dark.
