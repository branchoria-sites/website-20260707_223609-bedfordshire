## Introduction

The county’s folklore is unusually rich because it sits where several story-worlds meet: Border reiver ballads, Lowland witch belief, fairy lore, early Christian sacred places, Roman and medieval ruins, and modern heritage retellings. Its strongest traditions are not dragons or mermaids, but fairies in Annandale, witchcraft records in Dumfries, ballads of violent border justice, holy wells around Lochmaben and Moffat, old riding customs, and haunted or “deil”-named earthworks whose mystery comes from landscape memory as much as from the supernatural.

## Where Dumfriesshire folklore lives on the map

For this project, Dumfriesshire is best understood as a historic county rather than a modern council district. Its folklore naturally overlaps with the wider Dumfries and Galloway region, but the Dumfriesshire centre of gravity is the old shire: Dumfries and Nithsdale in the west, Annandale through the middle, Eskdale and Langholm towards the east, and the Solway-facing parishes to the south. GENUKI describes Dumfriesshire as a maritime county on Scotland’s southern border, washed by the Solway Firth and adjoining Lanarkshire, Peeblesshire and Selkirkshire to the north.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.genuki.org.uk/big/sct/DFS" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: genuki.org.uk">[GenUKI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">genuki.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Gen UKIDumfries-shire, Dumfriesshire</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Gen UKIDumfries-shire, Dumfriesshire</span></span></span>

That geography matters because Dumfriesshire stories often attach to routes and edges. Annandale follows one of the great north-south corridors between Scotland and England. The Gazetteer of British Place Names notes that Annandale has served as a route for millennia, with old inns, Roman forts and later roads along its line.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://gazetteer.org.uk/place/Dumfriesshire" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gazetteer.org.uk">[Gazetteer]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gazetteer.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Gazetteer Dumfriesshire, historiccounty</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Gazetteer Dumfriesshire, historiccounty</span></span></span> Folklore here is therefore rarely sealed inside one parish. Border ballads cross into Liddesdale and Cumberland; fairy lore shades into Galloway and the Borders; witchcraft records sit within a wider south-west Scottish pattern; and customs such as common riding belong to a belt of Border towns rather than to Dumfriesshire alone.

## Fairies in Annandale: the county’s strongest supernatural tradition

The most distinctive Dumfriesshire fairy tradition is Annandale’s reputation as a last Border refuge of the fairies. George Douglas’s *Scottish Fairy and Folk Tales* preserves the literary-antiquarian version of this idea in the tale “Elphin Irving”, where the vale of Corriewater in Annandale is described as a place where old people still imagined intercourse with the “good folk”, and where fairies danced on hills, revelled in glens and appeared among ordinary people.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://sacred-texts.com/neu/celt/sfft/sfft81.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sacred-texts.com">[Internet Sacred Text Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sacred-texts.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Internet Sacred Text Archive Elphin Irving</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Internet Sacred Text Archive Elphin Irving</span></span></span>

This is important because it gives Dumfriesshire fairy lore a named landscape rather than just a generic Scottish fairy atmosphere. Corriewater, Annandale and Burnswark are not abstract “Celtic” settings; they are pastoral Border places where fairy belief was remembered through local storytelling, literary adaptation and antiquarian collecting. A Wigtown Book Festival extract from Hugh McMillan’s *Galloway Tales* repeats Douglas’s description of Annandale as “the last Border refuge” of the fairies, showing how that older phrase still shapes modern regional retellings.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.wigtownbookfestival.com/library/galloway-tales-fairies-and-plagues" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wigtownbookfestival.com">[Wigtown Book Festival]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wigtownbookfestival.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wigtownbookfestival.com.</span></span></span>

Not all these fairies are sweet. J. Maxwell Wood’s *Witchcraft and Superstitious Record in the South-Western District of Scotland* says that in Annandale “the great fairy strength and palace” lay in Burnswark Hill, and that the Annandale fairies were remembered as dangerous as well as beautiful: young men and women could be carried off, sometimes to serve the fairy world.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/files/43966/43966-h/43966-h.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on gutenberg.org.</span></span></span> Burnswark itself is also a striking real place, a hill with archaeological and Roman associations, which helps explain why it became a natural magnet for stories of hidden powers and underground courts.

This is a useful distinction for readers: Dumfriesshire fairy lore is partly oral tradition, partly antiquarian collection, and partly literary shaping. The old material is not “proof” of fairies, but it is good evidence that people in and around Annandale told stories about fairy abduction, fairy feasts, fairy music and fairy hills, and that nineteenth- and early twentieth-century collectors found those stories memorable enough to preserve.

## Witchcraft, fear and folk protection in Dumfries

Dumfriesshire’s witchcraft tradition is unusually well documented because it sits at the meeting point of folklore, law and local records. Future Museum’s online exhibition on witchcraft and lore in south-west Scotland describes how witchcraft lore in the region was expressed through superstition, anxieties, art, legal practice, religion and local belief.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.futuremuseum.co.uk/collections/features/online-exhibitions/witchcraft-and-lore-in-south-west-scotland" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: futuremuseum.co.uk">[Future Museum]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">futuremuseum.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Future Museum Witchcraft and Lore in South West Scotland</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Future Museum Witchcraft and Lore in South West Scotland</span></span></span> The University of Edinburgh’s Survey of Scottish Witchcraft is the major research tool for this wider history, recording nearly 4,000 known accused people in early modern Scotland and allowing searches by place, accusation type and outcome.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://witches.hca.ed.ac.uk/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: witches.hca.ed.ac.uk">[Witches]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">witches.hca.ed.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on ed.ac.uk.</span></span></span>

Dumfries itself has one of the county’s darkest and best-attested episodes. Wood’s account records the burning of nine women on the Sands of Dumfries on 13 April 1659, after they had first been strangled. He also quotes Dumfries kirk-session material from 1658 calling for evidence against suspected witchcraft, while warning that false accusations should be disciplined.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/files/43966/43966-h/43966-h.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on gutenberg.org.</span></span></span> The detail matters because it shows how “witch” stories were not just fireside legends: in seventeenth-century Dumfriesshire, they could become formal investigations, trials and executions.

Later cases continued this pattern. Future Museum holds records connected with a 1671 witchcraft trial, including an order concerning women suspected of witchcraft and a manuscript order stating that Elspeth Thomson and Janet McMuldroch had been found guilty.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.futuremuseum.co.uk/collections/features/online-exhibitions/witchcraft-and-lore-in-south-west-scotland/verdict--witchcraft-trial--1671" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: futuremuseum.co.uk">[Future Museum]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">futuremuseum.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">verdict witchcraft trial 1671</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">verdict witchcraft trial 1671</span></span></span> Historian Lizanne Henderson argues that Dumfries and Galloway was one of the last Scottish regions to initiate witch prosecutions and one of the most reluctant to surrender belief in witches, especially in the late seventeenth and early eighteenth centuries.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://eprints.gla.ac.uk/7706/1/Lizanne2.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: eprints.gla.ac.uk">[Enlighten Publications]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">eprints.gla.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on gla.ac.uk.</span></span></span>

For folklore readers, the key point is that Dumfriesshire witch tradition has two layers. One is the tragic legal history of accused people, mostly women, caught in systems of fear and punishment. The other is everyday folk belief: charms, protective objects, suspicions about illness or cattle, and the idea that harm could be caused by hidden forces. Modern storytelling, museum exhibitions and memorial discussions increasingly treat these stories less as spooky entertainment and more as a painful part of local memory.

## Ballads, reivers and the making of folk heroes

Dumfriesshire’s Border ballads turn violent history into memorable story. The best-known local figure is [Johnnie Armstrong]({{ 'johnnie-armstrong/' | relative_url }}) of Gilnockie, the Border reiver associated with Gilnockie Tower near Canonbie. Scotland Starts Here describes him as one of the most notorious Border reivers, linked with the Debateable Land and said to have been tricked into meeting the young James V at Carlenrig in 1530, where he and his men were hanged.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://scotlandstartshere.com/point-of-interest/johnnie-armstrong-memorial/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: scotlandstartshere.com">[Scotland Starts Here]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">scotlandstartshere.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on scotlandstartshere.com.</span></span></span> Gilnockie Tower’s visitor material presents the tower as the Armstrong ancestral stronghold and a place where Border Reiver history is still interpreted for the public.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://gilnockietower.co.uk/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gilnockietower.co.uk">[Gilnockie Tower]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gilnockietower.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on gilnockietower.co.uk.</span></span></span>

The ballad tradition is not the same as a court record. Modern ballad commentary notes that the execution of Armstrong and his men certainly happened, but the details of royal deception and betrayal are contested and may have been sharpened by singers and later writers.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://singyonder.substack.com/p/roud-76-johnnie-armstrong" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: singyonder.substack.com">[singyonder.substack.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">singyonder.substack.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ROU D 76: Johnnie Armstrong</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ROU D 76: Johnnie Armstrong</span></span></span> That is exactly how local legend often works: it takes a real event, keeps the emotional truth that people remember, and reshapes it into a story of pride, injustice and identity.

Johnnie Armstrong matters within Dumfriesshire because he links place, family, song and tourism. He is not merely a “criminal” or a “hero”; he is a figure through whom Eskdale and the Border country remember a world of fortified towers, cattle raiding, local loyalties and uneasy royal power. His story also connects naturally to neighbouring county folklore across the Anglo-Scottish border, where reiver families, ballads and tower houses belong to a shared frontier culture.

## Sacred wells, saints and Christianised water

Dumfriesshire’s sacred-water traditions are quieter than its witch and fairy stories, but they are important because they show how older landscape reverence was absorbed into Christian naming and local practice. *Folklore of Scottish Lochs and Springs* lists St Thomas’s Well at Lochmaben in Dumfriesshire, and notes St John’s Wells at Moffat and other Scottish sites.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/files/56034/56034-h/56034-h.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on gutenberg.org.</span></span></span> British Holy Wells’ Dumfries and Galloway list also records several relevant Dumfriesshire-area dedications, including St Mary Magdalene’s Well at Lochmaben, Our Lady’s Well at Closeburn, St Mary’s Well at Morton and St Ninian’s Well near Kirkbog.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://britishholywells.co.uk/scotland/dumfriesandgalloway/dumfriesandgallowaylist.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: britishholywells.co.uk">[britishholywells.co.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">britishholywells.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on britishholywells.co.uk.</span></span></span>

Lochmaben gives a good example of how sacred water, castle history and local memory can overlap. A local heritage account of Bruce’s Castle says the medieval fortress drew water from springs near St Thomas’s Chapel, and mentions another nearby fountain called the Baptism Well.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://lochmaben.net/home/heritage/bruces-castle/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: lochmaben.net">[Lochmaben in Context]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">lochmaben.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">in Context Bruce&#x27;s Castle</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">in Context Bruce&#x27;s Castle</span></span></span> This does not prove a surviving ritual cult, but it does show how wells and named springs were woven into the practical and sacred geography of the parish.

The Ruthwell Cross adds another kind of sacred landscape. Historic Environment Scotland describes it as a 5.2-metre carved stone cross from the early 700s, created when the kings of Northumbria extended their rule into south-west Scotland, probably functioning as a preaching cross for Christian teaching.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.historicenvironment.scot/visit/all/ruthwell-cross/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: historicenvironment.scot">[Historic Environment Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">historicenvironment.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Historic Environment Scotland Ruthwell Cross</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Historic Environment Scotland Ruthwell Cross</span></span></span> It is not folklore in the narrow sense of a ghost story, but it belongs on a Dumfriesshire folklore page because saints, wells, crosses and preaching places often formed the Christian framework into which older local beliefs were reinterpreted.

## The Deil’s Dyke and the mystery of old earthworks

Dumfriesshire has several places where folklore begins with a question: who made this thing, and why? The Deil’s Dyke is the clearest example. Trove, using Historic Environment Scotland records, describes it as an earthwork whose purpose and date are uncertain, extending from east of Afton Water to Burnmouth. Its surviving sections are rounded earthen banks, generally 2–4 metres wide and up to 0.7 metres high, with no clear defensive qualities; the record suggests it may be best understood as an agricultural boundary dividing upland pasture from lowland arable land.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.trove.scot/place/101258" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: trove.scot">[Trove.scot]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">trove.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on trove.scot.</span></span></span>

The name “Deil’s Dyke” itself is folklore-rich. Unusual ancient earthworks are often credited to the Devil, giants, Picts or other half-mythic makers because they seem too old, too large or too puzzling to fit ordinary memory. In this case, later scholarship has pushed back against dramatic explanations. A Scottish Archaeological Research Framework discussion notes that works such as the Deil’s Dyke have attracted competing theories because they are discontinuous and difficult to date, though at least one Dumfriesshire sector has produced early Iron Age dating evidence.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://scarf.scot/national/iron-age-panel-report/3-land-as-arena-place-territory/3-4-territory/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: scarf.scot">[Scarf]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">scarf.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on scarf.scot.</span></span></span>

That uncertainty is the point. The Deil’s Dyke is not a simple “Devil built a wall” legend. It is a case where landscape archaeology, place-name imagination and local explanation overlap. For readers, it is a reminder that folklore often grows where memory has a gap: when people inherit a bank, mound, stone or ruin but not its original story, a supernatural builder can step into the silence.

## Haunted castles and ghostly retellings

Dumfriesshire has the right architecture for ghost stories: ruined castles, tower houses, old kirks and graveyards. Caerlaverock Castle, south of Dumfries, is the most visually memorable. Historic Environment Scotland calls its triangular shape unique among British castles, with three curtain walls, corner towers, a twin-towered gatehouse, moat and drawbridge.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.historicenvironment.scot/visit/all/caerlaverock-castle/history-and-stories/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: historicenvironment.scot">[Historic Environment Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">historicenvironment.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on historicenvironment.scot.</span></span></span> Its official statement of significance also notes its literary fame through Edward I’s siege of 1300, commemorated in the contemporary poem *The Siege of Caerlaverock*.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://app-hes-pubs-prod-neu-01.azurewebsites.net/api/file/5d189dde-3d96-4cf5-857d-b0fb00e65162" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: app-hes-pubs-prod-neu-01.azurewebsites.net">[HES Publications]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">app-hes-pubs-prod-neu-01.azurewebsites.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">HES Publications Caerlaverock Castle Statement of Significance</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">HES Publications Caerlaverock Castle Statement of Significance</span></span></span>

Modern ghost tourism often attaches a “Green Lady” or other spectral figures to Caerlaverock, but these claims are much less securely attested than the castle’s medieval history. They are best treated as modern haunted-castle retellings: part of how visitors imaginatively experience the ruin, not as evidence of a long, well-documented oral tradition. The stronger folklore value of Caerlaverock is that it demonstrates how a dramatic historic place becomes a story magnet. Its shape, sieges, family history and ruined setting invite ghostly interpretation.

Kirkbride near Durisdeer offers a more local example. The ruined kirk and graveyard are linked in modern accounts to the ghost of Abraham Crichton, a provost of Sanquhar associated with the suppression of the old parish and a hidden-fortune tradition.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kirkbride%2C_Durisdeer" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Kirkbride, Durisdeer</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Kirkbride, Durisdeer</span></span></span> Here again, the interest lies in how moral memory becomes haunting: a man associated with loss, church closure and money becomes the subject of a restless local tale.

## Seasonal customs and living tradition

Not all Dumfriesshire folklore is supernatural. Some of its most visible living traditions are civic and seasonal customs, especially riding the marches. Dumfries’ Guid Nychburris is a modern festival rooted in the old practice of marking burgh boundaries. Calendar Customs describes the day as involving horses gathering early, riding out to meet the Pursuivant, Riding the Marches, returning for the reading of the charter, the crowning of the Queen of the South and a parade.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://calendarcustoms.com/articles/dumfries-guid-nychburris-day/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: calendarcustoms.com">[calendarcustoms.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">calendarcustoms.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Dumfries Guid Nychburris Day</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Dumfries Guid Nychburris Day</span></span></span>

Langholm Common Riding is even more strongly associated with Border identity. The official Langholm Common Riding site says the event began in 1759 and that its morning traditions continue in the modern day, while Historic UK describes it as one of the oldest common ridings, held on the last Friday in July and marking the right of common land.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.langholmcommonriding.com/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: langholmcommonriding.com">[Langholm Common Riding]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">langholmcommonriding.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on langholmcommonriding.com.</span></span></span> These ceremonies are folklore in the broadest and most public sense: repeated community performances that connect people to land, rights, memory and belonging.

The important thing is not to reduce them to pageantry. Riding customs carry old concerns about boundaries, defence and communal identity into a modern festive form. In a county shaped by border insecurity and routeways, the act of riding the edge of a place still has symbolic force.

## Modern retellings: museums, trails and local mystery

Dumfriesshire folklore is still being collected, curated and reworked. Annan Museum’s recent “Legends of Old Annandale” exhibition was promoted as covering myths and magic in Dumfriesshire, including dragons, giants, fairy knowes, protective objects and local curiosities.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.dailyrecord.co.uk/news/myths-magic-dumfriesshire-exhibition-33874807" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: dailyrecord.co.uk">[Daily Record]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">dailyrecord.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">myths magic dumfriesshire exhibition 33874807</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">myths magic dumfriesshire exhibition 33874807</span></span></span> Future Museum’s witchcraft exhibition makes archival objects and legal documents accessible online, helping readers separate documented witch trials from later spooky embellishment.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.futuremuseum.co.uk/collections/features/online-exhibitions/witchcraft-and-lore-in-south-west-scotland" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: futuremuseum.co.uk">[Future Museum]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">futuremuseum.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Future Museum Witchcraft and Lore in South West Scotland</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Future Museum Witchcraft and Lore in South West Scotland</span></span></span>

This modern layer matters because many readers now encounter Dumfriesshire folklore through museums, tourism websites, walking routes, local festivals, ghost tours and online storytelling rather than through family fireside tradition. Some retellings are careful and source-aware; others blend Dumfriesshire into the broader “Dumfries and Galloway is haunted” tourism image. The best approach is to ask three simple questions of any story: where is it located, who first recorded it, and is it old oral tradition, antiquarian literature, modern interpretation or tourist invention?

Dumfriesshire’s strongest folklore survives precisely because it can answer those questions. Annandale fairies have named collectors and landscapes. The [Dumfries witch trials]({{ 'dumfries-witch-lore/' | relative_url }}) have records. Johnnie Armstrong belongs to both history and ballad. Wells and crosses have physical sites. Common ridings are still performed. The county’s mystery is therefore not a vague paranormal atmosphere, but a layered local culture in which old belief, hard history, landscape and performance continue to speak to one another.
