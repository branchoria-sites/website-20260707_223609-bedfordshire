## Introduction

Linlithgow Marches are [West Lothian]({{ 'why-west-lothians-legends-still-hold-on/' | relative_url }})’s clearest example of a folk custom that began as practical civic housekeeping and became public ritual. In its older form, “riding” or “walking” the marches meant checking the boundaries of the royal burgh’s lands and rights, making sure neighbours had not encroached and reminding townspeople where their community began and ended. Today it survives as a summer civic ceremony of processions, music, officers, march stones and shared local pride, centred on Linlithgow, the historic county town of Linlithgowshire, now West Lothian. The tradition is not a supernatural legend, but it belongs firmly within county folklore: it turns law, land, memory and belonging into repeated performance. Its importance lies in that transformation. Linlithgow does not simply remember its old burgh boundaries in archives; it still walks, rides, marks and celebrates them in public.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://website715.wixsite.com/linlithgowmarches/copy-of-marches-guide-1" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: website715.wixsite.com">[website715.wixsite.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">website715.wixsite.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Marches History &#124; linlithgowmarches</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Marches History &#124; linlithgowmarches</span></span></span>

## What Riding the Marches Means

To “ride the marches” is to inspect a boundary. The word “marches” here means borders or limits, not a military march, although Linlithgow’s modern event certainly has processions, bands and ceremonial order. In medieval and early modern Scotland, a burgh’s rights could include common lands, markets, tolls, court privileges and sometimes outlying interests. A yearly inspection made those rights visible. It was part legal memory, part local administration, and part public warning: these are the lands and privileges of the burgh, and the community knows them.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://website715.wixsite.com/linlithgowmarches/copy-of-marches-guide-1" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: website715.wixsite.com">[website715.wixsite.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">website715.wixsite.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Marches History &#124; linlithgowmarches</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Marches History &#124; linlithgowmarches</span></span></span>

Linlithgow’s Marches are especially rooted in the town’s royal burgh identity. The National Records of Scotland notes that Linlithgow probably had royal burgh status from as early as the reign of David I, though the earliest surviving charter proving it as a royal burgh is from Robert II in 1388, sometimes wrongly given as 1389. As a royal burgh, Linlithgow enjoyed trading privileges that helped its early prominence. That matters because the Marches were never only a pretty parade; they were linked to real economic and jurisdictional rights.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://catalogue.nrscotland.gov.uk/nrsonlinecatalogue/person.aspx?code=NA22044" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: catalogue.nrscotland.gov.uk">[National Records of Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">catalogue.nrscotland.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Records of Scotland NRS Catalogue</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Records of ScotlandNRS Catalogue - person record...</span></span></span>

The Deacons’ Court’s own history of the custom connects the Marches to common lands, later charters and formal recognition of the burgh’s rights. It records that a James VI charter of 1593 and a parliamentary ratification of 1661 referred to rights and lands held by the burgh according to its yearly perambulation “in times bygone”. The same account explains the practical purpose: the authorities rode round the burgh boundaries to make sure those outside the designated areas had not encroached on burgh lands.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://website715.wixsite.com/linlithgowmarches/copy-of-marches-guide-1" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: website715.wixsite.com">[website715.wixsite.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">website715.wixsite.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Marches History &#124; linlithgowmarches</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Marches History &#124; linlithgowmarches</span></span></span>

For a folklore reader, this is the key point. Linlithgow Marches are not folklore because the boundary is imaginary; they are folklore because the community repeatedly acts out the boundary. Like gala days, common ridings, saintly processions or well-dressings elsewhere, the old function has become a local drama. The town rehearses its own shape, rank and memory in public.

## March Stones, Burgh Rights and Public Memory

The most tangible objects in the tradition are the march stones. They make an invisible legal line visible in the landscape. Linlithgow’s modern Perambulation of the Marches follows the route of the 1832 Parliamentary Boundary, later also used as the boundary of Linlithgow Town Council’s jurisdiction after the Burgh Police (Scotland) Act 1892. Visit West Lothian describes the route as a way for citizens and visitors to walk a recorded boundary of the royal burgh, while Linlithgow Burgh Trust notes that the walk is about 4 miles, or 6.5 km, and pauses at seven of the eight march stones.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.visitwestlothian.co.uk/things-to-do/walks-old/easy/perambulation-of-linlithgows-marches/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: visitwestlothian.co.uk">[Co]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">visitwestlothian.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">website Perambulation of Linlithgow&#x27;s Marches &#124; Visit West Lothian</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">website Perambulation of Linlithgow&#x27;s Marches &#124; Visit West Lothian</span></span></span>

That detail gives the custom a layered quality. The Riding of the Marches looks back to older burgh rights and outlying interests, including Linlithgow Bridge and the port of Blackness. The Perambulation, by contrast, offers a walkable civic route around a nineteenth-century parliamentary and municipal boundary. These are not identical things, but together they show how Linlithgow has kept turning changing forms of authority into a shared map.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.visitwestlothian.co.uk/things-to-do/walks-old/easy/perambulation-of-linlithgows-marches/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: visitwestlothian.co.uk">[Co]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">visitwestlothian.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">website Perambulation of Linlithgow&#x27;s Marches &#124; Visit West Lothian</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">website Perambulation of Linlithgow&#x27;s Marches &#124; Visit West Lothian</span></span></span>

The stones also connect the custom to specific places. The Perambulation starts at the first march stone outside St Ninian’s Craigmailen Church on Falkirk Road, with the Provost of the Deacons’ Court, flagbearers, halberdiers and a bagpiper; the piper plays at each stone. The route is not just a stroll around town. It is a guided civic circuit in which sound, office, stone and movement all tell walkers that the boundary matters.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://new.lbt.scot/lct/perambulation/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: new.lbt.scot">[Linlithgow Burgh Trust]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">new.lbt.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Linlithgow Burgh Trust Perambulation of Linlithgow’s Marches</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Linlithgow Burgh TrustPerambulation of Linlithgow’s Marches - Linlithgow Burgh Trust...</span></span></span>

There is a useful distinction here between old boundary inspection and modern heritage walking. A medieval or early modern perambulation helped defend property and rights. A modern public walk helps residents recognise the older civic geography beneath the present town. It lets people encounter Linlithgow not only as a palace town or commuter settlement, but as a former royal burgh whose boundaries had to be known, remembered and physically marked.

The route also anchors the custom in the wider West Lothian landscape. Linlithgow Bridge and Blackness are not decorative add-ons. The Deacons’ Court history states that the burgh charter conveyed to the burgesses and community the port and haven of Blackness, with customs, toll dues and court rights; it also notes that the holding of a court on Castle Hill on Marches Day and the appointment of a Baron Bailie continue this older custom. The Marches therefore bind the High Street, the burgh boundary, the River Avon side of town and the Forth-side port of Blackness into one civic memory.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://website715.wixsite.com/linlithgowmarches/copy-of-marches-guide-1" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: website715.wixsite.com">[website715.wixsite.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">website715.wixsite.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Marches History &#124; linlithgowmarches</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Marches History &#124; linlithgowmarches</span></span></span>

## How Old Is the Linlithgow Custom?

The safest answer is that Linlithgow’s Riding of the Marches is securely recorded from the sixteenth century, with plausible older roots in the town’s medieval burgh rights. The Deacons’ Court history gives the first recorded reference as a council minute dated 19 October 1541, when the common lands were ordered to be visited yearly on Pasche Tuesday. It also argues that the custom may already have existed on varying dates before that minute fixed a regular day.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://website715.wixsite.com/linlithgowmarches/copy-of-marches-guide-1" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: website715.wixsite.com">[website715.wixsite.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">website715.wixsite.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Marches History &#124; linlithgowmarches</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Marches History &#124; linlithgowmarches</span></span></span>

The date matters because it keeps the tradition grounded. Many local customs like to claim great antiquity, and some do so with more enthusiasm than evidence. Linlithgow has a stronger case than many because it can point to council records, burgh charters, later legal ratifications and continuing civic practice. The claim is not that every modern feature is medieval. The claim is that a boundary-inspection custom is well attested from the 1500s and has been repeatedly adapted.

The present annual timing has its own history. The Deacons’ Court account says the Marches are now held on the first Tuesday after the second Thursday in June, retaining an old reference to Holy Thursday; it says this arrangement was fixed by the Council in 1767, with the second Thursday fixed as the date of the Whitsunday Fair. Before that, the fair moved with the date of Easter.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://website715.wixsite.com/linlithgowmarches/copy-of-marches-guide-1" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: website715.wixsite.com">[website715.wixsite.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">website715.wixsite.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Marches History &#124; linlithgowmarches</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Marches History &#124; linlithgowmarches</span></span></span>

That link with fairs and church calendar time is important for understanding the atmosphere of the custom. Boundary rituals often sat at the meeting point of law, religion, seasonal gathering and public festivity. Linlithgow’s Marches were not merely an inspection tour by officials. They became a town day: a time when authority dressed itself up, music filled the street, trades appeared in order, and spectators became part of the performance.

## The Day as Civic Theatre

Linlithgow’s Marches are a living example of civic theatre: a town acting out its own constitution. The cast includes the Provost, Bailies, Deacons, halberdiers, flagbearers, bell ringer, town crier, bands, guests, riders, walkers and crowds. These are not random historical costumes. They represent older layers of burgh government, craft organisation and public authority.

The Deacons’ Court history preserves striking details of order and rank. It says the procession followed an order laid down by a council minute of 18 April 1687: Hammermen, Tailors, Baxters, Cordiners, Weavers, Wrights, Coopers and Fleshers. It also notes the roles of the Deacon Convenor, the Dyers, the Burgh Standard, and two flags known as the Craft Pinzel and Merchant Pinzel. Such details matter because they show the Marches as a ritual map of the old burgh community, not just a route around land. The town’s trades, offices and symbols all had their place.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://website715.wixsite.com/linlithgowmarches/copy-of-marches-guide-1" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: website715.wixsite.com">[website715.wixsite.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">website715.wixsite.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Marches History &#124; linlithgowmarches</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Marches History &#124; linlithgowmarches</span></span></span>

The custom has also changed. Up to 1834, the Provost and Magistrates rode at the rear of the procession, but Provost Adam Dawson reversed the practice and introduced carriages. The same history records complaints about reckless horsemen in the streets and even about bottles being taken from public dinners. These small unruly details are part of the folklore of the event: they remind us that customs survive not as frozen pageants but as negotiated public behaviour, with rules, jokes, excesses and reforms.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://website715.wixsite.com/linlithgowmarches/copy-of-marches-guide-1" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: website715.wixsite.com">[website715.wixsite.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">website715.wixsite.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Marches History &#124; linlithgowmarches</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Marches History &#124; linlithgowmarches</span></span></span>

In the present day, the Marches still include formal ceremony, but their public meaning has widened. They are a day of belonging for the town, involving local organisations and spectators as much as officials. The event remains recognisably about boundaries, but the emotional centre has shifted from preventing encroachment to renewing civic identity.

## Why the Deacons’ Court Matters

One of the most important modern chapters in the story came not in the Middle Ages, but in the 1970s. The Local Government (Scotland) Act 1973 reorganised local government, and old burgh councils disappeared in 1975. In Linlithgow, that created a practical and symbolic problem: if the town council no longer existed in its old form, who would carry the Marches?<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://website715.wixsite.com/linlithgowmarches/copy-of-street-fair" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: website715.wixsite.com">[website715.wixsite.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">website715.wixsite.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Deacons Court &#124; linlithgowmarches</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Deacons Court &#124; linlithgowmarches</span></span></span>

The answer was the Court of the Deacons of the Ancient and Royal Burgh of Linlithgow. According to the Deacons’ Court’s own account, the organisation was formed because townsfolk were determined that the customs should not die with the old burgh council. The name deliberately echoed the historic deacons of trade guilds: servants or representatives of town organisations who would serve the community by continuing the Marches traditions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://website715.wixsite.com/linlithgowmarches/copy-of-street-fair" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: website715.wixsite.com">[website715.wixsite.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">website715.wixsite.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Deacons Court &#124; linlithgowmarches</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Deacons Court &#124; linlithgowmarches</span></span></span>

This is a classic example of how living folklore survives institutional change. The legal structure that once supported the custom altered, but the community created a new voluntary body to carry the ritual authority. The Court includes deacons from town organisations, elected members and living Past Provosts; its prime function is to finance, organise and manage the Riding of the Marches. It is also a registered Scottish charity, and the cost of the day is substantial, reported by the Court as near £50,000.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://website715.wixsite.com/linlithgowmarches/copy-of-street-fair" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: website715.wixsite.com">[website715.wixsite.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">website715.wixsite.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Deacons Court &#124; linlithgowmarches</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Deacons Court &#124; linlithgowmarches</span></span></span>

The first Marches Day organised by the Deacons’ Court took place on 19 June 1975. With permission from the Lord Lyon, the chief officials were allowed to wear the red, ermine-trimmed robes of office once worn by dignitaries of the previous authority. That permission is more than a costume note. It marks a handover from municipal government to civic guardianship, allowing the old visual language of burgh authority to continue in a new constitutional setting.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://website715.wixsite.com/linlithgowmarches/copy-of-street-fair" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: website715.wixsite.com">[website715.wixsite.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">website715.wixsite.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Deacons Court &#124; linlithgowmarches</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Deacons Court &#124; linlithgowmarches</span></span></span>

For West Lothian folklore, this 1975 transition is crucial. It shows that “tradition” is not simply inherited; it is implemented. Committees raise money, appoint officers, negotiate permissions, manage safety, involve schools and organisations, and keep the calendar alive. Without that work, the custom would be an archive entry rather than a town event.

## The Perambulation as a Public Walk

The modern Perambulation of the Marches is one of the most accessible parts of the tradition. It complements the official Riding by letting residents and visitors walk the town boundary for themselves. Linlithgow Burgh Trust describes it as an annual event on the Wednesday before Marches Day, following a 4-mile route and pausing at seven of the eight march stones; the procession is led by the Provost of the Deacons’ Court, with flagbearers, halberdiers and a bagpiper.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://new.lbt.scot/lct/perambulation/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: new.lbt.scot">[Linlithgow Burgh Trust]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">new.lbt.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Linlithgow Burgh Trust Perambulation of Linlithgow’s Marches</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Linlithgow Burgh TrustPerambulation of Linlithgow’s Marches - Linlithgow Burgh Trust...</span></span></span>

This matters because it broadens the custom from spectacle to participation. A person watching a parade sees the town perform. A person walking the Perambulation feels the boundary through their own feet. The walk passes through ordinary streets and recognisable heritage points, turning everyday Linlithgow into a civic memory trail. Visit West Lothian notes that the route can be walked at any time and highlights nearby points of interest such as Linlithgow Palace, the Ross Doocot, the Union Canal and the Canal Basin.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.visitwestlothian.co.uk/things-to-do/walks-old/easy/perambulation-of-linlithgows-marches/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: visitwestlothian.co.uk">[Co]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">visitwestlothian.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">website Perambulation of Linlithgow&#x27;s Marches &#124; Visit West Lothian</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">website Perambulation of Linlithgow&#x27;s Marches &#124; Visit West Lothian</span></span></span>

The route’s nineteenth-century basis also makes it a useful reminder that boundaries are not single, eternal lines. Linlithgow has royal burgh memories, parliamentary boundaries, municipal boundaries and modern council-area identities. The Perambulation does not erase those differences; it makes one historically recorded boundary legible to walkers. In a county folklore project, that is exactly the kind of practice that helps people understand how legal geography becomes local feeling.

The march stones themselves are modest objects, but their ritual use gives them weight. A stone inscribed with a boundary mark is not dramatic on its own. A stone visited each year by a town procession, accompanied by music and officers, becomes a memory device. It teaches people where the old line ran and why that line mattered.

## Linlithgow Among Scotland’s Boundary Customs

Linlithgow’s Marches belong to a wider Scottish family of common ridings and riding-the-marches customs. Intangible Cultural Heritage Scotland lists Common Ridings or Riding of the Marches as a festival tradition associated especially with the Scottish Borders, involving riders, costumes, musical instruments and administrative support. Many Borders towns use such events to remember common lands, burgh identity and, in some cases, Border conflict.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ichscotland.org/wiki/common-ridings-riding-marches" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ichscotland.org">[ichscotland.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ichscotland.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Common Ridings / Riding of the Marches &#124; ICH Scotland Wiki</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Common Ridings / Riding of the Marches &#124; ICH Scotland Wiki</span></span></span>

Linlithgow is different because it is not primarily a Border reiving or Flodden-memory custom. Its centre of gravity is Lowland burgh law, royal status, trade, local government and West Lothian civic pride. That distinction matters. Calling it a common riding is useful, but the local meaning is not interchangeable with Hawick, Selkirk or Lauder. Linlithgow’s Marches express the identity of a palace town and former county town whose burgh rights reached beyond the High Street to Linlithgow Bridge and Blackness.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://catalogue.nrscotland.gov.uk/nrsonlinecatalogue/person.aspx?code=NA22044" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: catalogue.nrscotland.gov.uk">[National Records of Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">catalogue.nrscotland.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Records of Scotland NRS Catalogue</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Records of ScotlandNRS Catalogue - person record...</span></span></span>

The custom also connects Linlithgow to other burghs. The Deacons’ Court history notes that burgh councils visited one another’s ridings, citing a Lanark record from 1586 in which John Bruce was paid for going to Linlithgow to see whether the Provost would come to the Lanark Riding. It adds that this connection continues through participation between Linlithgow and Lanark’s Lanimer Day traditions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://website715.wixsite.com/linlithgowmarches/copy-of-marches-guide-1" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: website715.wixsite.com">[website715.wixsite.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">website715.wixsite.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Marches History &#124; linlithgowmarches</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Marches History &#124; linlithgowmarches</span></span></span>

That inter-burgh relationship is a reminder that civic customs were never isolated curiosities. They formed a network of recognition. When one burgh attended another’s Marches, it acknowledged a shared language of rights, office, ceremony and public memory. For a folklore map of West Lothian, Linlithgow therefore acts as both a local anchor and a bridge to wider Lowland Scottish ritual culture.

## Why This Custom Still Matters in West Lothian

Linlithgow Marches matter because they keep an older West Lothian identity visible. Modern West Lothian is shaped by new towns, industry, commuting, local government reorganisation and changing administrative boundaries. Linlithgow’s Marches belong to a deeper county layer, when Linlithgowshire took its name from the burgh and the town’s royal, legal and market privileges mattered to the surrounding landscape.

The event also shows that folklore is not only about strange creatures or ghostly stories. West Lothian has those too: the [Black Bitch]({{ 'black-bitch/' | relative_url }}) of Linlithgow, haunted palace traditions, witchcraft records, saints’ sites and prehistoric ritual landscapes all sit elsewhere in the county’s story. The Marches add a different kind of enchantment: the power of repetition. A boundary walked once is administration. A boundary walked for centuries becomes identity.

The 2025 involvement of the Royal Regiment of Scotland shows how the custom can absorb modern ceremonial relationships while still pointing back to older forms of authority. The British Army reported that the Riding of the Marches dates to the sixteenth century, that the Deacons’ Court took stewardship in 1975, and that the regiment exercised the Freedom of West Lothian as part of the event. The report also quoted Major General Bob Bruce describing the Linlithgow Marches as an ancient ceremony embraced with enthusiasm and pride.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.army.mod.uk/news/royal-regiment-of-scotland-lead-linlithgow-marches/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: army.mod.uk">[British Army]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">army.mod.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on mod.uk.</span></span></span>

That modern layer should not distract from the older heart of the custom. The Marches are still about a town knowing itself through its edges. They make the boundary social, musical and memorable. They turn stones into stopping places, offices into roles, and old rights into public belonging.

## A Living Boundary, Not a Relic

The most useful way to understand Linlithgow Marches is not as a relic, but as a working tradition. Its original legal purpose has faded, but the custom still does civic work. It gathers organisations, teaches local geography, links generations, raises funds, brings people into the street, and reminds West Lothian that historic county identity is made from places people continue to use.

There is also a quiet tension inside the tradition, and that tension is part of its value. The Marches look ancient, but they have been repeatedly adjusted: dates fixed, processional order formalised, carriages introduced, burgh government abolished, the Deacons’ Court created, modern march stones installed, and public walking routes promoted for visitors. Each change could have weakened the custom. Instead, each has become another layer in its story.

That is why Linlithgow still walks its boundaries. Not because the town needs a yearly legal patrol to defend its medieval rights, but because the act of walking, riding, marking and watching still says something powerful: this place has a memory, and the memory has a route.

        menu.hidden = expanded;
        trigger.setAttribute('aria-expanded', expanded ? 'false' : 'true');
        var picker = trigger.closest('.fr-ebay-market-picker');
        if (picker) picker.classList.toggle('fr-ebay-market-picker--open', !expanded);
      });
      Array.prototype.slice.call(menu.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-option]')).forEach(function (button) {
        button.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
          event.stopPropagation();
          applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-option'), true);
        });
      });
      document.addEventListener('click', function () { closeMenu(section); });
    }
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      button.addEventListener('click', function () {
        applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button'), true);
      });
    });
    applyMarket(section, marketId, false);
  });
