## Introduction

St Swithun still “rules the rain” because Winchester gave [Hampshire]({{ 'where-hampshires-old-stories-still-haunt/' | relative_url }}) a saint whose feast day became one of the best-known weather sayings in England. The tradition is simple: if it rains on St Swithun’s Day, 15 July, rain will follow for forty days; if the day is fair, the weather will stay fair. The strict forecast is not true in a modern meteorological sense, but the proverb has lasted because it ties together a saint’s cult, Winchester Cathedral, the English summer, and Hampshire’s modern county identity. St Swithun was a ninth-century Bishop of Winchester whose body was moved to a more honourable shrine on 15 July 971; later folklore turned that act into a story of saintly displeasure, rain, and a memorable forty-day warning.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://weather.metoffice.gov.uk/learn-about/weather/how-weather-works/red-sky-at-night" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: weather.metoffice.gov.uk">[Met Office]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">weather.metoffice.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Met Office Red sky at night and other weather lore</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Met OfficeRed sky at night and other weather lore - Met Office...</span></span></span>

## The saint, shrine, and Winchester setting

The historical Swithun is both famous and elusive. Winchester Cathedral itself describes him as a “shadowy historical figure”, a secular cleric whose name and relics were later used by Bishop Æthelwold in the tenth-century reform of the Winchester church. That matters because the folklore does not rest on a full, reliable biography of a saintly rainmaker. It rests on the way a half-remembered bishop became a powerful local symbol after his death.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.winchester-cathedral.org.uk/news/matt-11-28-30-st-swithuns-day-eucharist/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: winchester-cathedral.org.uk">[winchester-cathedral.org.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">winchester-cathedral.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Winchester Cathedral &#124; Matt 11.28-30, St Swithun’s Day, Eucharist</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Winchester Cathedral &#124; Matt 11.28-30, St Swithun’s Day, Eucharist</span></span></span>

The usual outline is that Swithun served as Bishop of Winchester in the ninth century and died in 862 or 863. Hampshire County Council’s St Swithun’s Way page places him firmly in Winchester’s Wessex world, describing him as born in Winchester, serving in the royal household, becoming Bishop of Winchester in 852, and being buried in a simple grave outside the Saxon cathedral. That same source also preserves one of the humanising details often attached to him: he was remembered for rebuilding Winchester’s East Gate bridge.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.hants.gov.uk/thingstodo/countryside/walking/stswithunsway" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: hants.gov.uk">[hants.gov.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">hants.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">St Swithuns Way &#124; Things to do in Hampshire &#124; Hampshire County Council</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">St Swithuns Way &#124; Things to do in Hampshire &#124; Hampshire County Council</span></span></span>

The decisive folklore date is not the day of his death but the day of his translation. In church history, a “translation” means the moving of a saint’s remains to a new resting place or shrine. On 15 July 971, more than a century after Swithun’s death, his remains were moved from an outdoor grave into Winchester’s Old Minster. Later tradition said the saint had wanted to remain outside, where rain could fall on him and ordinary people could pass by, so the move indoors was reimagined as the cause of extraordinary rain.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://weather.metoffice.gov.uk/learn-about/weather/how-weather-works/red-sky-at-night" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: weather.metoffice.gov.uk">[Met Office]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">weather.metoffice.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Met Office Red sky at night and other weather lore</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Met OfficeRed sky at night and other weather lore - Met Office...</span></span></span>

This is why the story belongs so strongly to Hampshire rather than floating free as a general British proverb. Its landscape is precise: Winchester, the Old Minster, the later cathedral, the saint’s shrine, and the city’s watery association with rain, bridge, and pilgrimage. Even when the saying is quoted across Britain, its roots point back to a particular county town and a particular sacred site.

Winchester’s importance also helps explain why the cult could travel. Hampshire County Council describes medieval Winchester as a centre of royal and ecclesiastical power, with the shrine of St Swithun and the tomb of Alfred the Great making the city a major place of pilgrimage before Canterbury became dominant after the death of Thomas Becket. The modern St Swithun’s Way, a 34-mile route from Winchester to Farnham, continues that pilgrim memory in the landscape, using waymarks based on a shell and two croziers.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.hants.gov.uk/thingstodo/countryside/walking/stswithunsway" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: hants.gov.uk">[hants.gov.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">hants.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">St Swithuns Way &#124; Things to do in Hampshire &#124; Hampshire County Council</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">St Swithuns Way &#124; Things to do in Hampshire &#124; Hampshire County Council</span></span></span>

## The forty-day weather proverb

The best-known form of the rhyme runs: if St Swithun’s Day is rainy, rain remains for forty days; if it is fair, rain will come no more for forty days. The Met Office gives a common version of the saying and explains the attached legend: Swithun asked to be buried outside, his body was moved indoors on 15 July, and the rain that followed was read as the saint’s anger at being disturbed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://weather.metoffice.gov.uk/learn-about/weather/how-weather-works/red-sky-at-night" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: weather.metoffice.gov.uk">[Met Office]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">weather.metoffice.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Met Office Red sky at night and other weather lore</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Met OfficeRed sky at night and other weather lore - Met Office...</span></span></span>

The Royal Meteorological Society is more cautious about the origin story. It notes that the earliest surviving written reference to the St Swithun weather legend dates back to the fourteenth century, while also warning that the reburial-rain explanation has a major problem: early accounts of the translation do not mention rain. In other words, the famous story may be a later explanation grafted onto an older or wider habit of treating certain feast days as weather markers.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.rmets.org/metmatters/behind-folklore-st-swithins-day-does-rain-today-really-mean-ruined-summer" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: rmets.org">[RMetS]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">rmets.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on rmets.org.</span></span></span>

That caution is important for reading the tradition honestly. The rain story is folklore, not a weather record from 971. It tells us how later generations understood Swithun: humble, close to ordinary people, resistant to pomp, and powerful enough to make the heavens answer human pride. The saint’s outdoor grave becomes the key image. Rain is not just rain; it is the sign of a saint who supposedly preferred the open air to a splendid shrine.

The proverb also belongs to a wider European pattern. The Royal Meteorological Society points out that similar weather beliefs were attached to other feast days, including St Medard in France and other northern European traditions. That does not make St Swithun less Hampshire-based. It shows how local saints could be fitted into broader seasonal thinking, with each region giving the same human anxiety — “what will the summer do next?” — its own holy name and local story.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.rmets.org/metmatters/behind-folklore-st-swithins-day-does-rain-today-really-mean-ruined-summer" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: rmets.org">[RMetS]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">rmets.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on rmets.org.</span></span></span>

The forty-day number gives the lore its staying power. It is long enough to feel like a season, but short enough to be watched. From 15 July, forty days takes the imagination through the heart of late summer: harvest weather, school holidays, outdoor work, travel, gardens, and village events. That is why the rhyme still feels useful even when people know it is not literally reliable.

## Does the weather lore work?

As a strict forecast, St Swithun’s Day does not work. The Met Office states that, since records began, there has been no occurrence of rainfall for such a prolonged period as forty days and nights. The Royal Meteorological Society gives the same broad verdict even more sharply: there has not been a UK summer with a single forty-day drought, nor any time of year with forty consecutive days of rainfall.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://weather.metoffice.gov.uk/learn-about/weather/how-weather-works/red-sky-at-night" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: weather.metoffice.gov.uk">[Met Office]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">weather.metoffice.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Met Office Red sky at night and other weather lore</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Met OfficeRed sky at night and other weather lore - Met Office...</span></span></span>

Yet the saying is not complete nonsense as seasonal observation. Both the Met Office and the Royal Meteorological Society point to the jet stream as the small grain of weather sense behind the proverb. Around late June and mid-July, the position of the jet stream can help shape whether the UK experiences a more settled, dry spell or a cooler, wetter Atlantic pattern. A northerly jet stream can allow high pressure and brighter weather; a southerly track can bring more unsettled conditions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://weather.metoffice.gov.uk/learn-about/weather/how-weather-works/red-sky-at-night" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: weather.metoffice.gov.uk">[Met Office]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">weather.metoffice.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Met Office Red sky at night and other weather lore</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Met OfficeRed sky at night and other weather lore - Met Office...</span></span></span>

This makes the proverb a good example of how folklore can compress observation into a memorable rule. It is not that rain on one feast day magically causes rain for forty days. It is that mid-July often arrives when larger summer weather patterns are becoming visible. The rhyme turns that pattern into a dramatic yes-or-no test: look at the sky over Winchester on 15 July and imagine the rest of the summer answering.

That also explains why the saying remains popular with newspapers, weather presenters, gardeners, and local history writers. It is easy to check, easy to quote, and easy to argue with. A wet St Swithun’s Day invites mock dread; a sunny one invites playful relief. The point is less prediction than participation. People retell the rhyme because it gives ordinary weather a ritual date.

## Why Winchester makes the legend memorable

St Swithun’s story is unusually compact: a humble bishop, an outdoor grave, an indoor shrine, a storm, and a proverb. Winchester gives each part a setting. The city was not merely where Swithun happened to live; it was the religious and political centre that allowed his cult to grow. Winchester Cathedral’s own reflection on the saint stresses that later accounts of him were written a century after his life and were shaped by Æthelwold’s reforming church. That makes the legend a product of institutional memory as much as oral fancy.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.winchester-cathedral.org.uk/news/matt-11-28-30-st-swithuns-day-eucharist/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: winchester-cathedral.org.uk">[winchester-cathedral.org.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">winchester-cathedral.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Winchester Cathedral &#124; Matt 11.28-30, St Swithun’s Day, Eucharist</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Winchester Cathedral &#124; Matt 11.28-30, St Swithun’s Day, Eucharist</span></span></span>

The shrine mattered because saints in medieval England were not only remembered; they were visited. A saint’s relics could draw pilgrims, gifts, healing hopes, and prestige. Hampshire County Council’s account of the pilgrimage route from Winchester towards Canterbury shows how Swithun’s shrine sat within a larger medieval map of movement, devotion, and sacred travel.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.hants.gov.uk/thingstodo/countryside/walking/stswithunsway" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: hants.gov.uk">[hants.gov.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">hants.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">St Swithuns Way &#124; Things to do in Hampshire &#124; Hampshire County Council</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">St Swithuns Way &#124; Things to do in Hampshire &#124; Hampshire County Council</span></span></span>

The folklore also keeps alive the tension between humility and honour. In the story, Swithun wants a modest grave open to rain and footsteps. Church authorities give him a grand indoor shrine. The weather then becomes a kind of moral commentary. Whether or not anyone in 971 saw it that way, the later tale made the landscape preach: holiness does not always want splendour.

That is a very Hampshire kind of legend because it joins national history to a walkable place. A visitor can stand in Winchester, think of the Old Minster beneath the cathedral close, visit the cathedral, and then hear the rhyme differently. The proverb is not just something said on the radio each July. It is attached to stone, burial, reform, pilgrimage, and the weather over the Itchen valley.

## Hampshire Day and modern retellings

St Swithun’s Day has recently gained a second public life as Hampshire Day. The Flag Institute recognised Hampshire’s county flag and county day in 2019, recording 15 July as the obvious date because it is St Swithun’s Day and because Swithun is treated as patron saint of Winchester and Hampshire. The same announcement notes the popular belief that rain on St Swithun’s Day means another forty days of rain, showing how the weather proverb helped make the date instantly recognisable.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.flaginstitute.org/wp/uk-flag-registry-news/hampshire-flag-day-recognised/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: flaginstitute.org">[The Flag Institute]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">flaginstitute.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Flag Institute Hampshire Flag &amp; Day recognised &#124; The Flag Institute</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Flag Institute Hampshire Flag &amp; Day recognised &#124; The Flag Institute</span></span></span>

Hampshire Cultural Trust likewise describes 15 July 2019 as the first Hampshire Day, an annual opportunity to celebrate the county’s history, culture, and traditions, with the date chosen because it is the feast day of St Swithun, patron saint of Winchester Cathedral. This is a useful modern turn: an old saint’s feast has become a county identity marker, not just a church observance or weather joke.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cultureoncall.com/hampshire-day/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cultureoncall.com">[Culture on Call]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cultureoncall.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Culture on Call Hampshire Day: Hampshire Favourites</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Culture on Call Hampshire Day: Hampshire Favourites</span></span></span>

Local councils continue to use the date in that way. Hampshire Association of Local Councils described 15 July as St Swithun’s Day and Hampshire Day, noting the saint’s burial at Winchester Cathedral and the 971 reinterment that removed his remains from the rain and footsteps outside. In modern civic retelling, the legend becomes a way to talk about Hampshire communities, not only Winchester Cathedral.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.hampshirealc.org.uk/news/hampshire-alc-celebrates-hampshire-day-tuesday-15th-july/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: hampshirealc.org.uk">[Hampshire ALC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">hampshirealc.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Hampshire ALCNews &#124; HALC</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Hampshire ALCNews &#124; HALC</span></span></span>

The county flag adds another layer. Its recognised design uses a Saxon crown, partly because Winchester was the capital of Wessex, and a rose-and-crown pattern associated with Hampshire over centuries. Placing Hampshire Day on St Swithun’s Day therefore links three identities at once: the saint, Winchester’s Anglo-Saxon memory, and the modern county.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.flaginstitute.org/wp/uk-flag-registry-news/hampshire-flag-day-recognised/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: flaginstitute.org">[The Flag Institute]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">flaginstitute.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Flag Institute Hampshire Flag &amp; Day recognised &#124; The Flag Institute</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Flag Institute Hampshire Flag &amp; Day recognised &#124; The Flag Institute</span></span></span>

This is how folklore survives without staying frozen. Medieval Swithun was a saint of relics, miracles, and pilgrimage. Early modern and later Swithun became a weather proverb. Modern Hampshire has made him a county-day figure, a heritage symbol, and a light-hearted annual prompt to look at the sky. The belief has changed form, but the date and place still hold it together.

## What the tradition tells us about Hampshire folklore

St Swithun’s weather lore shows that Hampshire folklore is not only about uncanny creatures, haunted lanes, or dramatic apparitions. It can also be a calendar habit: a date people remember, a rhyme they repeat, and a place where history and weather meet. Its power comes from being both local and portable. Winchester supplies the saint and shrine; the English climate supplies the annual suspense.

It also shows how folklore often grows from uncertainty. The historical Swithun is thinly documented. The famous rain story is not securely recorded in the earliest accounts of the translation. The proverb is meteorologically false if read literally. Yet the tradition remains meaningful because it expresses recognisable truths: people notice seasonal patterns, worry about summer weather, remember saints through stories, and turn local places into symbols.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.rmets.org/metmatters/behind-folklore-st-swithins-day-does-rain-today-really-mean-ruined-summer" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: rmets.org">[RMetS]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">rmets.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on rmets.org.</span></span></span>

For readers exploring Hampshire’s legendary landscape, St Swithun is a key starting point because he connects several strands at once. He belongs to Winchester Cathedral and the lost Old Minster; to pilgrimage routes and saintly cults; to civic Hampshire Day; and to the old British habit of making the weather speak in rhyme. Other Hampshire stories may be stranger, darker, or more dramatic, but few are so widely recognised.

The charm of St Swithun’s Day is that it asks everyone to become a folklorist for a moment. On 15 July, people look up, remember a Winchester bishop, half-believe a rhyme they know is unreliable, and place their own patch of sky inside a story more than a thousand years old.
