## Introduction

[Renfrewshire]({{ 'where-renfrewshires-legends-still/' | relative_url }})’s saint legends turn ordinary ground into a sacred landscape: Paisley becomes the town of St Mirin’s shrine, Inchinnan becomes the landing-place and burial landscape of St Conval, and a pair of weathered stones near Renfrew preserve a tradition of miraculous travel and healing water. These stories are not best read as simple biography. They are local memory shaped around churches, relics, rivers, routeways and stones: the kinds of things people could visit, touch, walk past and retell.



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/Renfrewshire_6a148e_saints_healing_stone_8691fa-overview.webp" | relative_url }}" alt="Overview image for Saints &amp; Stones" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

The core tradition is this: St Mirin was remembered as the early holy man behind Paisley’s Christian origin story, while St Conval was remembered as an Irish saint carried to Renfrewshire on a stone that later attracted pilgrims and the sick. Paisley Abbey’s own history links its medieval priory to an older church associated with St Mirin, and Historic Environment Scotland records the Conval legend as part of the early ecclesiastical landscape around Inchinnan and the Cart waters.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.paisleyabbey.org.uk/history/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: paisleyabbey.org.uk">[Paisley Abbey]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">paisleyabbey.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Paisley Abbey History – Paisley Abbey</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Paisley Abbey History – Paisley Abbey</span></span></span>

## St Mirin and Paisley’s sacred origin story

St Mirin, also spelled Mirren in local usage, matters because Paisley’s religious identity is built around him. The modern Abbey stands on a site said to have held an older Celtic church founded by St Mirin in the sixth century; when Walter FitzAlan founded the Cluniac priory around 1163, monks from Much Wenlock established their house on that earlier sacred ground. In 1245 the priory became an abbey, dedicated to St Mary, St James, St Mirin and St Milburga.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.paisleyabbey.org.uk/history/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: paisleyabbey.org.uk">[Paisley Abbey]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">paisleyabbey.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Paisley Abbey History – Paisley Abbey</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Paisley Abbey History – Paisley Abbey</span></span></span>

That sequence gives Paisley a layered origin story. The medieval abbey was a powerful institution with royal and aristocratic connections, but local tradition pushed the sacred beginning further back, to an Irish missionary saint and a pre-Norman church. For folklore readers, that matters because it shows how a saint can become a town’s explanation of itself. Paisley is not only a weaving town, an abbey town or a later industrial centre; in its older sacred story, it is a place chosen, settled and spiritually marked by Mirin.

The most vivid part of the Mirin story preserved in local retelling is his journey from Ireland and his arrival where Paisley now stands. Paisley.is, the area’s visitor and place-story platform, tells the tradition that Mirin was given a small field near the river by a local chieftain; this plot became known as St Mirin’s Croft, and after his death his shrine became a centre of pilgrimage. The same account notes that his image was engraved on the seal of Paisley Abbey, that a chapel in the Abbey is dedicated to him, and that St Mirin’s Cathedral carries his name today.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://paisley.is/stories-from-renfrewshire/monks-missionaries-and-pilgrims/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: paisley.is">[Paisley]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">paisley.is</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Monks. missionaries and pilgrims</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Monks. missionaries and pilgrims - Paisley.is...</span></span></span>

There is a clear difference between the likely historical core and the legendary dressing. A missionary figure associated with early Christianity in the Paisley area is plausible within the broader world of Irish and western Scottish Christianity. The more dramatic miracle tales belong to hagiography: saintly story-writing designed to show holiness, authority and divine favour. One local version says Mirin was refused entry by an Irish king and prayed that the king would feel his wife’s labour pains until Mirin was granted permission to preach. That is not everyday biography; it is a moral miracle story about a saint overcoming worldly power.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://paisley.is/stories-from-renfrewshire/monks-missionaries-and-pilgrims/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: paisley.is">[Paisley]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">paisley.is</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Monks. missionaries and pilgrims</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Monks. missionaries and pilgrims - Paisley.is...</span></span></span>

## Why St Mirin still belongs to Paisley

St Mirin’s power in Renfrewshire folklore comes less from one spectacular miracle than from repetition across the townscape. His name attaches to the Abbey, the Cathedral, the burn, the street, the football club and the wider Catholic Diocese of Paisley. That spread is not accidental. It shows how a medieval saint can move from shrine cult into civic identity.

The Abbey connection is especially important. Paisley Abbey’s official history says the Cluniac priory was founded on the site of the older church associated with St Mirin, and that the Abbey was dedicated in part to him as the local saint who first brought Christianity to this part of Scotland.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.paisleyabbey.org.uk/history/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: paisleyabbey.org.uk">[Paisley Abbey]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">paisleyabbey.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Paisley Abbey History – Paisley Abbey</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Paisley Abbey History – Paisley Abbey</span></span></span> The Abbey therefore acts as a bridge between saint legend and built heritage: it gives the Mirin story a visible centre, even if the earliest evidence is later, filtered and devotional.

Local retelling also keeps Mirin from becoming a remote church figure. Paisley.is points to St Mirren FC, St Mirin Burn and St Mirren Street as modern survivals of the name in everyday Paisley.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://paisley.is/stories-from-renfrewshire/monks-missionaries-and-pilgrims/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: paisley.is">[Paisley]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">paisley.is</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Monks. missionaries and pilgrims</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Monks. missionaries and pilgrims - Paisley.is...</span></span></span> That is one reason he matters within Renfrewshire’s folklore map. He is not simply a saint in a book; he is a name embedded in the way people move through and talk about the town.

This is also where the page’s scope needs a boundary. St Mirin is part of wider Scottish and Irish Christian tradition, and Paisley Abbey has a much larger royal and architectural history, including the Stewarts. But for Renfrewshire’s saint folklore, the key point is narrower: Mirin gives Paisley a sacred origin story, a remembered shrine and a pattern of place-names that keep the medieval cult visible in modern life.

## St Conval’s stone voyage from Ireland

If St Mirin gives Paisley a shrine-centred origin story, St Conval gives Renfrew and Inchinnan one of Renfrewshire’s strangest saint legends: the holy man who crossed the sea on a stone. The tradition says that Conval, an Irish saint, was standing or resting on a stone by the Irish Sea when it broke free, carried him over the water, came up the Firth of Clyde and landed near Renfrew. From there he went on to found his church at Inchinnan, close to the meeting of the White Cart and Black Cart waters. Historic Environment Scotland records this as a later legend written down in the sixteenth-century Aberdeen Breviary, where Conval is made the son of an Irish prince and a student of St Mungo or Kentigern.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://portal.historicenvironment.scot/apex/f?p=1505%3A300%3A%3A%3A%3A%3AVIEWTYPE%2CVIEWREF%3Adesignation%2CSM2792" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: portal.historicenvironment.scot">[portal.historicenvironment.scot]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">portal.historicenvironment.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Inchinnan, site of All Hallows Church (SM2792)...</span></span></span>

The stone itself is remembered as St Conval’s Chariot. That name matters. It turns a boulder into a vehicle, and a landscape object into evidence of a sacred journey. In folklore terms, the story solves several things at once: it explains why this saint belongs in Renfrewshire, why a stone by the Cart waters was special, and why Inchinnan’s church could claim authority from a saintly founder.

The physical setting strengthens the tale. Inchinnan sits in a watery, low-lying landscape where routes, crossings and river edges matter. The University of Glasgow’s Saints in Scottish Place-Names project places St Conval’s Church at Inchinnan, in Renfrewshire, overlooking the confluence of the White Cart Water and Black Cart Water, and notes that early medieval carved stones survive in the old graveyard.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://saintsplaces.gla.ac.uk/place.php?id=1331828410" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: saintsplaces.gla.ac.uk">[saintsplaces.gla.ac.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">saintsplaces.gla.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Saints in Scottish Place-Names</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">St Conval&#x27;s Church, Inchinnan...</span></span></span> This is not a saint story floating in the abstract. It belongs to a specific river landscape of crossing-points, church ground and carved stone.

Historic Environment Scotland’s scheduled monument record for All Hallows at Inchinnan adds the archaeological frame. It describes the site as slightly raised ground within a bend of two adjoining rivers, with the graveyard serving the people of Inchinnan for at least 600 years and possibly containing burials dating from around AD 600. It also notes that nearby monuments include the Argyll Stone and St Conval’s Chariot, although their exact relationship to the church site has not been fully established.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://portal.historicenvironment.scot/apex/f?p=1505%3A300%3A%3A%3A%3A%3AVIEWTYPE%2CVIEWREF%3Adesignation%2CSM2792" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: portal.historicenvironment.scot">[portal.historicenvironment.scot]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">portal.historicenvironment.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Inchinnan, site of All Hallows Church (SM2792)...</span></span></span>

## Healing water, relic stones and local belief

The healing part of the Conval tradition is beautifully concrete. Travellers and sick people were said to visit the stone, drink rainwater gathered in its hollow, and believe that it had curative power. Renfrewshire Council’s account of the Argyll Stones states that the stone which carried St Conval from Ireland was later believed to have become the base of a church cross, and that pilgrims drank the rainwater collected in its hollow for healing.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www1.renfrewshire.gov.uk/media/2706/Argyll-Stones/pdf/ArygllStones.pdf?m=1467367028317" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: www1.renfrewshire.gov.uk">[Renfrewshire Council]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">www1.renfrewshire.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Renfrewshire Council</span></span></span> Historic Environment Scotland similarly records that the stone, known in Latin tradition as Currus Sancti Convalli, remained on the bank of the Cart and was said to have miraculous curative properties.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://portal.historicenvironment.scot/apex/f?p=1505%3A300%3A%3A%3A%3A%3AVIEWTYPE%2CVIEWREF%3Adesignation%2CSM2792" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: portal.historicenvironment.scot">[portal.historicenvironment.scot]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">portal.historicenvironment.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Inchinnan, site of All Hallows Church (SM2792)...</span></span></span>

That belief sits within a much wider British and Irish pattern of holy wells, rainwater hollows and saintly relics, but its Renfrewshire expression is very local. The power is not simply in water, and not simply in stone. It is in the combination: a saint’s journey, a boulder understood as his vehicle, a hollow that holds water, and a church landscape that gives the practice religious authority.

The pair of stones now often called the Argyll Stones also show how different layers of memory can gather around the same place. The National Record of the Historic Environment entry locates the stones in Renfrew parish, within the former county of Renfrewshire, at OS grid reference NS 49471 67830.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.trove.scot/place/43064" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: trove.scot">[Trove Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">trove.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">landRenfrew, &#x27;argyll Stone&#x27; &amp; &#x27;st... &#124; Place &#124; trove.scot...</span></span></span> Historic Environment Scotland distinguishes them: the Argyll Stone is described as the remains of a sandstone cross base, reputedly part of a memorial to St Conval, while St Conval’s Chariot is an erratic granite boulder said to be the stone on which the saint floated from Ireland.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://portal.historicenvironment.scot/apex/f?p=1505%3A300%3A%3A%3A%3A%3AVIEWTYPE%2CVIEWREF%3Adesignation%2CSM2792" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: portal.historicenvironment.scot">[portal.historicenvironment.scot]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">portal.historicenvironment.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Inchinnan, site of All Hallows Church (SM2792)...</span></span></span>

The “Argyll” part of the name belongs to a later, non-saintly memory. Renfrewshire Council’s account says the second stone became known as the Argyll Stone because Archibald Campbell, 9th Earl of Argyll, was said to have paused there in 1685 while fleeing after his failed rising against the king; he was then captured, taken to Glasgow and Edinburgh, and executed at the Mercat Cross on 30 June 1685.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www1.renfrewshire.gov.uk/media/2706/Argyll-Stones/pdf/ArygllStones.pdf?m=1467367028317" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: www1.renfrewshire.gov.uk">[Renfrewshire Council]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">www1.renfrewshire.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Renfrewshire Council</span></span></span> That later political legend does not replace the Conval story. Instead, it shows how a prominent stone can keep collecting meanings: saintly, healing, commemorative and historical.

## How well-attested is the tradition?

The St Conval material is better anchored than many local miracle stories because it is tied to named medieval and archaeological records, but that does not make every detail straightforward history. The University of Glasgow’s place-name project notes the Aberdeen Breviary and Aberdeen Calendar entries for St Conval at Inchinnan, and confirms the old parish kirk’s dedication to St Conval. It also records surviving early medieval carved stones in the old graveyard.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://saintsplaces.gla.ac.uk/place.php?id=1331828410" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: saintsplaces.gla.ac.uk">[saintsplaces.gla.ac.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">saintsplaces.gla.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Saints in Scottish Place-Names</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">St Conval&#x27;s Church, Inchinnan...</span></span></span> Historic Environment Scotland likewise treats Inchinnan as a site with strong potential to illuminate the early ecclesiastical landscape of Strathgryffe and south-west Scotland.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://portal.historicenvironment.scot/apex/f?p=1505%3A300%3A%3A%3A%3A%3AVIEWTYPE%2CVIEWREF%3Adesignation%2CSM2792" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: portal.historicenvironment.scot">[portal.historicenvironment.scot]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">portal.historicenvironment.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Inchinnan, site of All Hallows Church (SM2792)...</span></span></span>

The uncertainty lies in the interpretation. A stone with a hollow can be an archaeological object, a cross-base, a relocated monument, a healing focus and a legendary “chariot” all at once. Historic Environment Scotland notes that the Argyll Stone and St Conval’s Chariot were both thought to have been moved to their current location before 1836, and that their exact relationship with the church site has not yet been established, though there is a high probability of a link.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://portal.historicenvironment.scot/apex/f?p=1505%3A300%3A%3A%3A%3A%3AVIEWTYPE%2CVIEWREF%3Adesignation%2CSM2792" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: portal.historicenvironment.scot">[portal.historicenvironment.scot]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">portal.historicenvironment.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Inchinnan, site of All Hallows Church (SM2792)...</span></span></span> That is a useful caution: the tradition is strong, but the present placement of the stones is not necessarily their earliest sacred position.

There is also a deeper medieval politics behind the saint’s story. Place-name scholar Thomas Clancy, writing for the University of Glasgow’s Place-Names of the Coalfield Communities project, points out that Inchinnan’s name appears to preserve another saint, Finnian, yet in the later Middle Ages it was Conval whose cult was celebrated there. He argues that Conval’s link to St Kentigern or Mungo in the Aberdeen Breviary may reflect Glasgow’s attempt to assert ecclesiastical authority over Inchinnan: if Conval was Kentigern’s disciple, then a Conval church could be imagined as subordinate to Glasgow’s great saint.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ayr-placenames.glasgow.ac.uk/cumnock-and-glasgow-conval-and-kentigern-saints-and-their-authority/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ayr-placenames.glasgow.ac.uk">[Ayr Place-Names]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ayr-placenames.glasgow.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on glasgow.ac.uk.</span></span></span>

That does not make the folklore “fake”. It makes it more interesting. Saint legends were often used to explain land, authority, church rights and local status. St Conval’s stone voyage is therefore both a miracle tale and a claim about belonging: this saint, this church, this river crossing, this healing stone and this part of Renfrewshire all belong in one sacred story.

## What the stones reveal about Renfrewshire’s folklore

The strongest thing about Renfrewshire’s saint traditions is their attachment to reachable places. St Mirin’s memory gathers around Paisley Abbey, St Mirin’s Croft, the Cathedral and Paisley’s civic naming. St Conval’s memory gathers around Inchinnan, the Cart waters, the old church site, carved stones, wells and the stone called his Chariot. These are not distant mythic landscapes. They are town-centre, riverside and parish landscapes.

That makes the traditions useful for understanding Renfrewshire as a historic county. The older county stretches beyond the modern Renfrewshire Council area, and its folklore follows older patterns of parish, river, pilgrimage and church dedication rather than today’s administrative boundaries. Wikishire describes Renfrewshire as lying along the River Clyde and Firth of Clyde, with towns shaped by the Clyde corridor, while the Association of British Counties summarises the historic county as stretching along the south bank of the Clyde from the southern Glasgow suburbs to the coast opposite Cowal.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://wikishire.co.uk/wiki/Renfrewshire" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wikishire.co.uk">[Wikishire]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wikishire.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wikishire.co.uk.</span></span></span>

The saints also show a distinctive Renfrewshire mechanism: sacred memory is made durable by objects. A shrine keeps St Mirin present in Paisley. A cross-base, boulder and water-filled hollow keep St Conval present near Renfrew and Inchinnan. A church dedication preserves a cult. A place-name complicates the story by hinting that older dedications may have been overwritten. A visitor today may not accept the miracle literally, but the landscape still works as folklore because the story explains why these places feel charged.

## How the tradition is retold today

Today, these saint legends survive through churches, heritage records, local-history groups, tourism writing and walking routes. Paisley Abbey presents St Mirin as the local saint behind the older church on which the medieval Abbey was founded. Paisley.is retells the Mirin story for visitors and links it to the Abbey, the Cathedral, St Mirren FC and modern pilgrimage routes through Renfrewshire.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.paisleyabbey.org.uk/history/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: paisleyabbey.org.uk">[Paisley Abbey]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">paisleyabbey.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Paisley Abbey History – Paisley Abbey</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Paisley Abbey History – Paisley Abbey</span></span></span>

Inchinnan’s Conval story is also still visible in community heritage. The Inchinnan Historical Interest Group’s material on the stained-glass depiction of St Conval and St Mungo describes Conval riding from Ireland over the sea on a stone and landing near the All Hallows Church site, where he established his church.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://myinchinnan.org.uk/stained-glass/saint-conval-and-saint-mungo/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: myinchinnan.org.uk">[myinchinnan.org.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">myinchinnan.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Saint Conval &amp; Saint Mungo</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Saint Conval &amp; Saint Mungo</span></span></span> The Saints in Scottish Place-Names database, Historic Environment Scotland and the National Record of the Historic Environment preserve the more technical record: church dedication, grid references, carved stones, archaeological potential and the distinction between the Argyll Stone and St Conval’s Chariot.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://portal.historicenvironment.scot/apex/f?p=1505%3A300%3A%3A%3A%3A%3AVIEWTYPE%2CVIEWREF%3Adesignation%2CSM2792" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: portal.historicenvironment.scot">[historicenvironment.scot]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">portal.historicenvironment.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Inchinnan, site of All Hallows Church (SM2792)...</span></span></span>

For a modern reader, the appeal lies in the meeting of the tangible and the marvellous. Paisley and Inchinnan do not ask the visitor to imagine a vanished fairy palace or an untraceable ghost road. They offer an abbey, a cathedral, a church site, old carved stones, a boulder with a healing-water tradition and a river landscape where saints are said to have arrived, preached, died or been remembered. Renfrewshire’s sacred folklore is therefore not just about saints. It is about how communities use stones, shrines and water to make history feel touchable.

    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-domain-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = domainLabel(marketId);
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-option]')).forEach(function (button) {
      button.setAttribute('aria-selected', button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-option') === marketId ? 'true' : 'false');
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      var active = button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button') === marketId;
      button.setAttribute('aria-pressed', active ? 'true' : 'false');
    });
    section.setAttribute('data-ebay-selected-market', marketId);
    if (persist) {
      try { if (window.localStorage) localStorage.setItem('phoenix-ebay-market', marketId); } catch (err) {}
    }
    closeMenu(section);
  }
  sections.forEach(function (section) {
    if (section.getAttribute('data-ebay-localized-init') === '1') return;
    section.setAttribute('data-ebay-localized-init', '1');
    var available = availableMarkets(section);
    var marketId = defaultMarket;
    if (window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation && window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation.inferMarket) {
      marketId = window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation.inferMarket(available, {
        storageKey: 'phoenix-ebay-market',
        defaultMarket: defaultMarket
      });
    } else if (available.indexOf(defaultMarket)< 0) {
      marketId = available[0] || defaultMarket;
    }
    var select = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-select]');
    if (select) {
      select.addEventListener('change', function () {
        applyMarket(section, select.value, true);
      });
    }
    var trigger = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-trigger]');
    var menu = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-menu]');
    if (trigger && menu) {
      trigger.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
        event.stopPropagation();
        var expanded = trigger.getAttribute('aria-expanded') === 'true';
        menu.hidden = expanded;
        trigger.setAttribute('aria-expanded', expanded ? 'false' : 'true');
        var picker = trigger.closest('.fr-ebay-market-picker');
        if (picker) picker.classList.toggle('fr-ebay-market-picker--open', !expanded);
      });
      Array.prototype.slice.call(menu.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-option]')).forEach(function (button) {
        button.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
          event.stopPropagation();
          applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-option'), true);
        });
      });
      document.addEventListener('click', function () { closeMenu(section); });
    }
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      button.addEventListener('click', function () {
        applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button'), true);
      });
    });
    applyMarket(section, marketId, false);
  });
