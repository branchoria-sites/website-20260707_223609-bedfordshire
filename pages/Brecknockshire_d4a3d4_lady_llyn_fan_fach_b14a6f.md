## Introduction

The Lady of Llyn y Fan Fach is one of the great Welsh lake-fairy legends, but it is not quite “owned” by [Brecknockshire]({{ 'why-do-brecknockshires-hills-feel/' | relative_url }}). The lake itself lies on the Carmarthenshire side of the Black Mountain, near Myddfai, while the high ridge and neighbouring walking landscape pull the story into the imaginative borderland of Brecknockshire and the wider Bannau Brycheiniog. That is why the tale matters here: it shows how folklore often follows mountains, cattle routes, healing reputations and tourist paths rather than neat county lines.



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/Brecknockshire_d4a3d4_lady_llyn_fan_fach_b14a6f-overview.webp" | relative_url }}" alt="Overview image for Lake Lady" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

In its best-known form, a mysterious woman rises from Llyn y Fan Fach, marries a local farmer under a strict taboo, leaves him when the taboo is broken, and returns only to teach their sons the arts of healing. Those sons become linked with the Physicians of Myddfai, a celebrated medical tradition preserved in medieval and later Welsh manuscripts. The result is a border legend with two lives: a fairy-bride story rooted in place, and a foundation myth explaining why one upland community became famous for medicine.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://isfnr.org/files/Printed.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: isfnr.org">[isfnr.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">isfnr.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Reverend John Williams, &#x27;Ab Ithel&#x27;, in his introduction to the book The Physicians of...Read more...</span></span></span>

## The lake is Carmarthenshire, but the horizon is Brecknockshire

Llyn y Fan Fach sits beneath the Carmarthen Fans, on the northern edge of the Black Mountain in what is historically Carmarthenshire. Modern visitors usually meet it through the language of Bannau Brycheiniog, the national park landscape formerly better known in English as the Brecon Beacons, which can blur the old county distinction. Discover Carmarthenshire’s walking guide places the lake under the ridgeline of Bannau Sir Gaer and presents it as one of the county’s dramatic mountain walks, while modern walking coverage treats it as part of the western Bannau experience.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.discovercarmarthenshire.com/explore/walking-in-carmarthenshire/carmarthen-fans-short-walk/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: discovercarmarthenshire.com">[Discover Carmarthenshire]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">discovercarmarthenshire.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Discover CarmarthenshireCarmarthen FansLlyn y Fan Fach sits beneath the precipitous ridgeline of Bannau Sir Gaer. For a Brecknockshire folklore page, the crucial point is not to annex the story, but to understand why it travels east. The Black Mountain range straddles Carmarthenshire and Brecknockshire, and Fan Brycheiniog, whose name points back to Brecknock, is part of the same mountain world that walkers associate with Llyn y Fan Fach and Llyn y Fan Fawr. The Association of British Counties describes the Black Mountain as straddling into Brecknockshire from Carmarthenshire, with Fan Brycheiniog as the county’s high point.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://abcounties.com/articles/a-portrait-of-brecknockshire/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: abcounties.com">[Association of British Counties]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">abcounties.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The highest point is Fan Brycheiniog (2,631 ft).Read more</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Association of British CountiesA portrait of Brecknockshire1 Mar 2022 — In the south-west, the Black Mountain range straddles into Breckn...</span></span></span>

This makes the Lady of the Lake a useful test case for county folklore. On a legal or historic-county map, the tale belongs most directly to Carmarthenshire and Myddfai. On the ground, it belongs to a ridge-and-lake landscape that readers often approach through Brecknockshire’s western mountain edge. The old story therefore works as a border legend: not a possession to be claimed, but a tradition that helps explain how the Bannau are imagined as a connected supernatural landscape.

## The marriage taboo and the vanishing cattle

The heart of the story is simple, memorable and unsettling. A young man from the Myddfai area sees a beautiful woman at or upon Llyn y Fan Fach. She agrees to marry him, but only on a condition: if he strikes or wrongs her three times, she will return to the lake. For a while they live together, raise children, and prosper with a supernatural dowry of cattle. Then the taboo is broken. In the familiar nineteenth-century version, the “blows” are not brutal attacks in the usual sense, but ambiguous touches or rebukes at socially charged moments, including a wedding and a funeral. Once the third offence occurs, she leaves for the lake and takes her cattle with her.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/download/b2475769x/b2475769x.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on archive.org.</span></span></span>

That vanishing herd is one reason the story feels so firmly local. The legend does not just say “a fairy went away”; it sends animals, ploughing, household wealth and farm order back into the mountain. In several retellings, the cattle are not decorative magical props but proof of the household’s prosperity and of what is lost when a human bargain with the otherworld collapses. The Fforest Fawr Geopark retelling stresses that the tale centres not only on the lake but also on identifiable farmed places below it, anchoring the supernatural drama in the everyday upland economy.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.fforestfawrgeopark.org.uk/understanding/myths-and-legends/the-lady-of-the-lake/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: fforestfawrgeopark.org.uk">[Fforest Fawr Geopark]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">fforestfawrgeopark.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on fforestfawrgeopark.org.uk.</span></span></span>

For readers coming from Brecknockshire, this is where the tale speaks most strongly to the wider county’s folklore. Brecknockshire has many stories in which water, hills, roads and lonely farms are not background scenery but active parts of the narrative. The Lady’s cattle crossing back towards the lake make the mountain border feel porous: ordinary domestic life and the hidden world briefly overlap, then separate again.

The taboo also gives the tale its moral tension. It is not simply a romance with a sad ending. It asks whether humans can live properly with a gift from outside their own world. The answer, in this tradition, is only temporarily. Wealth, marriage and knowledge may come from the lake, but they remain conditional.

## Myddfai healers and fairy medicine

The Lady’s most important legacy is not the marriage itself, but the sons she leaves behind. The Physicians of Myddfai were remembered as a line of healers associated with Myddfai in Carmarthenshire. The legend explains their skill by making them the children of the lake woman, who later returns to teach them healing knowledge. This is what turns a fairy-bride story into a medical origin legend.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://isfnr.org/files/Printed.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: isfnr.org">[Folk Narrative Research Society]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">isfnr.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Reverend John Williams, &#x27;Ab Ithel&#x27;, in his introduction to the book The Physicians of...Read more...</span></span></span>

There is a real manuscript tradition behind the Physicians, although it must be handled carefully. The National Library of Wales notes that the Red Book of Hergest, compiled between 1382 and 1410, contains a medicinal tract by the Physicians of Myddfai. It also records later manuscript transmission, including an eighteenth-century transcript of “Meddygon Myddfai” from the Red Book of Hergest.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.library.wales/about-nlw/press-and-news/press-releases/2014-press-releases/final-three-weeks-for-the-four-books" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: library.wales">[Library Wales]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">library.wales</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Final three weeks for the Four Books&#x27; The Red Book of Hergest contains treasured texts such as the Four Branches of the Mabinogi, the Dre...</span></span></span>

The famous printed edition, *The Physicians of Myddvai: Meddygon Myddfai*, appeared in 1861. The Internet Archive catalogue describes it as presenting the medical practice of Rhiwallon and his sons of Myddfai, physicians to Rhys Gryg, lord of Dinefwr and Ystrad Tywi, around the middle of the thirteenth century. The National Library of Wales also holds proof sheets and drafts connected with that 1861 publication.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/details/b2475769x" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on archive.org.</span></span></span>

This distinction matters. The medical texts are medieval and manuscript-based; the fully developed lake-lady origin story, as usually told today, is much more visible in later antiquarian and oral-tradition collection. R. Gwyndaf’s study of the legend notes that it was first recorded in print in 1821 in *The Cambro-Briton*, while the more detailed and best-known version was printed in 1861 by the Reverend John Williams, “Ab Ithel”, in his introduction to *The Physicians of Myddvai*.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://isfnr.org/files/Printed.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: isfnr.org">[Folk Narrative Research Society]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">isfnr.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Reverend John Williams, &#x27;Ab Ithel&#x27;, in his introduction to the book The Physicians of...Read more...</span></span></span>

So the safest reading is this: Myddfai’s medical reputation and manuscript remedies are historically attested, while the Lady of Llyn y Fan Fach provides a legendary explanation for that reputation. The legend does not need to be treated as literal history to be valuable. It tells us how a community imagined healing knowledge: as something partly learned, partly inherited, and partly gifted from a mysterious landscape.

## How old is the tradition?

The Lady of Llyn y Fan Fach feels ancient, and parts of its pattern certainly belong to a very old family of stories. But the evidence for this specific local form is more layered than a simple “medieval legend” label suggests.

The broader motif is the fairy bride or otherworld wife: a woman from a supernatural realm marries a mortal man, lives with him under a condition, and leaves when the condition is broken. Modern scholarship places the Llyn y Fan Fach tale within that wider tradition. A Cardiff University article discussing *The Doctor of Myddfai* summarises the folktale as part of the supernatural legend tradition in which an otherworld woman marries a mortal and later returns to her own world, leaving husband and children behind; it also notes Juliette Wood’s observation that there are around three dozen Welsh variants of this fairy-bride legend.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://orca.cardiff.ac.uk/id/eprint/147819/10/the-rain-soaked-sky-is-leaden-welsh-identity-and-dystopian-impulses-in-the-doctor-of-myddfai.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: orca.cardiff.ac.uk">[ORCA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">orca.cardiff.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ORCAWelsh identity and dystopian Impulses in The Doctor of Myddfai</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ORCAWelsh identity and dystopian Impulses in The Doctor of Myddfai</span></span></span>

The local Myddfai-Llyn y Fan Fach connection is best documented in the nineteenth century. R. Gwyndaf identifies the first printed record as the 1821 *Cambro-Briton* version and the best-known detailed form as the 1861 Ab Ithel edition. Another accessible survey of the tradition points to Richard Fenton’s 1809 travel journal as an early written account collected during his tour through Carmarthenshire, where he recorded a story from an old woman about the first of the physicians and a woman’s voice beneath the lake.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://isfnr.org/files/Printed.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: isfnr.org">[Folk Narrative Research Society]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">isfnr.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Reverend John Williams, &#x27;Ab Ithel&#x27;, in his introduction to the book The Physicians of...Read more...</span></span></span>

That does not mean the story was invented in 1821. Nineteenth-century print often captured older oral material only after it had circulated for generations. But it does mean that a responsible account should separate three things:

* **The medieval medical tradition:** remedies and medical material associated with the Physicians of Myddfai survive in manuscript contexts, including the Red Book of Hergest.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.library.wales/about-nlw/press-and-news/press-releases/2014-press-releases/final-three-weeks-for-the-four-books" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: library.wales">[Library Wales]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">library.wales</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Final three weeks for the Four Books&#x27; The Red Book of Hergest contains treasured texts such as the Four Branches of the Mabinogi, the Dre...</span></span></span>
* **The local origin legend in print:** the lake-lady story appears in print in the early nineteenth century and is elaborated in the 1861 edition.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://isfnr.org/files/Printed.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: isfnr.org">[Folk Narrative Research Society]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">isfnr.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Reverend John Williams, &#x27;Ab Ithel&#x27;, in his introduction to the book The Physicians of...Read more...</span></span></span>
* **The older story pattern:** the fairy-bride structure belongs to a wider Welsh and European legendary pattern, but that does not automatically prove every local detail is medieval.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://orca.cardiff.ac.uk/id/eprint/147819/10/the-rain-soaked-sky-is-leaden-welsh-identity-and-dystopian-impulses-in-the-doctor-of-myddfai.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: orca.cardiff.ac.uk">[ORCA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">orca.cardiff.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ORCAWelsh identity and dystopian Impulses in The Doctor of Myddfai</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ORCAWelsh identity and dystopian Impulses in The Doctor of Myddfai</span></span></span>

This layered evidence is part of the story’s interest. The Lady of Llyn y Fan Fach is not a fragile modern invention with no roots, nor a neatly documented medieval tale in every detail. It is a living local legend built from oral testimony, antiquarian collection, manuscript medicine and landscape memory.

## Why border legends travel across counties

County borders are useful for organising folklore, but they can mislead if treated as walls. Llyn y Fan Fach shows why. The lake lies in Carmarthenshire, Myddfai gives the tale its village and medical identity, Dinefwr and Ystrad Tywi connect it to the older lordship world of south-west Wales, and the mountain ridge draws it towards Brecknockshire and the Bannau. The story is local, but its localness is not flat; it is made from routes, slopes, views and neighbouring communities.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/details/b2475769x" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on archive.org.</span></span></span>

The modern national park frame strengthens that cross-border movement. Visitors looking for “Brecon Beacons folklore” or “Bannau Brycheiniog legends” often meet Llyn y Fan Fach alongside Pen y Fan, Fan Brycheiniog, Llyn y Fan Fawr, Waterfall Country and other landscapes that belong to different historic counties. The Guardian’s 2024 travel coverage, for example, presents Llyn y Fan Fach as a hidden lake in the wild west of Bannau Brycheiniog and retells the Lady of the Lake legend for a broad national audience.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.theguardian.com/travel/article/2024/jun/08/five-uk-less-visited-national-parks" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theguardian.com">[The Guardian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theguardian.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on theguardian.com.</span></span></span>

For Brecknockshire, this travelling quality is not a weakness. It helps explain the county’s western edge. Brecknockshire folklore is often about thresholds: mountains between valleys, roads between markets, waters that divide and connect, and old cultural zones where Welsh literary memory, farming practice and later tourism overlap. The Lady of Llyn y Fan Fach belongs just beyond the county, but she shapes the atmosphere of the Black Mountain that Brecknockshire shares.

A good county-based reading therefore avoids two mistakes. It should not pretend the lake is in Brecknockshire. It should also not exclude the story simply because a historic boundary puts the water on the Carmarthenshire side. The tale matters to Brecknockshire because people experience the mountain as a joined landscape, not as a set of administrative compartments.

## The Lady today: walking route, heritage story and popular retelling

Today, many people encounter the Lady of Llyn y Fan Fach before or after a walk. Tourism and walking guides often introduce the lake through its scenery and then add the legend as part of the place’s appeal. Discover Carmarthenshire gives practical routes for Llyn y Fan Fach and the Carmarthen Fans, noting the lake’s dramatic glacial setting and the folklore of the Lady of the Lake. Ordnance Survey’s GetOutside route coverage likewise frames Llyn y Fan Fach and Llyn y Fan Fawr as a major Bannau Brycheiniog walk.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.discovercarmarthenshire.com/explore/walking-in-carmarthenshire/carmarthen-fans-short-walk/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: discovercarmarthenshire.com">[Discover Carmarthenshire]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">discovercarmarthenshire.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Discover CarmarthenshireCarmarthen FansLlyn y Fan Fach sits beneath the precipitous ridgeline of Bannau Sir Gaer. This modern presentation changes how the legend works. In the older local frame, the tale explains a medical lineage, a farm landscape and the strange authority of a lake. In the visitor frame, it gives emotional shape to a mountain walk: the water is no longer just a scenic destination but a place where a story can be imagined. That can be powerful, but it can also flatten the tradition if the Physicians of Myddfai are reduced to a passing sentence.

The strongest modern retellings keep all three elements together: the lake, the broken marriage taboo, and the healing knowledge. Fforest Fawr Geopark’s version is useful because it explicitly connects the story to Llyn y Fan Fach and to identifiable places below the mountain, while the National Botanic Garden of Wales uses the legend as a route into the herbal and medical associations of Myddfai.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.fforestfawrgeopark.org.uk/understanding/myths-and-legends/the-lady-of-the-lake/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: fforestfawrgeopark.org.uk">[Fforest Fawr Geopark]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">fforestfawrgeopark.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on fforestfawrgeopark.org.uk.</span></span></span>

The tale has also entered children’s literature, folklore blogs, local interpretation, walking journalism and modern Welsh cultural discussion. That wide retelling is not a sign that the story has become less authentic; it is how major folklore survives. What changes is the emphasis. A nineteenth-century antiquarian might value the tale for its link to manuscripts and Welsh antiquity. A walker may value it for the atmosphere it gives the lake. A Brecknockshire reader may value it for what it reveals about border country: the sense that the most powerful stories are sometimes just over the line, but still part of the view.

## Who owns the Lady of the Lake?

The best answer is: Carmarthenshire owns the location, Myddfai owns the medical lineage, and the wider Black Mountain landscape shares the legend’s afterlife. Brecknockshire should not claim the Lady of Llyn y Fan Fach as if the lake sat within its historic boundary. But Brecknockshire cannot be cleanly separated from the story either, because Fan Brycheiniog, the western Bannau, and the shared upland walking culture bring the legend into the county’s imaginative neighbourhood.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://abcounties.com/articles/a-portrait-of-brecknockshire/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: abcounties.com">[Association of British Counties]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">abcounties.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The highest point is Fan Brycheiniog (2,631 ft).Read more</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Association of British CountiesA portrait of Brecknockshire1 Mar 2022 — In the south-west, the Black Mountain range straddles into Breckn...</span></span></span>

That makes the Lady a model for reading Welsh county folklore fairly. Some stories sit neatly inside a parish or county. Others belong to borderlands, where the map says one thing and the lived landscape says something more fluid. Llyn y Fan Fach is one of those cases. Its fairy woman rises from Carmarthenshire water, but the tale’s shadow falls across the Brecknockshire mountains.

For the folklore reader, that border tension is the point. The Lady of Llyn y Fan Fach is not merely a romantic lake story. It is a tradition about conditions, loss, healing, inheritance and place. It reminds us that county folklore is not just a catalogue of tales inside boundaries, but a way of noticing how stories move through the landscapes people actually walk, farm, remember and retell.
