## Introduction

Kinross-shire’s folklore is concentrated, like the county itself, around a small number of powerful places: [Loch Leven]({{ 'loch-leven/' | relative_url }}), St Serf’s Inch, [Scotlandwell]({{ 'scotlandwell/' | relative_url }}), Bishop Hill, Crook of Devon, and the old parish landscapes of Portmoak, Orwell, Cleish, Kinross and Fossoway. It is not a county with an enormous printed folklore shelf, but it has unusually sharp local traditions: a healing well associated with Robert the Bruce, an island monastery tied to saintly memory and early Scottish writing, a witch-trial record from 1662, a stone witch on Bishop Hill, and a lochscape where history, legend and tourism still overlap. Kinross-shire is best read as a small historic county where folklore clings to landmarks rather than spreading into a large catalogue of tales.

For this project, Kinross-shire means the historic county, not simply the modern council area. The old county lies between Perthshire and Fife, centred on Loch Leven; modern local government places it within Perth and Kinross, but the older county identity remains useful because many of the stories attach to parishes, old roads, loch islands and named hills rather than present-day administrative lines. Wikishire describes the county as lying between Perthshire and Fife on Loch Leven, while Gazetteer for Scotland places Loch Leven at the heart of the county and notes the castle, islands and surrounding lowland landscape that dominate local identity.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://wikishire.co.uk/wiki/Kinross-shire" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wikishire.co.uk">[Wikishire]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wikishire.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Kinross shire</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Kinross shire</span></span></span>

## Why Kinross-shire folklore feels different

Kinross-shire’s legendary landscape is small, enclosed and easy to visualise. Loch Leven is not a distant scenic backdrop; it is the county’s centre of gravity. Castle Island, St Serf’s Inch, the parish of Portmoak, the slopes of Bishop Hill and the villages around the loch form a ring of memory in which saints, [witches]({{ 'witches-266472/' | relative_url }}), healing waters, political imprisonment and place-name lore all sit close together. That compactness matters. In a larger county, a holy well, a witch trial and a stone witch might feel like separate subjects. In Kinross-shire they are part of the same walkable and boat-linked landscape.

The historic-county frame also helps explain why some “Kinross-shire” traditions now appear under Perth and Kinross in heritage databases, visitor guides and council material. Canmore, Scotland’s national record of the historic environment, lists St Serf’s Priory Church on Loch Leven under the modern local authority of Perth and Kinross but also records its former county as Kinross-shire.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://canmore.org.uk/site/27872/loch-leven-st-serfs-island-st-serfs-priory-church?display=image" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: canmore.org.uk">[Canmore]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">canmore.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on canmore.org.uk.</span></span></span> That is the pattern readers will meet again and again: the folklore belongs to an older county geography, while modern sources often file it under a wider administrative area.

What survives most strongly is not a single grand myth-cycle, but a cluster of traditions with different levels of evidence:

That mixture is part of the county’s appeal. Kinross-shire folklore is not always “ancient oral tradition” in a simple sense. Some of it comes through medieval religious memory, some through legal documents, some through 19th-century antiquarian collection, and some through modern heritage retelling.

## Loch Leven: the county’s mythic centre

Loch Leven is the obvious starting point because so many Kinross-shire stories either sit on the loch or look towards it. Gazetteer for Scotland calls it the largest loch in the Scottish lowlands and places it at the heart of the county, with seven islands and the ruins of Lochleven Castle on Castle Island.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://gazetteer.org.uk/place/Kinross-shire" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gazetteer.org.uk">[Gazetteer]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gazetteer.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on gazetteer.org.uk.</span></span></span> That geography gives the loch a natural legendary quality: islands close enough to be seen, old religious sites separated by water, a royal prison, and surrounding hills that turn the loch into a bowl of memory.

The best-known story attached to the loch is historical rather than supernatural: Mary, Queen of Scots was imprisoned at Lochleven Castle in 1567–1568, forced to abdicate, and later escaped with help from the family of her gaoler. Historic Environment Scotland’s visitor material identifies Lochleven Castle as the place where Mary was “once a guest and a prisoner”, while Gazetteer for Scotland gives the same core account in county context.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.historicenvironment.scot/visit/all/balvaird-castle/plan-your-visit/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: historicenvironment.scot">[Historic Environment Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">historicenvironment.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on historicenvironment.scot.</span></span></span> Over time, such sites often attract ghost stories, and Lochleven Castle is no exception: paranormal listings repeat a tradition that Mary haunts the place where she signed her abdication.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.paranormaldatabase.com/lowlands/pertdata.php?pageNum_paradata=1" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: paranormaldatabase.com">[Paranormal Database]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">paranormaldatabase.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Paranormal Database The Paranormal Database</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Paranormal Database The Paranormal Database</span></span></span>

That ghost tradition should be handled carefully. The imprisonment and abdication are historically central to the castle’s identity; the haunting is a later supernatural retelling layered onto a dramatic historical memory. For readers, the value of the legend is not that it proves a ghostly presence, but that it shows how strongly Mary’s captivity still shapes the emotional atmosphere of the loch. The place is remembered not merely as a ruin, but as a scene of confinement, betrayal, escape and national consequence.

Loch Leven also has older sacred associations through St Serf’s Inch, the largest island in the loch. Canmore records the site as St Serf’s Priory Church, also known as a Culdee monastery, and identifies it as a medieval priory in the former county of Kinross-shire. Its references include antiquarian descriptions, Royal Commission inventory work and modern archaeological investigation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://canmore.org.uk/site/27872/loch-leven-st-serfs-island-st-serfs-priory-church?display=image" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: canmore.org.uk">[Canmore]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">canmore.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on canmore.org.uk.</span></span></span> This gives the island a firmer documentary base than many folklore sites: it is not simply a “mysterious island” in local talk, but a recorded religious place whose memory helped shape the loch’s sacred geography.

## St Serf’s Inch and the saintly landscape

St Serf’s Inch is one of Kinross-shire’s most important folklore-adjacent sites because it combines saintly tradition, early Christian memory, medieval monastic history and literary significance. Canmore’s record identifies it as St Serf’s Priory Church on Loch Leven, formerly in Kinross-shire, with the alternative name “Culdee Monastery”.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://canmore.org.uk/site/27872/loch-leven-st-serfs-island-st-serfs-priory-church?display=image" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: canmore.org.uk">[Canmore]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">canmore.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on canmore.org.uk.</span></span></span> The term “Culdee” is often used in older Scottish religious history for early ecclesiastical communities, though the details can be complex; for a general reader, the key point is that St Serf’s Inch was remembered as an early religious island before and during its medieval priory phase.

The saint himself belongs to a wider east-central Scottish religious landscape rather than to Kinross-shire alone. St Serf is associated with Fife, Culross, Orkney traditions and stories involving St Mungo. Those wider stories matter because they show how Kinross-shire’s island tradition fits into a network of Scottish saintly legends rather than standing alone. But the county’s strongest local claim is geographical: the saint’s name is fixed to an island in Loch Leven, and the island’s ecclesiastical remains are documented in national heritage records.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://canmore.org.uk/site/27872/loch-leven-st-serfs-island-st-serfs-priory-church?display=image" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: canmore.org.uk">[Canmore]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">canmore.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on canmore.org.uk.</span></span></span>

The island also matters because of Andrew of Wyntoun, prior of Loch Leven and author of the *Orygynale Cronykil of Scotland*, one of medieval Scotland’s major narrative histories. This is where folklore and literary history begin to meet. Wyntoun’s chronicle helped transmit stories about Scottish kings and earlier history; later readers encountered such material not just as history, but as part of Scotland’s legendary imagination. St Serf’s Inch therefore sits at a crossroads: saintly dedication, monastic record, national chronicle and loch-island romance.

For the Kinross-shire page, this distinction is important. St Serf’s Inch should not be treated as a fairy island or a purely supernatural site without evidence. Its strongest significance is sacred and literary. The folklore lies in the way saintly memory, island isolation and medieval storytelling made the loch feel like a place where ordinary geography and sacred history touched.

## Scotlandwell: healing water, pilgrimage and royal memory

Scotlandwell is one of the clearest examples of Kinross-shire’s living folk landscape. The village is named for its well, and the tradition is direct: the water was believed to have healing power. The Northern Antiquarian summarises the site as a healing well in Scotlandwell, notes early forms of the name including “de fonte Scotie”, and cites place-name scholar Simon Taylor’s view that the name may go back very early.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.thenorthernantiquarian.org/tag/kinross-shire/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: thenorthernantiquarian.org">[thenorthernantiquarian.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">thenorthernantiquarian.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">kinross shire</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">kinross shire</span></span></span> Kinross Museum’s local-history account also presents the well as the centre of the village’s story and links it to the medieval hospital and the tradition of Robert the Bruce taking the waters.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.kinrossmuseum.org.uk/Kinross_Stories/scotlandwell.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: kinrossmuseum.org.uk">[kinrossmuseum.org.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">kinrossmuseum.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on kinrossmuseum.org.uk.</span></span></span>

The most famous belief is that Robert the Bruce came to Scotlandwell and was cured, or at least relieved, of leprosy. This should be described as tradition rather than settled medical history. The British Pilgrimage Trust’s entry calls it a holy well at Scotlandwell and notes both the Red Friars’ use of the site and the Bruce cure tradition.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.britishpilgrimage.org/places/holy-well-scotlandwell-kinross" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: britishpilgrimage.org">[British Pilgrimage Trust]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">britishpilgrimage.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on britishpilgrimage.org.</span></span></span> The Northern Antiquarian also records that later visitors still valued the water for curative properties, citing Ruth and Frank Morris’s 1982 survey of Scottish healing wells and a 1970s example of people travelling from Edinburgh to collect the water.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.thenorthernantiquarian.org/tag/kinross-shire/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: thenorthernantiquarian.org">[thenorthernantiquarian.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">thenorthernantiquarian.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">kinross shire</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">kinross shire</span></span></span>

What makes Scotlandwell especially interesting is that it does not depend on a single neat saint’s legend. Some holy wells are tied to one named saint; Scotlandwell’s identity is broader. It is a healing place, a pilgrimage place, a monastic service place and a royal-memory place. That breadth may be why the story has remained memorable. The well offers several ways into the past: medieval care for the sick, water belief, local place-name history, Bruce legend and modern heritage tourism.

For readers interested in folklore, Scotlandwell is also a good reminder that “folk belief” is not always about monsters or apparitions. A healing well belongs to the everyday supernatural: water that was thought to help the body, a place people visited in hope, and a tradition that survived because it answered a human need.

## Carlin Maggie: the witch turned to stone

Carlin Maggie is Kinross-shire’s most vivid witch-legend landmark. It is a rock stack on the slopes below Bishop Hill, overlooking the Loch Leven side of the Portmoak landscape. Modern walking sources identify it as a distinctive rock pillar, and local tradition says that Maggie was a witch or old woman who quarrelled with the Devil and was turned to stone. Fife Walking summarises the legend: the Devil and the local witch Maggie fell out; he threw rocks at her and finally turned her to stone.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://fifewalking.com/find-a-walk/lomond-hills/bishop-hill/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: fifewalking.com">[Fife Walking]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">fifewalking.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Fife Walking Bishop Hill – Fife Walking</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Fife Walking Bishop Hill – Fife Walking</span></span></span> A Kinross Museum PDF snippet gives the local vocabulary behind the tale, explaining that Maggie was a “carlin”, a Lowland Scots term for an old hag or witch.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.kinrossmuseum.org.uk/our_portmoak/Carlin_Magie_and_stories..pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: kinrossmuseum.org.uk">[kinrossmuseum.org.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">kinrossmuseum.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on kinrossmuseum.org.uk.</span></span></span>

The name matters. “Carlin” belongs to a Scottish and northern English world of folk speech in which old women, witches and supernatural female figures often blur together. The stone does what landscape folklore often does: it turns a striking natural feature into a story with a character, a conflict and a moral shape. The rock is no longer just geology; it becomes Maggie.

The Carlin Maggie legend also links Kinross-shire to a wider British and Scottish pattern of “petrification” tales, where stones are explained as people, giants, witches or dancers turned into rock. Such stories are common around standing stones and odd rock formations, but Carlin Maggie’s local force comes from its visibility above Loch Leven and the Portmoak villages. It is a landmark that can be pointed out, walked to, photographed and retold.

There is no need to treat the story as a literal survival from prehistory to make it valuable. Its importance lies in how a community explained an uncanny shape in the landscape. A jagged rock on a hill became an old witch, a quarrel with the Devil, and a warning fixed in stone.

## Crook of Devon: witchcraft as record, not just legend

The Crook of Devon witch trials are among the most sobering pieces of Kinross-shire’s folklore-related history. Unlike Carlin Maggie, this is not simply a landscape tale. It is rooted in legal proceedings from 1662, during the wider Scottish witch-hunt period. The Proceedings of the Society of Antiquaries of Scotland published R. Burns Begg’s “Notice of Trials for Witchcraft at Crook of Devon, Kinross-shire, in 1662” in 1888, covering pages 211–241 of volume 22.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://journals.socantscot.org/index.php/psas/article/view/6264" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journals.socantscot.org">[Society of Antiquaries of Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journals.socantscot.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on socantscot.org.</span></span></span>

Modern historical discussion stresses that the accused were ordinary people caught in a wave of fear, accusation and legal violence rather than fairy-tale witches. Dundee Scottish Culture’s account, by historian Allan Kennedy, describes the Crook of Devon accused as rural people swept up in the witch-paranoia that affected Scotland in the early 1660s; it also notes that the “Witches’ Maze” at Tullibole Castle was unveiled in 2012 as a memorial to those indicted at Crook of Devon.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://dundeescottishculture.org/history/the-crook-of-devon-witches/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: dundeescottishculture.org">[dundeescottishculture.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">dundeescottishculture.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on dundeescottishculture.org.</span></span></span> Kinross Museum likewise presents the Crook of Devon trials as persecution, not as proof of magical practice.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.kinrossmuseum.org.uk/Kinross_Stories/persecution.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: kinrossmuseum.org.uk">[kinrossmuseum.org.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">kinrossmuseum.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on kinrossmuseum.org.uk.</span></span></span>

This is a crucial distinction for public folklore writing. Witch-trial material belongs in a folklore project because it shows what people believed, feared, alleged and punished. But the people accused should not be turned into spooky characters for entertainment. The folklore is in the accusations and the belief-world: malefice, pacts, quarrels, illness, neighbourly suspicion and the legal imagination of witchcraft. The human reality is persecution.

Crook of Devon also gives Kinross-shire a strong connection to the wider Scottish witch-hunt. The University of Edinburgh’s Survey of Scottish Witchcraft is a national resource for the history of witchcraft and witch-hunting in Scotland, and its reading list includes the Crook of Devon trial publication among important printed materials.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://witches.hca.ed.ac.uk/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: witches.hca.ed.ac.uk">[Witches]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">witches.hca.ed.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on ed.ac.uk.</span></span></span> In county terms, Crook of Devon matters because it shows that even a small rural shire was not outside the national machinery of fear.

## Saints, dragons and neighbouring echoes

Kinross-shire itself is not best known for a dragon legend, but its folklore sits very close to dragon-bearing saintly traditions in neighbouring Perthshire and Fife. Historic Environment Scotland’s visitor material for nearby sites repeatedly describes St Serf’s Church and Dupplin Cross at Dunning as the place where the 6th-century St Serf supposedly slew a dragon.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.historicenvironment.scot/visit/all/balvaird-castle/plan-your-visit/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: historicenvironment.scot">[Historic Environment Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">historicenvironment.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on historicenvironment.scot.</span></span></span> Dunning is outside the core Kinross-shire county focus, but the connection is still useful because St Serf’s name also anchors St Serf’s Inch in Loch Leven.

This is a good example of how folklore crosses county borders. Saints did not stay inside later administrative boundaries, and neither did their stories. St Serf’s Kinross-shire presence is tied to an island priory and sacred lochscape; his Dunning legend presents the saint as a dragon-slayer. The two should not be collapsed into one local Kinross-shire dragon tale, but they belong to the same regional saintly imagination.

For readers, this helps explain why Kinross-shire folklore can feel both local and connected. A tiny county may preserve one part of a saint’s geography while a neighbouring place preserves another. The result is a patchwork: island, church, dragon, cross, well, priory and parish tradition spread across a compact part of east-central Scotland.

## Standing stones, place-names and older ground

Kinross-shire also has prehistoric and early historic remains that invite folklore, even where specific legends are thin. Gazetteer for Scotland notes standing stones near Milnathort and describes settlement evidence in the area going back millennia.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://gazetteer.org.uk/place/Kinross-shire" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gazetteer.org.uk">[Gazetteer]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gazetteer.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on gazetteer.org.uk.</span></span></span> The modern place-name Milnathort has itself been discussed in relation to “standing stones”: one account gives its origin as connected with the “mill in land of standing stones”, probably the Orwell stones, while noting that older interpretations varied.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Milnathort" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wikipedia.org.</span></span></span>

This kind of material is easy to overstate. Not every standing stone has a surviving fairy, giant or witch story attached to it. But in folklore terms, prehistoric stones often become story magnets because they are visible, ancient and unexplained in ordinary village memory. They may attract tales of giants, petrified people, hidden treasure, fairy movement or ritual danger elsewhere in Britain. In Kinross-shire, the safer claim is that the stones contribute to the county’s mythic texture rather than forming a large, well-attested legendary cycle of their own.

The place-name evidence is valuable because it shows how older landscape memory survives in everyday speech. Milnathort, Portmoak, Scotlandwell, Carlin Maggie and Crook of Devon all carry stories or historical explanations in their names. For a small county, names are one of the main archives of folk culture.

## What is old tradition, and what is modern retelling?

Kinross-shire is a good county for learning how to separate layers of tradition. Not every story has the same kind of evidence, and a good folklore page should make those differences clear without draining the stories of interest.

**The strongest documentary layer** is the Crook of Devon witch-trial material. The 1662 proceedings were printed in the Proceedings of the Society of Antiquaries of Scotland in 1888, and modern historians continue to discuss them as part of Scotland’s witch-hunt history.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://journals.socantscot.org/index.php/psas/article/view/6264" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journals.socantscot.org">[Society of Antiquaries of Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journals.socantscot.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on socantscot.org.</span></span></span>

**The strongest heritage-landscape layer** is St Serf’s Inch. Canmore records the priory site, its former county, its alternative “Culdee Monastery” name and a chain of antiquarian and archaeological references.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://canmore.org.uk/site/27872/loch-leven-st-serfs-island-st-serfs-priory-church?display=image" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: canmore.org.uk">[Canmore]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">canmore.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on canmore.org.uk.</span></span></span> The saintly associations are older and wider than the county, but the island itself gives Kinross-shire a firm sacred landmark.

**The strongest healing-belief layer** is Scotlandwell. The site is named for its water, appears in place-name and holy-well discussions, and is still presented in local and pilgrimage material as a healing well associated with the Red Friars and Robert the Bruce tradition.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.thenorthernantiquarian.org/tag/kinross-shire/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: thenorthernantiquarian.org">[thenorthernantiquarian.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">thenorthernantiquarian.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">kinross shire</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">kinross shire</span></span></span>

**The strongest landscape-legend layer** is Carlin Maggie. This is the classic folk process: a striking rock formation receives a witch story. The evidence is not legal or medieval, but the tale is locally attached, visually memorable and still used in walking and local-history contexts.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://fifewalking.com/find-a-walk/lomond-hills/bishop-hill/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: fifewalking.com">[Fife Walking]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">fifewalking.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Fife Walking Bishop Hill – Fife Walking</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Fife Walking Bishop Hill – Fife Walking</span></span></span>

**The modern supernatural layer** includes ghost retellings at Lochleven Castle. These are best understood as later atmospheric traditions built on a real historical event: Mary’s imprisonment and abdication.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.paranormaldatabase.com/lowlands/pertdata.php?pageNum_paradata=1" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: paranormaldatabase.com">[Paranormal Database]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">paranormaldatabase.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Paranormal Database The Paranormal Database</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Paranormal Database The Paranormal Database</span></span></span>

This layering does not make the folklore weaker. It makes it more interesting. Kinross-shire shows how a county’s story culture is built from documents, ruins, water, stones, parish memory, local speech and visitor imagination.

## How Kinross-shire is retold today

Today, Kinross-shire’s folklore is encountered less through fireside storytelling and more through museums, local-history websites, walking routes, heritage records, tourism pages and community memory. Kinross Museum preserves and shares local stories, including Scotlandwell and the Crook of Devon witch trials.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.kinrossmuseum.org.uk/Kinross_Stories/scotlandwell.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: kinrossmuseum.org.uk">[kinrossmuseum.org.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">kinrossmuseum.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on kinrossmuseum.org.uk.</span></span></span> Canmore and Historic Environment Scotland provide the formal heritage frame for places such as St Serf’s Inch and Lochleven Castle.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://canmore.org.uk/site/27872/loch-leven-st-serfs-island-st-serfs-priory-church?display=image" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: canmore.org.uk">[Canmore]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">canmore.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on canmore.org.uk.</span></span></span> Walking guides and outdoor sources keep Carlin Maggie visible as a named landmark on Bishop Hill.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://fifewalking.com/find-a-walk/lomond-hills/bishop-hill/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: fifewalking.com">[Fife Walking]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">fifewalking.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Fife Walking Bishop Hill – Fife Walking</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Fife Walking Bishop Hill – Fife Walking</span></span></span>

This modern retelling changes the folklore. A tale once known locally as a warning, explanation or parish anecdote becomes a stop on a walk, a museum panel, a heritage article or a tourism hook. That can flatten stories if they are reduced to “spooky facts”, but it can also preserve them. Without modern retelling, a small county’s traditions are easily overshadowed by better-known Scottish folklore from Skye, Orkney, Edinburgh or the Highlands.

The most responsible modern version of Kinross-shire folklore is therefore both lively and careful. It can enjoy the witch turned to stone at Carlin Maggie, but it should not romanticise the real persecution at Crook of Devon. It can tell the Mary ghost story at Lochleven Castle, but it should first explain the historical imprisonment. It can present Scotlandwell as a healing well, but it should describe the Bruce cure as tradition rather than medical fact. It can treat St Serf’s Inch as sacred and evocative, while grounding it in the recorded priory site.

## The county’s folklore identity

Kinross-shire’s folklore identity is not built on a single famous monster or one nationally dominant legend. It is built on a tight relationship between water, stone and memory. Loch Leven gives the county its centre. St Serf’s Inch gives it sacred depth. Scotlandwell gives it healing belief. Carlin Maggie gives it a witch in the hills. Crook of Devon gives it a hard historical encounter with witchcraft accusation. Lochleven Castle gives it royal drama and later ghostly atmosphere.

That combination makes Kinross-shire quietly distinctive. Its legends are not especially numerous, but they are unusually placeable. A reader can look at a map and understand how the stories sit together: the loch, the island, the castle, the well, the hill, the village, the trial site. In a folklore project organised by historic counties, that is exactly what makes Kinross-shire worth its own page.

    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-trigger-market-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = selectedText;
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-domain-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = domainLabel(marketId);
    });

      button.setAttribute('aria-selected', button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-option') === marketId ? 'true' : 'false');
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      var active = button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button') === marketId;
      button.setAttribute('aria-pressed', active ? 'true' : 'false');
    });
    section.setAttribute('data-ebay-selected-market', marketId);
    if (persist) {
      try { if (window.localStorage) localStorage.setItem('phoenix-ebay-market', marketId); } catch (err) {}
    }

        if (picker) picker.classList.toggle('fr-ebay-market-picker--open', !expanded);

        });
      });
      document.addEventListener('click', function () { closeMenu(section); });
    }

      button.addEventListener('click', function () {
        applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button'), true);
      });
    });
    applyMarket(section, marketId, false);
