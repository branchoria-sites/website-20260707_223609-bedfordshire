## Introduction

Schiehallion is called a fairy mountain because its long-established Gaelic name is commonly interpreted as the “Fairy Hill of the Caledonians”, and because later [Perthshire]({{ 'where-perthshires-strange-stories-meet/' | relative_url }}) tradition has treated the mountain as a place where landscape, older belief, walking culture and modern retelling meet. It is not simply a pretty hill with a romantic nickname. Its shape, caves, mist, isolation and visibility across [Highland Perthshire]({{ 'gaelic-tales/' | relative_url }}) all help explain why stories gathered around it: fairies inside the mountain, strange music, spectral hounds, a winter witch, healing waters and a sense that the hill is more than ordinary ground. The evidence is uneven: the place-name is well attested, while individual tales often survive through antiquarian writing, local retelling, walking literature and recent conservation storytelling rather than a single neat medieval source. That is part of its interest. Schiehallion shows how Perthshire folklore lives in place: on paths, ridges, old shieling ground and the stories walkers carry uphill with them.

## The Gaelic Name and Fairy Reputation

Schiehallion stands in Highland Perthshire, between the Rannoch, Tummel and Tay landscapes, and rises to 1,083 metres as one of Scotland’s best-known Munros. The John Muir Trust, which owns and manages East Schiehallion, gives the Gaelic form as Sìth Chailleann and translates it as “Fairy Hill of the Caledonians”; it also places the mountain within the Loch Rannoch and Glen Lyon National Scenic Area and notes its Site of Special Scientific Interest status for geology and limestone-influenced habitats.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.johnmuirtrust.org/about-us/where-we-work/east-schiehallion" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: johnmuirtrust.org">[johnmuirtrust.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">johnmuirtrust.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on johnmuirtrust.org.</span></span></span>

The name matters because it gives the fairy tradition a firmer footing than many later mountain legends. Gaelic place-name discussion treats sìth or sìdh as a word that can carry the sense of “fairy” and “hill”, especially in names associated with fairy mounds or otherworldly places. A Gaelic place-name article in *The Bottle Imp* explains that sìth or sìdh can mean “fairy” and “hill” and is usually understood in Scottish place-names as denoting a fairy hill, while a Scotland’s Nature essay identifies Schiehallion as the best-known example interpreted by place-name scholar W. J. Watson as the Fairy Hill of the Caledonians.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.thebottleimp.org.uk/2009/11/gaelic-place-names-sith-and-sithean/?print=print" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: thebottleimp.org.uk">[The Bottle Imp]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">thebottleimp.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Bottle Imp Gaelic Place Names: &#x27;Sìth&#x27; and &#x27;Sìthean</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Bottle Imp Gaelic Place Names: &#x27;Sìth&#x27; and &#x27;Sìthean</span></span></span>

That does not mean every detail attached to Schiehallion is ancient, fixed or equally well evidenced. The place-name gives a strong traditional frame, but many of the stories now told around the mountain have passed through nineteenth- and twentieth-century antiquarian writing, local memory, hillwalking culture, podcasts, magazine features and conservation projects. This makes Schiehallion a good example of living folklore rather than a fossilised legend: the same hill can be read as a Gaelic fairy place, a Perthshire landmark, a scientific site, a conservation landscape and a modern walking destination.

There is also a useful caution about translation. “Fairy” in this setting should not be imagined as a harmless Victorian winged sprite. In Highland tradition, fairy beings are often powerful, morally ambiguous neighbours: connected with music, glamour, abduction, illness, warning, luck, cattle, death and the underground world. The mountain’s name therefore points less to decorative fantasy than to a landscape in which the unseen was once treated as close, dangerous and woven into ordinary life.

## Why This Mountain Attracted Stories

Schiehallion looks like a mountain made for legend. From some angles it appears strikingly conical; from the walking route it reveals itself as a long east-west ridge. Its isolation made it useful for science in the eighteenth century, but the same isolation also helps explain its imaginative force. The Royal Society describes how Nevil Maskelyne and Charles Hutton used Schiehallion in 1774 for an attempt to calculate the density of the Earth, partly because the mountain’s shape and separation from neighbouring hills made it unusually suitable for measurement.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://royalsociety.org/blog/2022/08/song-of-schiehallion/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: royalsociety.org">[Royal Society]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">royalsociety.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">song of schiehallion</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">song of schiehallion</span></span></span>

That scientific history is not separate from the folklore; it sharpens the point. Schiehallion is a hill people have repeatedly used to think about invisible powers. For Maskelyne and Hutton, the unseen force was gravity. For folk tradition, it was the presence of fairies, spectral beings or older powers within the land. The Royal Society notes that the mountain’s “fairy hill” reputation has become part of the wider story of the experiment, while later writing has linked Schiehallion’s contours, weather and legend into a single cultural memory.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://royalsociety.org/blog/2022/08/song-of-schiehallion/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: royalsociety.org">[Royal Society]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">royalsociety.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">song of schiehallion</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">song of schiehallion</span></span></span>

The mountain’s physical character encourages this layering. John Muir Trust material describes East Schiehallion as a place of quartzite, limestone-influenced habitats, rare mountain willows and upland wildlife, while walking guides emphasise the long maintained path, the rougher boulder field near the summit and the wide views over the surrounding Perthshire and Highland landscape.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.johnmuirtrust.org/about-us/where-we-work/east-schiehallion" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: johnmuirtrust.org">[johnmuirtrust.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">johnmuirtrust.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on johnmuirtrust.org.</span></span></span> To a walker, these are practical details. To a storyteller, they become thresholds: woodland to moor, path to boulder field, clear weather to mist, ordinary daylight to a summit world that can suddenly feel remote.

Schiehallion also sits in a wider Perthshire habit of attaching tradition to named natural places. In this county, folklore often belongs to a particular hill, well, glen, cave, stone or route rather than to an abstract mythological system. That is why Schiehallion works so well as a pillar of Perthshire fairy lore: it gives the tradition a visible body on the map.

## Hollow Mountain Tales and Fairy Entrances

One of the most memorable strands of Schiehallion lore is the idea that the mountain is hollow, or that a cave on or near it leads into a fairy realm. Modern retellings often point to a cave in the Gleann Mòr area, sometimes named as the dwelling place or entrance of the fairies of Schiehallion. Cameron McNeish’s hillwriting places the fairy cave at Uamh Tom a’ Mhor-fhir in the upper reaches of the glen, while other walking and heritage retellings describe a cave whose narrowing passage is imagined as a gateway too small for ordinary human entry.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cameronmcneish.wordpress.com/2013/12/18/schiehallion-one-of-the-worlds-holy-hills/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cameronmcneish.wordpress.com">[cameronmcneish.wordpress.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cameronmcneish.wordpress.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Schiehallion – one of the world&#x27;s Holy hills?</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Schiehallion – one of the world&#x27;s Holy hills?</span></span></span>

This motif fits a much wider Gaelic and British pattern: fairy people living under hills, inside mounds, beneath green knolls or beyond narrow entrances that mark the border between human and otherworldly space. What makes Schiehallion distinctive is the scale. Many fairy hills are small rounded mounds; Schiehallion turns the same idea into a whole mountain. A walker looking at the long ridge or a cave-mouth under the slopes can understand how the old fairy-hill idea becomes grander here.

The hollow mountain story should be read as tradition rather than geology. Schiehallion is not literally a vast empty mountain, and its geology is better explained through quartzite, limestone-influenced lower habitats and glacial shaping than through supernatural chambers. But folklore does not need the mountain to be physically hollow in a modern scientific sense. The story gives form to a feeling: that the visible hill is only the outer skin of a deeper, hidden world.

That hidden-world idea is why music, doors, lights and vanished people often appear in retellings of fairy hills. A piper tempted by the fairy mountain, a cave that opens at certain times, or a person who strays across the boundary all belong to the same family of stories. They make the landscape morally charged. The warning is not simply “do not go there”; it is “know where you are, and know that some places are not fully yours”.

## Spectral Hounds and Darker Fairy Lore

Schiehallion’s fairy reputation is not only delicate or beautiful. It also connects with darker Highland motifs, including spectral hounds. Recent folklore writing has linked Schiehallion with sightings or traditions of the Cù Sìth, the fairy hound of Scottish tradition, and Atlas Obscura summarises the wider belief as an otherworldly dog associated with fairies, death omens, hills, mounds and wild places.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.atlasobscura.com/articles/cu-sith-scottish-folklore-black-dog" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: atlasobscura.com">[Atlas Obscura]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">atlasobscura.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Atlas Obscura Death as a Dog: The Cù Sith of Scotland</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Atlas Obscura Death as a Dog: The Cù Sith of Scotland</span></span></span>

The important point for a Perthshire reader is that this is not the same as a generic “black dog” ghost story imported from elsewhere. Across Britain, spectral dogs appear in many regional forms: churchyard dogs, road-haunting black dogs, hellhounds, death omens and fairy hounds. In the Highland version, the fairy connection is especially strong because the hound is tied linguistically and imaginatively to the same otherworld as the fairy hill. Atlas Obscura notes that “Cù” means hound and that the second element is linked with fairies or the mounds where they dwelt.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.atlasobscura.com/articles/cu-sith-scottish-folklore-black-dog" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: atlasobscura.com">[Atlas Obscura]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">atlasobscura.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Atlas Obscura Death as a Dog: The Cù Sith of Scotland</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Atlas Obscura Death as a Dog: The Cù Sith of Scotland</span></span></span>

This darker layer changes how Schiehallion should be understood. A fairy mountain was not necessarily a place of sweetness. It could be beautiful, alluring and dangerous at once. The same landscape that offers views and wonder to a modern walker could once be imagined as a place where a person might hear uncanny music, encounter an omen, lose time, fall under a spell or meet a hound that belonged to powers beyond the human community.

Some claims about Schiehallion’s spectral dogs are easier to find in modern folklore journalism and retelling than in a single early local source. That does not make the motif worthless, but it does mean it should be handled carefully. The strongest statement is that Schiehallion’s fairy-hill identity makes it a natural home for fairy-hound retellings, and that recent writers have explicitly placed the Cù Sìth on or around its slopes. A more cautious reading is better than pretending every modern detail has the same age as the Gaelic place-name.

## The Winter Witch, Healing Waters and Local Memory

Another set of Schiehallion traditions centres on a wintery female figure, often presented in modern retellings as a frost-haired witch or queen of winter. The John Muir Trust has retold a Schiehallion winter story under the title “Schiehallion and the Queen of Winter”, again introducing the mountain through its Gaelic name and fairy-hill translation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.johnmuirtrust.org/whats-new/news/1574-schiehallion-and-the-queen-of-winter" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: johnmuirtrust.org">[johnmuirtrust.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">johnmuirtrust.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">1574 schiehallion and the queen of winter</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">1574 schiehallion and the queen of winter</span></span></span> Hillwriter Cameron McNeish, drawing on local Rannoch storytelling, also refers to an old witch associated with Schiehallion, described as blue with cold, white-haired with frost and wrapped in a grey plaid.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cameronmcneish.wordpress.com/2013/12/18/schiehallion-one-of-the-worlds-holy-hills/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cameronmcneish.wordpress.com">[cameronmcneish.wordpress.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cameronmcneish.wordpress.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Schiehallion – one of the world&#x27;s Holy hills?</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Schiehallion – one of the world&#x27;s Holy hills?</span></span></span>

This figure belongs to a familiar Highland way of personifying weather, winter and mountain harshness. The story works because Schiehallion is not only a symbolic fairy hill; it is a real upland place where weather can change quickly and where mist, cold, snow and exposure shape experience. The “witch” is not best treated as a named historical person, but as a story-form that makes winter visible and memorable.

Healing-water motifs also appear in modern writing about the mountain. A Financial Times feature on Schiehallion notes tales of a frosted-haired witch and a well with healing waters, placing them alongside the hollow mountain and fairy realm traditions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ft.com/content/d23aa2e3-db1c-4dab-aa2d-71131192b6c7" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ft.com">[Financial Times]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ft.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on ft.com.</span></span></span> This pairing of danger and cure is common in folk landscapes: the same uncanny place may threaten, heal, bless or test, depending on how it is approached.

There is a useful distinction here between old oral tradition and modern packaging. A conservation article, a walking feature or a podcast may preserve genuine local memory, but it also reshapes it for contemporary readers and visitors. Schiehallion’s legends today often arrive through guided walks, hill blogs, podcasts, magazine essays and John Muir Trust storytelling, not through a village hearthside chain that can be reconstructed in full. The responsible reading is not to dismiss the stories, but to notice how each generation has found new ways to tell them.

## Walking the Fairy Hill Today

For many people, Schiehallion’s fairy reputation is first encountered not in a folklore book but on the path from Braes of Foss. Walkhighlands describes the route beginning at the car park, passing through planted native woodland and old sheepfanks before climbing towards the east ridge. The Great Outdoors’ John Muir Trust route guide likewise presents the walk as a clear ridge route from Braes of Foss, returning the same way after the summit.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.walkhighlands.co.uk/perthshire/schiehallion.shtml" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: walkhighlands.co.uk">[walkhighlands]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">walkhighlands.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on walkhighlands.co.uk.</span></span></span>

That modern path is part of the folklore landscape now. Schiehallion’s popularity caused serious erosion, and the John Muir Trust’s management of East Schiehallion has focused on repairing and redirecting access so that walkers can enjoy the hill without cutting an ever-wider scar into it. UKHillwalking reported in 2013 on the visible success of restoration work, describing before-and-after evidence of a heavily eroded trail being returned towards nature over more than a decade.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ukhillwalking.com/news/2013/08/trust_in_schiehallion_restoration_success-68252" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ukhillwalking.com">[UKHillwalking]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ukhillwalking.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">UKH News</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UKH News</span></span></span>

This matters because modern folklore is not only about what people say; it is also about how people behave around meaningful places. A fairy hill treated as a disposable backdrop becomes a prop. A fairy hill walked with care remains a living landscape. The Trust’s account of East Schiehallion stresses both natural value and cultural meaning: rare upland species, limestone habitats, geology, the Gaelic name and the mountain’s public accessibility all sit together.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.johnmuirtrust.org/about-us/where-we-work/east-schiehallion" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: johnmuirtrust.org">[johnmuirtrust.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">johnmuirtrust.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on johnmuirtrust.org.</span></span></span>

The walk also teaches why the legend has lasted. In clear weather, Schiehallion feels open and almost orderly, its ridge leading the eye across Perthshire and towards the Highlands. In mist, the same ridge can feel disorientating and otherworldly. The final boulder field, where the made path gives way to rough quartzite, is a physical reminder that the mountain is not fully tamed by popularity. That shift from managed path to broken summit ground is exactly the kind of threshold on which fairy tradition thrives.

## Modern Retellings and Perthshire Identity

Schiehallion now belongs to several overlapping publics. Munro-baggers know it as an accessible but still serious mountain. Conservationists know it as a restored upland site. Scientists know it through the 1774 experiment and the development of contour mapping. Folklore readers know it as the Fairy Hill of the Caledonians. Local tourism pages for Rannoch and Tummel present it as an iconic Highland Perthshire mountain with many myths attached, while Hidden Scotland frames it as a mountain of myth, caves, walking and deep history.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://rannochandtummel.co.uk/Schiehallion" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: rannochandtummel.co.uk">[Rannoch and Tummel]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">rannochandtummel.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Rannoch and Tummel Schiehallion The Iconic Mountain</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Rannoch and Tummel Schiehallion The Iconic Mountain</span></span></span>

Recent creative projects have strengthened that layered identity. The Stories of Scotland podcast has devoted an episode to Schiehallion, Highland folklore and second sight, drawing on archive material, oral history and older sources to retell stories of the fairy hill, Rannoch beliefs and local memory.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.storiesofscotland.com/podcast/2021/9/20/schiehallion-highland-folklore-and-second-sight" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: storiesofscotland.com">[Stories of Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">storiesofscotland.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Stories of Scotland Schiehallion: Highland Folklore &amp; Second Sight</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Stories of Scotland Schiehallion: Highland Folklore &amp; Second Sight</span></span></span> Artist and storyteller Cat Burton’s “Stories of Schiehallion” project with the John Muir Trust aimed to draw attention not only to the summit view but to the wider landscape: slopes, glen, woodland, waterfalls, ruined shielings, history, folklore and conservation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.catburton.co.uk/stories-of-schiehallion" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: catburton.co.uk">[CAT BURTON]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">catburton.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">CAT BURTONStories Of Schiehallion</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">CAT BURTONStories Of Schiehallion</span></span></span>

The mountain’s scientific story has also become part of its mythic aura rather than a rival to it. The Royal Society’s account of the Schiehallion experiment describes Maskelyne’s attempt to calculate the Earth’s density, while later scholarship and reporting highlight Charles Hutton’s use of lines of equal height in calculating the mountain’s volume, a key step in the history of contour lines.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://royalsociety.org/blog/2022/08/song-of-schiehallion/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: royalsociety.org">[Royal Society]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">royalsociety.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">song of schiehallion</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">song of schiehallion</span></span></span> In a strange but fitting way, the fairy mountain helped teach modern map-readers how to see the shape of land.

For Perthshire, that makes Schiehallion more than a scenic Munro. It is a county landmark where Gaelic place-names, Highland fairy belief, Rannoch storytelling, upland ecology, scientific measurement and modern walking all converge. It also shows why historic Perthshire remains a useful folklore frame even when modern administrative boundaries are more complicated. The stories cling to the old mountain, glens, paths and local names, not to council lines.

## What Is Well Attested and What Should Be Read Carefully?

The best-attested element is the name and its fairy-hill interpretation. The John Muir Trust, Gaelic place-name discussion and wider Scottish place-name writing all support Schiehallion’s identity as Sìth Chailleann, commonly translated as the Fairy Hill of the Caledonians.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.johnmuirtrust.org/about-us/where-we-work/east-schiehallion" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: johnmuirtrust.org">[johnmuirtrust.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">johnmuirtrust.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on johnmuirtrust.org.</span></span></span> That gives the mountain a stronger folklore foundation than a legend based only on a late tourist anecdote.

The broader fairy reputation is also well established in modern and local cultural use. John Muir Trust storytelling, Rannoch tourism material, walking features, folklore podcasts and Scottish travel writing all present Schiehallion as a fairy mountain.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.johnmuirtrust.org/whats-new/news/1594-schiehallion-fairies" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: johnmuirtrust.org">[johnmuirtrust.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">johnmuirtrust.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">1594 schiehallion fairies</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">1594 schiehallion fairies</span></span></span> These are not all the same kind of evidence, but together they show a durable public tradition.

Individual tale details need more caution. The hollow mountain, fairy cave, winter witch, healing well, spectral hounds, piper stories and second-sight material are part of the Schiehallion story-world, but they do not all come with the same documentary weight. Some are clearly grounded in broader Highland motifs; some are preserved through local or antiquarian retelling; some are modern presentations of older themes. A careful reader should treat them as layers rather than as one single, fixed legend.

That layered quality is exactly why Schiehallion matters. It is not a museum specimen of one tale told once. It is a Perthshire mythic landscape still being walked, repaired, photographed, researched and retold. Its fairy identity survives because it is attached to something people can see from miles away, climb with effort, and remember long after they have left the ridge.

      link.setAttribute('href', buildUrl(query, reference, marketId));
    });
    var select = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-select]');
    if (select && available.indexOf(marketId) >= 0) select.value = marketId;
    var selectedText = marketId;
    if (select && select.selectedIndex >= 0) selectedText = select.options[select.selectedIndex].text;
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-selected-market-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = selectedText;
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-selected-market-flag], [data-ebay-trigger-market-flag]')).forEach(function (flag) {
      flag.className = flagClass(marketId);
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-trigger-market-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = selectedText;
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-domain-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = domainLabel(marketId);
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-option]')).forEach(function (button) {
      button.setAttribute('aria-selected', button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-option') === marketId ? 'true' : 'false');
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      var active = button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button') === marketId;
      button.setAttribute('aria-pressed', active ? 'true' : 'false');
    });

      try { if (window.localStorage) localStorage.setItem('phoenix-ebay-market', marketId); } catch (err) {}
    }

      });

      marketId = available[0] || defaultMarket;
    }
    var select = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-select]');

        applyMarket(section, select.value, true);

    }

        applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button'), true);

  });
