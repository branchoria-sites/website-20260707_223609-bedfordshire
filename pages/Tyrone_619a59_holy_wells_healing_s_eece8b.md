## Introduction

[Tyrone]({{ 'where-tyrone-turns-landscape-into-legend/' | relative_url }})’s holy wells and healing stones are not a single neat “pilgrimage route”, but a scatter of local places where water, rock, saint-lore and folk medicine meet. The strongest examples are St Patrick’s Chair and Well at Altadaven Glen near Augher, St Patrick’s Well at Magherakeel near Killeter, and the bog stone at Garvagh Pultons whose rain-filled hollows were remembered as a toothache cure. Together they show a very Tyrone pattern: sacred power is not only in church buildings or famous ruins, but in damp woodland, boulders, wells, rag trees, roads to Lough Derg, and stones marked by strange impressions. These traditions matter because they preserve everyday beliefs about illness, hope and protection in the county landscape, while also showing how Christian devotion absorbed older-looking practices around water, wishing and marked stone.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.visitmidulster.com/things-to-do/st-patricks-chair-and-well-p763941" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: visitmidulster.com">[visitmidulster.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">visitmidulster.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Visit Mid Ulster St Patrick’s Chair and Well</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Visit Mid Ulster St Patrick’s Chair and Well</span></span></span>

Tyrone is treated here as the historic county in Northern Ireland, centred on places such as Omagh, Dungannon, Strabane, Clogher, Augher, Castlederg and the Sperrin borderlands. That matters because some of the best surviving records sit close to county edges, especially in west Tyrone near Donegal and the old pilgrim approaches to Lough Derg. Tyrone’s healing-place folklore is therefore both local and borderland: rooted in named townlands, but connected to wider Ulster and Irish traditions of holy wells, curing water, rag trees and water held in stone.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://wikishire.co.uk/wiki/Tyrone" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wikishire.co.uk">[Wikishire]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wikishire.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wikishire.co.uk.</span></span></span>

## Altadaven’s Chair and Well

The best-known healing-stone site in Tyrone is St Patrick’s Chair and Well at Altadaven Glen, near Augher. Visit Mid Ulster describes it as a large two-metre sandstone “chair”, also known as the Druids Chair and Well or St Brigid’s Well, with folklore that sitting in the chair and making a wish can bring fulfilment. The “well” is not a conventional spring with masonry around it, but a separate rock with a water-filled depression; local interpretation identifies this kind of hollow as a bullaun, a stone basin associated in folklore with healing water.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.visitmidulster.com/things-to-do/st-patricks-chair-and-well-p763941" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: visitmidulster.com">[Visit Mid Ulster]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">visitmidulster.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Visit Mid Ulster St Patrick’s Chair and Well</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Visit Mid Ulster St Patrick’s Chair and Well</span></span></span>

The place is vivid because the elements are so closely packed together: a throne-like stone, a rock basin that holds water, rag trees, steep woodland and a name linked in later folklore to Patrick’s triumph over dark or hostile powers. Voices from the Dawn places the site in Altadaven Glen on the Ulster Way, within the former Favour Royal Demesne and now associated with the People’s Millennium Forest. It notes that water naturally collects in a sculpted bullaun of about 25 cm in a horizontal rock platform, and that the basin is said in local folklore never to run dry.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://voicesfromthedawn.com/st-patricks-chair-and-well/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: voicesfromthedawn.com">[Voices from the Dawn]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">voicesfromthedawn.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Voices from the Dawn St. Patrick&#x27;s Chair and Well</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Voices from the Dawn St. Patrick&#x27;s Chair and Well</span></span></span>

The curing tradition at Altadaven is especially attached to warts. The same account records that the water was said to cure many ailments, but was particularly known for wart treatment; Visit Mid Ulster gives the broader public-facing version, saying that water in such depressions or bullauns has healing powers. The important point is not that this should be treated as medical advice, but that the belief fits a very widespread Irish pattern in which rainwater gathered in a stone hollow was thought to carry special force.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://voicesfromthedawn.com/st-patricks-chair-and-well/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: voicesfromthedawn.com">[Voices from the Dawn]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">voicesfromthedawn.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Voices from the Dawn St. Patrick&#x27;s Chair and Well</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Voices from the Dawn St. Patrick&#x27;s Chair and Well</span></span></span>

Altadaven also shows how one site can carry several names and layers at once. It is St Patrick’s Chair, St Brigid’s Well, the Druids Chair and Well, a wishing place, a healing place and a woodland walk. That mixture is exactly what makes it valuable in Tyrone folklore. The Christian names give the site saintly authority, while the chair, stone basin and rag trees keep alive older-looking practices of touching, sitting, leaving tokens and seeking help from the landscape itself.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.visitmidulster.com/things-to-do/st-patricks-chair-and-well-p763941" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: visitmidulster.com">[Visit Mid Ulster]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">visitmidulster.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Visit Mid Ulster St Patrick’s Chair and Well</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Visit Mid Ulster St Patrick’s Chair and Well</span></span></span>

## Bog Stones and Toothache Cures

Not every Tyrone healing place is a famous visitor site. One of the most revealing records comes from the Schools’ Collection at Dúchas, where a short account from Gleneely describes a “peculiar flat shaped rock” in the townland of Garvagh Pultons, “in Co Tyrone just over the Donegal border”. The stone lay in the middle of a bog and bore the impression of a goose’s foot, as well as many small round holes. Rainwater lying in those holes was described as a common local cure for toothache, with the collector adding that many cures had been effected.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.duchas.ie/en/cbes/4547216/4413794/4548054" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: duchas.ie">[Dúchas]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">duchas.ie</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on duchas.ie.</span></span></span>

This small record is easy to overlook, but it is one of the clearest Tyrone examples of healing power being attached to a marked stone rather than to a named saint’s well. The goose-foot impression gives the rock a story-like quality: it is not just a practical source of water, but a stone that looks as if something unusual has touched it. The little holes then become natural cups, holding rainwater that can be used ritually. In folklore terms, the cure depends on the combination of mark, water and place.

The Garvagh Pultons stone also helps explain why “holy wells and healing stones” belong together. In ordinary speech, a well suggests flowing or springing water, but in local practice a stone hollow could do similar work. If water gathered in a basin-like depression and the site had a memorable story or mark, people might treat it as powerful. This is the same logic that makes Altadaven’s bullaun well meaningful, but the Garvagh example is rougher, boggier and less officially packaged.

The toothache detail is especially useful because it points to folk medicine rather than only to pilgrimage. Toothache cures appear widely in Irish folklore, often involving water, charms, pins, stones, plants or ritual actions. In Tyrone’s case, the Garvagh record keeps the cure grounded in a specific bog rock, while the nearby Lough Derg border tradition also links holy water with toothache relief. A Schools’ Collection account of the Sliabh Dubh holy well, just half a mile from the Donegal-Tyrone border, says its water was used for toothache, sore heads and sore legs, and that water taken away had to be corked with rushes from the well-side.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.duchas.ie/en/cbes/4428281/4391441" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: duchas.ie">[Dúchas]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">duchas.ie</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on duchas.ie.</span></span></span>

## St Patrick’s Well at Magherakeel

West Tyrone has another important holy well at Magherakeel near Killeter, commonly known as St Patrick’s Well or Tober Patrick. Discover Northern Ireland places it along the ancient pilgrimage trail to Lough Derg in County Donegal, while the Ireland’s Holy Wells County-by-County record identifies it as lying in Magherakeel townland, near Killeter village, in Aghyaran or Termonamongan parish.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://discovernorthernireland.com/listing/st-patrick%E2%80%99s-well/70024101/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: discovernorthernireland.com">[Discover Northern Ireland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">discovernorthernireland.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on discovernorthernireland.com.</span></span></span>

The Magherakeel record is valuable because it preserves local devotional context rather than only scenery. It says the parish has three holy wells: St Patrick’s Well at Magherakeel, St Davog’s in Croighdenis and Fr McLoughlin’s in Meenard, Slievedoo. It also records the belief that such wells were blessed by early saints and that St Patrick himself blessed this one after stopping to rest and drink on his way from Lough Derg.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ihwcbc.omeka.net/items/show/97" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ihwcbc.omeka.net">[ihwcbc.omeka.net]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ihwcbc.omeka.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">St Patrick&#x27;s Well. Unlike the more atmospheric Altadaven, Magherakeel is strongly tied to a calendar practice. The county-by-county well record states that on 17 March bands parade to the well before the rosary is recited at 3 pm, and that the date remains important in the parish calendar. It also records year-round visits by people seeking cures and holy water for the sick, with coins listed as modern offerings.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ihwcbc.omeka.net/items/show/97" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ihwcbc.omeka.net">[ihwcbc.omeka.net]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ihwcbc.omeka.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">St Patrick&#x27;s Well. (Tober Patrick) · Ireland&#x27;s Holy Wells County-by-County</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">St Patrick&#x27;s Well. (Tober Patrick) · Ireland&#x27;s Holy Wells County-by-County</span></span></span>

A local travel account adds a more specific curative claim: tradition says the water from St Patrick’s Well near Killeter can help ease toothache. This connects Magherakeel with the Garvagh bog stone and the Sliabh Dubh border well, making toothache one of the recurring ailment traditions in west Tyrone’s sacred-water landscape.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.theirishplace.com/featured/st-patricks-well-killeter/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theirishplace.com">[theirishplace.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theirishplace.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Irish Place St Patrick&#x27;s Well, Killeter</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Irish Place St Patrick&#x27;s Well, Killeter</span></span></span>

## Saint-Lore, Wishing and Older Ritual

Tyrone’s wells and healing stones are best read as layered places. The saint’s name gives a Christian frame: Patrick rests, drinks, blesses, baptises or overcomes hostile powers; Brigid’s name appears at Altadaven; local devotion gathers around feast days and prayers. But the actions around the places often look older or at least more folk-religious than formal church practice: water is carried away, rags or offerings are left, stones are touched, wishes are made, and cure-seekers focus on a bodily ailment.

This does not mean every Tyrone well is “pagan” in any simple sense. A safer reading is that Christian saint-lore and older habits of reverence for water, trees and stones became entangled over time. The National Museum of Ireland describes Irish holy wells as often being located beside trees where ribbons and rags are left by pilgrims, with blessed water believed to cure particular conditions in St Brigid traditions. That wider pattern helps make sense of Altadaven’s rag trees and healing basin without needing to force a single origin story onto the site.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.museum.ie/en-IE/News/St-Brigids-Day-a-weaving-tradition-of-Celtic-ritu" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: museum.ie">[National Museum of Ireland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">museum.ie</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Museum of Ireland St Brigid&#x27;s Day</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Museum of Ireland St Brigid&#x27;s Day</span></span></span>

The bullaun element is especially important. Bullaun stones are rock basins or hollowed stones that hold water, and Irish folklore often treats that water as curative, especially for warts. A 2020 study of wart cures in the Irish Schools’ Folklore Collection notes that medical folklore and curative holy wells were prominent in the Schools’ Scheme, and that the study examined 6,843 cures, including 749 wart cures, the most frequently recorded ailment in its sample.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.duchas.ie/assets/pdf/Snails-Water-Stone.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: duchas.ie">[Dúchas]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">duchas.ie</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on duchas.ie.</span></span></span>

That wider evidence makes Altadaven less isolated. Its wart-cure tradition is not an odd tourist embellishment, but part of a recognised Irish folk-medical pattern: warts, skin troubles, toothache and sore limbs are exactly the kinds of everyday complaints that people often brought to wells, stones and ritual water. At the same time, Tyrone’s evidence is more fragmentary than in many counties south of the border, because the Schools’ Collection was gathered through schools in the Irish Free State and was not extended to Northern Ireland.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.duchas.ie/assets/pdf/Snails-Water-Stone.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: duchas.ie">[Dúchas]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">duchas.ie</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on duchas.ie.</span></span></span>

## How Well Attested Are These Traditions?

The strongest Tyrone evidence comes in three different forms, and each has its own value. Altadaven is well attested as a modern public heritage site, with official tourism material, archaeological-style descriptions and repeated local retellings all agreeing on the main features: the chair, the water-filled rock depression, the healing association and the wishing tradition.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.visitmidulster.com/things-to-do/st-patricks-chair-and-well-p763941" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: visitmidulster.com">[Visit Mid Ulster]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">visitmidulster.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Visit Mid Ulster St Patrick’s Chair and Well</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Visit Mid Ulster St Patrick’s Chair and Well</span></span></span>

Magherakeel is best attested as a living devotional well. The Ireland’s Holy Wells County-by-County entry records its townland, parish, saint association, March 17 pattern, offerings and continued visits for holy water and cures. Discover Northern Ireland also presents it as a recognised attraction on the pilgrim route to Lough Derg, which shows how local devotion has become part of a wider visitor landscape.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ihwcbc.omeka.net/items/show/97" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ihwcbc.omeka.net">[ihwcbc.omeka.net]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ihwcbc.omeka.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">St Patrick&#x27;s Well. The Garvagh Pultons bog stone is different again. It survives in the record as a brief oral-tradition item, collected through the Dúchas Schools’ Collection material from the Donegal-Tyrone border. It gives fewer details than a modern heritage entry, but its plainness is part of its strength: a named townland, a distinctive stone, a specific ailment, and a remembered local practice.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.duchas.ie/en/cbes/4547216/4413794/4548054" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: duchas.ie">[Dúchas]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">duchas.ie</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on duchas.ie.</span></span></span>

What should be treated with caution are broad claims that a site is certainly pre-Christian, druidic or ancient in an unbroken line. Altadaven’s atmosphere and stone features make such interpretations tempting, and local retellings often use them. But the secure evidence is better described more carefully: the site has stone basins, saint-lore, healing traditions, wishing practices and rag-tree devotion, all of which are compatible with long layering rather than proof of one fixed origin.

## Why These Places Matter in Tyrone

Holy wells and healing stones make Tyrone’s folklore intimate. They are not mainly about heroic battles or grand supernatural beings, but about people living with pain, uncertainty and hope. A person with toothache, warts or a sick relative might go to a well, collect water, leave a coin, tie a rag or trust a stone hollow that neighbours remembered as powerful. The cure was social as well as spiritual: it depended on memory, repetition and local agreement that this place mattered.

They also show how Tyrone’s sacred geography spreads beyond obvious church sites. Altadaven is a wooded glen, Garvagh Pultons is a bog, and Magherakeel is a parish well on a pilgrim line towards Lough Derg. These are not interchangeable “mystic” places. Each has its own texture: Altadaven’s chair and bullaun, Garvagh’s goose-foot stone, Magherakeel’s St Patrick’s Day devotion and holy water.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.visitmidulster.com/things-to-do/st-patricks-chair-and-well-p763941" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: visitmidulster.com">[visitmidulster.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">visitmidulster.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Visit Mid Ulster St Patrick’s Chair and Well</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Visit Mid Ulster St Patrick’s Chair and Well</span></span></span>

For the wider Tyrone folklore map, these sites connect naturally with fairy bushes, saint roads, standing stones, old inauguration places and borderland pilgrimage. They show the same underlying rule found across much of the county’s tradition: do not dismiss small features of the landscape. A hollow in a rock, a ragged tree, a wet stone in a bog or a well beside a road may carry more memory than a passer-by expects.

## Retelling Tyrone’s Wells and Stones Today

Today, Tyrone’s holy wells and healing stones are retold in several registers at once. Tourism bodies present Altadaven and Magherakeel as quiet heritage or walking destinations. Local and parish traditions keep devotional meanings alive, especially around St Patrick’s Day at Magherakeel. Folklore archives preserve smaller accounts such as the Garvagh Pultons toothache stone, which might otherwise vanish from public memory.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.visitmidulster.com/things-to-do/st-patricks-chair-and-well-p763941" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: visitmidulster.com">[visitmidulster.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">visitmidulster.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Visit Mid Ulster St Patrick’s Chair and Well</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Visit Mid Ulster St Patrick’s Chair and Well</span></span></span>

The most responsible modern retelling keeps the wonder without overstating the proof. These places should not be reduced to quaint superstition, because they record how communities understood illness, blessing, landscape and inherited sacredness. But they should not be presented as confirmed supernatural or medical facts either. Their power as folklore lies in the meeting point: water that gathers in stone, saints who pass through wild ground, offerings left by ordinary people, and a county landscape where cure, memory and belief still cling to particular places.
