## Introduction

Robin Hood belongs to [Nottinghamshire]({{ 'where-nottinghamshire-turns-history-into/' | relative_url }}) not because one archive has solved the identity of a single historical outlaw, but because the county gives the legend its most persuasive stage: Nottingham, the Sheriff, Sherwood Forest, Edwinstowe and the ancient oaks. The earliest written Robin Hood material is late medieval and already knows the power of Nottingham as a place of danger, law and escape; later retellings tightened that connection until Sherwood became almost inseparable from the outlaw. What matters for folklore is the layering. Robin Hood is part ballad hero, part social protest figure, part forest outlaw, part civic emblem and part visitor-trail companion. Sherwood’s “outlaw landscape” is therefore not just a backdrop. It is the place where medieval forest law, royal hunting, roads, villages, old trees, parish churches and modern tourism make the story feel locally rooted, even while historians continue to debate how much of Robin was ever one real man.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://metseditions.org/read/600kMjRec8gzLTPqqt1eBvIvmvdWQYwN" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: metseditions.org">[metseditions.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">metseditions.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">METS Editions</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Introduction to Robin Hood and the MonkRobin Hood and the Monk is preserved in Cambridge University manuscript Ff.5.48. ## Why the legend feels rooted in Nottinghamshire

Robin Hood’s Nottinghamshire identity rests on a triangle: the outlaw in the greenwood, the Sheriff of Nottingham as his enemy, and Sherwood Forest as a landscape where law could be dodged, mocked or overturned. The oldest surviving stories do not give the neat modern version of “steal from the rich and give to the poor” in every detail, but they do establish the dramatic ingredients that later audiences recognised: archery, loyalty, disguise, ambush, religious devotion, suspicion of corrupt authority and repeated conflict with Nottingham’s officers.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robin_Hood" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Robin Hood</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Robin Hood</span></span></span>

The famous “real Robin Hood” question is tempting, but it can easily mislead. Medieval records contain names resembling Robin Hood or Robert Hood, and scholars have proposed candidates from several counties; that does not turn the legend into a solved biography. The more useful Nottinghamshire question is why this county became the story’s strongest imaginative home. The answer is that Nottinghamshire offered both a named urban authority and a forest jurisdiction. Nottingham supplied the Sheriff, prison, market and castle-world of official power; Sherwood supplied the green, road-crossed, semi-wild space where an outlaw could plausibly vanish.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robin_Hood" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[wikipedia.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Robin Hood</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Robin Hood</span></span></span>

That distinction matters for a folklore page. Robin Hood is not a saint’s life, a documented criminal file or a simple local ghost story. He is a long-lived narrative tradition that has been repeatedly adapted to suit the concerns of different audiences: late medieval singers, Tudor May games, antiquarians, children’s writers, civic boosters, film-makers, conservation bodies and tourists. Nottinghamshire’s claim is strongest when treated as a landscape claim rather than a courtroom claim.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://uol.de/en/einblicke/22/robin-hood-vom-wegelagerer-zum-nationalhelden" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: uol.de">[Carl von Ossietzky University Oldenburg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">uol.de</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Carl von Ossietzky University Oldenburg Robin Hood</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Carl von Ossietzky University Oldenburg Robin Hood</span></span></span>

## Ballads, outlaws and the Sheriff

The Robin Hood tradition was already known before the surviving narrative ballads were written down. William Langland’s *Piers Plowman*, usually dated to the later 14th century, contains the famous allusion to “rhymes of Robin Hood”, suggesting that audiences already recognised such songs before the fuller written texts that survive today. Visit Nottinghamshire’s public chronology treats the *Piers Plowman* reference as the earliest sign of the existing tales, while scholarly summaries likewise place Robin among well-known late medieval popular story material rather than as a character invented by a single author.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.visit-nottinghamshire.co.uk/ideas-and-inspiration/robin-hood/the-real-robin-hood" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: visit-nottinghamshire.co.uk">[Visit Nottinghamshire]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">visit-nottinghamshire.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">the real robin hood</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">the real robin hood</span></span></span>

One of the key early pieces for Nottinghamshire is *Robin Hood and the Monk*. Preserved in Cambridge University Library manuscript Ff.5.48, it is usually dated to the second half of the 15th century and was first printed under its modern title by Robert Jamieson in 1806. Its plot is not a gentle children’s tale: Robin goes to Nottingham, is recognised by a monk, captured by the Sheriff, imprisoned, and then rescued through the violent loyalty of Little John and Much the Miller’s Son. Nottingham is not ornamental here. It is the dangerous place where Robin’s forest freedom meets the machinery of arrest and confinement.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://metseditions.org/read/600kMjRec8gzLTPqqt1eBvIvmvdWQYwN" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: metseditions.org">[METS Editions]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">metseditions.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Introduction to Robin Hood and the MonkRobin Hood and the Monk is preserved in Cambridge University manuscript Ff.5.48. The manuscript is...</span></span></span>

*A Gest of Robyn Hode*, surviving in early printed form around 1500 and drawing together older story material, also helps explain why the Sheriff became so central. The Gest brings together Robin, Little John, a distressed knight, church wealth, royal authority and the Sheriff of Nottingham in a sequence of linked adventures. Modern editions note that the Sheriff is Robin’s traditional adversary, even when the geography of different early tales is not always tidy. That untidiness is important: Robin Hood’s early story-world is not a modern map with fixed pins, but a body of ballads and episodes that were combined, reworked and localised over time.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://metseditions.org/read/976AKyAFRDDQFWvztV5Meuxbpld4M6V" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: metseditions.org">[METS Editions]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">metseditions.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on metseditions.org.</span></span></span>

The Sheriff is powerful because he is both a person and a system. In the stories he can be fooled, tricked, humiliated or defeated, but he also stands for courts, prisons, taxation, property, rank and the right to punish. The National Archives’ recent discussion of Robin Hood places the legend against medieval records of outlawry and ordinary people’s experience of law, while making clear that the legend has travelled through folk songs, tavern stories, pageants and royal festivals rather than through a single official case file.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cdn.nationalarchives.gov.uk/documents/robin-hood-show-notes.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cdn.nationalarchives.gov.uk">[cdn.nationalarchives.gov.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cdn.nationalarchives.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">robin hood show notes</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">robin hood show notes</span></span></span>

A useful reader’s rule is this: the earlier the Robin Hood material, the less it resembles a complete modern franchise. Maid Marian, the noble birth story, King Richard, Prince John, the full “rob the rich” slogan and the tidy band of named Merry Men were strengthened, rearranged or popularised across later centuries. Yet the Nottinghamshire core of outlaw versus Sheriff, forest versus authority, and cunning versus official force is already old enough to explain why the county’s identity became so closely tied to him.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maid_Marian" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[wikipedia.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Maid Marian</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Maid Marian</span></span></span>

## Sherwood as a working royal forest

Modern readers often picture Sherwood as a continuous deep wood, but medieval “forest” did not simply mean woodland. It meant a royal legal jurisdiction, especially concerned with hunting rights, deer and the protection of the king’s interests. Nottinghamshire County Council explains that Sherwood became a royal hunting forest after the Norman Conquest, and that in the 1200s — the period popularly associated with Robin Hood — it covered about 100,000 acres, around a fifth of Nottinghamshire.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nottinghamshire.gov.uk/culture-leisure/country-parks/history-of-sherwood-forest-robin-hood-and-major-oak" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nottinghamshire.gov.uk">[Nottinghamshire County Council]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nottinghamshire.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">history of sherwood forest robin hood and major oak</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Nottinghamshire County CouncilHistory of Sherwood Forest, Robin Hood and Major OakIn the 1200s, popularly thought to be the time of Robin...</span></span></span>

That legal meaning gives the Robin Hood story much of its bite. A royal forest was not empty wilderness. It could include woodland, heath, grassland, settlements, fields, parks, roads and people trying to make a living under rules that restricted hunting and the use of resources. The Nottinghamshire Heritage Gateway stresses that Sherwood contained sandy heath, woodland, deer parks, Mansfield, Nottingham and almost fifty settlements, while also noting the special forest officers and jurisdiction that governed it.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nottsheritagegateway.org.uk/places/sherwoodforest.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nottsheritagegateway.org.uk">[Notts Heritage Gateway]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nottsheritagegateway.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nottsheritagegateway.org.uk.</span></span></span>

Sherwood’s ecology also matters. The forest was not only a stage for chase scenes; it was a particular Nottinghamshire landscape of ancient oaks, birch, heathland, poor sandy soils and managed resources. Visit Sherwood describes the name as first recorded in the 10th century as “Sciryuda”, meaning the wood belonging to the shire, and presents the present reserve as a remnant of a much larger historic forest. This makes the legend feel less like a floating medieval romance and more like a story attached to a recognisable Midlands environment.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://visitsherwood.co.uk/about-sherwood-forest/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: visitsherwood.co.uk">[Sherwood Forest - Where legends grow]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">visitsherwood.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Sherwood Forest</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Sherwood Forest</span></span></span>

The Great North Way is another reason Sherwood works as outlaw country. Nottinghamshire County Council notes that the main London to York road ran through Sherwood, bringing travellers into a landscape where robbers and officials could both plausibly appear. Folklore often thrives on thresholds and routes: crossroads, old roads, river crossings, parish edges and forest paths. Sherwood’s outlaw landscape is exactly that kind of threshold, where settled authority and risky movement meet.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nottinghamshire.gov.uk/culture-leisure/country-parks/history-of-sherwood-forest-robin-hood-and-major-oak" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nottinghamshire.gov.uk">[Nottinghamshire County Council]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nottinghamshire.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">history of sherwood forest robin hood and major oak</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Nottinghamshire County CouncilHistory of Sherwood Forest, Robin Hood and Major OakIn the 1200s, popularly thought to be the time of Robin...</span></span></span>

There is a useful correction here to a common modern assumption. Stephen Knight’s study of Robin Hood and the forest laws argues that the now-familiar opening scene of a brutal authority punishing a poor peasant for breaking forest law is much more central to modern Robin Hood novels and films than to the earliest Robin Hood tradition. In other words, forest law is historically real and highly relevant to Sherwood, but the exact “oppressed peasant rescued for killing the king’s deer” template is a later storytelling emphasis rather than the automatic starting point of the oldest ballads.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://openjournals.bsu.edu/biarhs/article/download/2837/1693/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: openjournals.bsu.edu">[openjournals.bsu.edu]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">openjournals.bsu.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on bsu.edu.</span></span></span>

## Major Oak, Edwinstowe and modern memory

For many visitors, the Robin Hood landscape begins at the Major Oak. The great tree stands in Sherwood Forest near Edwinstowe and has long been promoted as the oak where Robin and his outlaws hid from the Sheriff. Visit Sherwood treats this carefully as legend, not proof, while still recognising the tree as one of the forest’s most powerful story anchors. The point is not that a document proves Robin used the hollow trunk. The point is that the tree has become a physical meeting place between folklore, childhood imagination, ancient woodland and Nottinghamshire identity.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://visitsherwood.co.uk/explore-the-forest/the-major-oak/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: visitsherwood.co.uk">[Sherwood Forest - Where legends grow]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">visitsherwood.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Sherwood Forest</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Sherwood Forest</span></span></span>

The Major Oak also shows how folklore can attach itself to living nature, and how that attachment changes when the living object changes. In June 2026, the RSPB confirmed that the Major Oak had died after failing to produce leaves, with reports linking its decline to age, heat, drought, soil compaction from visitors and older conservation interventions. The tree is expected to remain standing and to continue supporting wildlife as deadwood, which means its cultural role has not simply ended; it has shifted from living giant to memorial presence.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.theguardian.com/environment/2026/jun/18/most-famous-tree-world-sherwood-forest-ancient-major-oak-dies" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theguardian.com">[The Guardian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theguardian.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Visited by around 350,000 people annually, the Major Oak became a symbol of natural heritage, named after Maj Hayman Rooke in 1790. Edwinstowe adds another layer. St Mary’s Church is widely visited because legend says Robin Hood and Maid Marian were married there. The church itself dates from around 1175 and remains an active place of worship, but the marriage story belongs to later Robin Hood tradition rather than to the earliest ballads, where Marian is absent. That contrast is exactly what makes Edwinstowe interesting: it shows how a real medieval church can gather a later romantic legend around itself and become part of the county’s tourist and emotional map.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.visit-nottinghamshire.co.uk/things-to-do/st-marys-church-edwinstowe-p585761" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: visit-nottinghamshire.co.uk">[visit-nottinghamshire.co.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">visit-nottinghamshire.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on visit-nottinghamshire.co.uk.</span></span></span>

Nottingham city supplies the civic face of the same memory. The Robin Hood statue at Castle Place, unveiled in 1952 and designed by James Arthur Woodford, is now Grade II listed. Historic England gives its reasons as both artistic and historic: the sculpture is a skilled depiction of legendary figures and has a strong association with Nottingham Castle, as well as with the city’s 1949 quincentenary celebrations. The statue turns a medieval outlaw into a modern civic emblem, placed where visitors can photograph the legend in the shadow of Nottingham’s castle story.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://historicengland.org.uk/listing/the-list/list-entry/1454561" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: historicengland.org.uk">[Historic England]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">historicengland.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">list entry</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">list entry</span></span></span>

These places do different kinds of folklore work. The Major Oak offers the “hideout” image; Edwinstowe offers romance and parish memory; Nottingham Castle offers authority, imprisonment and civic theatre; Sherwood’s trails offer bodily movement through a legendary landscape. Together they allow modern visitors to experience Robin Hood not as a single text, but as a circuit of places.

## What is old, what is later, and what is local pride?

The strongest way to understand Robin Hood in Nottinghamshire is to separate layers without stripping away the pleasure of the story.

**Old ballad layer:** Robin is a late medieval outlaw of the greenwood, already associated with archery, fellowship, violence, religious observance, hostility to corrupt authority and the Sheriff of Nottingham. *Robin Hood and the Monk* and *A Gest of Robyn Hode* are crucial here because they show that Nottingham and the Sheriff are not merely Victorian decorations.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://metseditions.org/read/600kMjRec8gzLTPqqt1eBvIvmvdWQYwN" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: metseditions.org">[METS Editions]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">metseditions.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Introduction to Robin Hood and the MonkRobin Hood and the Monk is preserved in Cambridge University manuscript Ff.5.48. **Romantic and festive layer:** Maid Marian’s role is later than many readers expect. She is not part of the earliest surviving Robin Hood ballads and seems to have entered through May-game and festive traditions before becoming Robin’s standard beloved. That does not make her “fake” in folklore terms; it means she belongs to a different stage in the legend’s growth.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maid_Marian" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Maid Marian</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Maid Marian</span></span></span>

In fact, Robin Hood’s power comes from his ability to absorb new concerns while keeping a recognisable Nottinghamshire shape: greenwood freedom, sharp arrows, loyal companions, corrupt authority and the hope that law is not always the same thing as justice.

## Why Robin Hood still defines Nottinghamshire folklore

Many counties have legendary figures, haunted roads, [holy wells]({{ 'holy-wells-e8a1cf/' | relative_url }}), black dogs, witches or giants. Nottinghamshire has those smaller traditions too, but Robin Hood dominates because he joins local place to national myth. He can be read as a medieval outlaw, a comic trickster, a yeoman hero, a proto-social rebel, a children’s champion, a tourism brand, a conservation symbol and a civic mascot. Few county legends can carry that many meanings without losing their local address.

The landscape is the reason he remains anchored. Without Sherwood, Robin Hood risks becoming a generic archer in green. Without Nottingham, the Sheriff has no civic weight. Without Edwinstowe and the Major Oak, the legend loses the walkable, touchable quality that lets families, schoolchildren and visitors imagine themselves inside the story. Nottinghamshire’s Robin Hood tradition survives because it is not only told; it is mapped.

The death of the Major Oak in 2026 underlines that this mapped folklore is also fragile. Ancient trees, old paths, village churches and civic monuments do not merely decorate the legend; they carry it. When they are damaged, restored, reinterpreted or mourned, the story changes with them. Sherwood’s outlaw landscape therefore matters not just as a medieval memory, but as a living example of how folklore attaches to real places and asks each generation what it wants to protect.
