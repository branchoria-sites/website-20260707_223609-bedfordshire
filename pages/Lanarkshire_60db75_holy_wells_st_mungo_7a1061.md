## Introduction

[Lanarkshire]({{ 'what-makes-lanarkshires-folklore-so/' | relative_url }})’s holy wells matter because they make the county’s sacred history visible at ground level: in a cathedral crypt, beside old roads, in town names, and in healing stories remembered long after formal pilgrimage was discouraged. The strongest example is St Mungo’s Well in Glasgow Cathedral, where the city’s patron saint, also known as St Kentigern, is tied to burial, baptism, pilgrimage and Glasgow’s origin story. Around it sit other Lanarkshire water traditions: Glasgow’s Lady Well by the Necropolis, Motherwell’s lost Lady Well or Mother Well, and St Patrick’s Well at Stonehouse, remembered for cures of tuberculosis and skin disease. These are not just picturesque survivals. They show how medieval devotion, local medicine, Marian memory, Reformation suspicion and modern heritage all meet in the old county of Lanarkshire.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.historicenvironment.scot/visit/all/glasgow-cathedral/history-and-stories/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: historicenvironment.scot">[historicenvironment.scot]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">historicenvironment.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">This inspiring edifice dates mostly from the 1200s. ## St Mungo’s Well and Glasgow’s Origin Story

St Mungo’s Well is important because it sits at the point where Lanarkshire folklore becomes city history. Glasgow Cathedral is dedicated to St Kentigern, better known as St Mungo, and Historic Environment Scotland describes him as the first bishop of the area that became modern Strathclyde. He is thought to have been buried on the cathedral site around 612, and the medieval building that now stands there dates largely from the 1200s. The Lower Church was built to house his tomb, and his shrine drew pilgrims from across medieval Scotland.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.historicenvironment.scot/visit/all/glasgow-cathedral/history-and-stories/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: historicenvironment.scot">[Historic Environment Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">historicenvironment.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">This inspiring edifice dates mostly from the 1200s. It was dedicated to St Kentigern, also known as St Mungo. Kentigern is believed...Re...</span></span></span>

That makes the well more than an architectural curiosity. In tradition and modern interpretation, it belongs to the same sacred cluster as the saint’s tomb, the lower church, the old pilgrimage route, and the Molendinar Burn, the stream associated with early Glasgow. The current heritage project for the well describes it as hidden in the crypt and built into the cathedral’s buttress walls when the lower church was expanded as a major pilgrimage centre in the mid-13th century.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.aproxima.co.uk/the-well-overview" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: aproxima.co.uk">[Aproxima]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">aproxima.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Aproxima The Well</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Aproxima The Well</span></span></span>

The exact early history of the well is harder to prove than the later importance of the shrine. Modern interpretation sometimes presents the spring as a very ancient sacred water source, while a more cautious historical reading notes that the surviving medieval life of St Kentigern does not give the well a clear miracle-story of its own. That silence matters. It suggests that the well’s power in local memory may come less from a single famous legend and more from its position: beside the saint’s shrine, inside the cathedral’s most sacred space, and within the landscape where Glasgow imagined its beginnings.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.aproxima.co.uk/the-well-overview-1" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: aproxima.co.uk">[Aproxima]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">aproxima.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on aproxima.co.uk.</span></span></span>

For folklore, that is often how place works. A well does not need a neat tale with a dragon, curse or named apparition to become meaningful. St Mungo’s Well gathers force from association. It is close to the tomb of the city’s saint, set below the cathedral rather than out in an open field, and remembered as part of the deep religious landscape of old Glasgow. It turns a foundation story into something physical: stone, water, darkness, pilgrimage and the idea that the city began around a holy presence.

## Why the Shrine, Well and Crypt Still Feel Powerful

The atmosphere of St Mungo’s Well comes from the way medieval Glasgow layered holiness into one site. The cathedral was not simply a parish church in later terms; it was a pilgrimage destination focused on the saint’s tomb. The cathedral’s own heritage account says the site contained the tomb of the 6th-century missionary to Strathclyde and was understood as holy because it gave access to the special grace of a saint close to God.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://glasgowcathedral.org/heritage-and-journey" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: glasgowcathedral.org">[glasgowcathedral.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">glasgowcathedral.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on glasgowcathedral.org.</span></span></span>

This helps explain why a well in the crypt could attract such strong modern interest even when its earliest ritual use is uncertain. Water, relics, burial and saintly presence are powerful ingredients in medieval devotion. A spring could be used practically, ritually, medicinally or symbolically; the evidence does not always let us separate those uses cleanly. The most careful recent interpretation of St Mungo’s Well admits several possibilities: it may have been connected with the saint’s cult, used for baptism or washing, used near the shrine or a neighbouring altar, or simply preserved because it was already important when the cathedral was built around it.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.aproxima.co.uk/the-well-overview-1" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: aproxima.co.uk">[Aproxima]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">aproxima.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on aproxima.co.uk.</span></span></span>

The well’s recent restoration also shows how old sacred places are reinterpreted rather than merely preserved. Aproxima’s Glasgow Wellspring project says the well was “reawakened” in October 2025 for Glasgow’s 850th anniversary, after being largely forgotten and unstudied for more than 70 years. Glasgow City Heritage Trust’s exhibition account describes a new mosaic artwork in the ancient well and notes that excavation brought up objects such as dice, rings, glasses and coins from around the world.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.aproxima.co.uk/the-well" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: aproxima.co.uk">[Aproxima]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">aproxima.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Aproxima The Well &#124; Explore, Support, Connect Today</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Aproxima The Well &#124; Explore, Support, Connect Today</span></span></span>

Those finds are revealing. Coins and personal objects are exactly the sort of things that make a well feel like a living tradition rather than a sealed monument. Some may be casual tourist deposits, some devotional, some playful, some merely accidental. Together, they show that people continued to treat the well as a place where water, memory and offering belonged together. The old saint’s well has become a modern heritage site, but it has not become emotionally neutral.

## Lady Well, Motherwell and Marian Memory

Lanarkshire’s holy wells are not all attached to St Mungo. The county also preserves a strong Marian thread through wells dedicated to Our Lady or known as Lady Wells. These are especially important because they connect sacred water with place-names and everyday geography rather than only with cathedral pilgrimage.

Glasgow’s Lady Well, near Ladywell Street and the Necropolis, is a good example of how a holy well could move between sacred, civic and practical meanings. The Trove Scotland record describes it as an ancient holy well dedicated to the Virgin Mary and says it was probably one of Glasgow’s sixteen public wells in 1736. It once flowed from the east bank of the Merchants’ Park, but when that land was converted to the Necropolis the water became polluted and the well was closed. A niche was built in 1836 to mark the place, then rebuilt in 1874.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.trove.scot/place/45037" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: trove.scot">[Trove Scot]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">trove.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Glasgow, Ladywell Street, Lady WellThe Merchants&#x27; House, in 1836, built a niche on the spot to mark the site of its exit from the brae. This history is telling because it shows a holy well surviving not through uninterrupted worship, but through civic memory. The dedication to Mary remained attached to the place; the water supply became part of Glasgow’s public infrastructure; then urban change, burial-ground development and pollution altered the well’s use. The sacred landmark did not vanish entirely. It became a marked remnant in the cityscape.

Motherwell carries an even stronger place-name memory. The University of Glasgow’s Saints in Scottish Place-Names database records Lady Well, also known as Mother Well, in the parish of Dalziel, and links it as the source of the settlement name Motherwell, while noting that the association is treated as hypothetical.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://saintsplaces.gla.ac.uk/place.php?id=1334737451" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: saintsplaces.gla.ac.uk">[Saints in Scottish Place-Names]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">saintsplaces.gla.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Saints in Scottish Place-Names Lady Well, aka Mother Well, Dalziel Mother Well</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Relationships with other place names. That cautious wording is useful. It avoids pretending that every local etymology is beyond dispute, while still showing why the tradition matters. In popular Lanarkshire memory, Motherwell’s name is bound to a Marian well; in scholarly place-name recording, the connection is important enough to preserve but not stated with false certainty. Folklore often lives in that space between proof and attachment. The well may be gone, but the name keeps its sacred echo in daily use.

## St Patrick’s Well and Local Healing Traditions

St Patrick’s Well at Stonehouse brings the holy-well tradition closer to folk medicine. Stonehouse local history places the well on the banks of the Avon on the lands of Patrickholm and says it was known for healing properties, especially for tuberculosis and skin diseases. The same local source notes the striking coincidence that Stonehouse Hospital, founded in 1896, was built to care for sufferers from tuberculosis and related diseases.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.stonehouseonline.org.uk/holywells.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: stonehouseonline.org.uk">[stonehouseonline.org.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">stonehouseonline.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Holy WellsSituated on the banks of the Avon on the lands of Patrickholm lies St.Patrick&#x27;s well, known for its healing properties in curin...</span></span></span>

This does not mean the well medically cured tuberculosis. It means the community remembered the spring through a healing story, and that story became more memorable because later medical history seemed to rhyme with it. A sacred spring, a disease of breath and wasting, and a hospital built nearby form the kind of pattern local tradition notices and retells.

Lanarkshire’s broader pattern supports this reading. A modern holy-wells list for the historic county records multiple saintly dedications, including St Bride’s Well at Walston, St Catherine’s wells at Dalziel and Shotts, St Ninian’s wells at Lamington and Stonehouse, Our Lady’s Well at Nemphlar, St Oswald’s Well at Sandford, several St Patrick’s wells, and a destroyed St Mungo’s Well at Lanark. Some are extant, some lost or destroyed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://britishholywells.co.uk/scotland/lanarkshire/lanarkshirelist.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: britishholywells.co.uk">[britishholywells.co.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">britishholywells.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on britishholywells.co.uk.</span></span></span>

The pattern matters more than any one minor entry. Lanarkshire’s sacred-water map is not a single isolated curiosity in Glasgow Cathedral. It is a scatter of named springs across the Clyde valley and its towns, each carrying a different degree of survival: a visible wellhead, a place-name, a local cure, an antiquarian note, a parish memory, or a destroyed site preserved only in lists.

## What Reformation Suspicion Changed

Holy wells became difficult places after the Reformation because they sat too close to older Catholic habits of pilgrimage, saintly intercession and local ritual. In Scotland, official Protestant suspicion did not always erase the wells themselves, but it changed how people could speak about them and how openly they could be used.

A detailed modern discussion of St Kentigern’s Well notes that pilgrimage to “chapels, wells, crosses and such other monuments of idolatry” was treated as superstition and associated with Catholic practice. It also points to repeated official attempts to suppress such practices, including parliamentary action in 1581, Privy Council action in 1629, and later anti-Catholic legislation in 1700.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.aproxima.co.uk/the-well-overview-1" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: aproxima.co.uk">[Aproxima]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">aproxima.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on aproxima.co.uk.</span></span></span>

Older antiquarian writing about St Mungo’s Well preserves the same tension in sharper language. A collected account describes the well as a place of pilgrimage and says it was called an “idolatrous well” in 1614; it also quotes later action against people resorting to chapels and wells. The language is hostile, but that hostility is itself evidence that people still cared about such places. Authorities do not usually legislate against dead customs.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.thenorthernantiquarian.org/2014/06/28/st-mungos-well-glasgowc/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: thenorthernantiquarian.org">[thenorthernantiquarian.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">thenorthernantiquarian.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">St. Mungo&#x27;s Well, Glasgow Cathedral, Lanarkshire</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">St. Mungo&#x27;s Well, Glasgow Cathedral, Lanarkshire</span></span></span>

For Lanarkshire folklore, this is one of the most important interpretive points. Reformation suspicion did not simply turn sacred wells into ruins overnight. It pushed them into mixed categories: suspicious to church authorities, useful as water sources, cherished in local memory, occasionally visited in private, and later revalued by antiquarians, local historians and heritage bodies. That is why the same well can appear in records as holy, idolatrous, public, polluted, restored, lost, or picturesque.

## Place-Names Keep What Buildings Lose

Many holy wells survive best in names. This is especially true in a county like Lanarkshire, where urban growth, industrial development, roads, burial grounds and modern water systems changed the physical landscape dramatically. A spring could be capped, diverted, polluted or destroyed, while the name stayed behind on a street, district, town or local memory.

Saints in Scottish Place-Names is especially valuable here because it treats saintly names as evidence in the landscape rather than as decorative labels. The wider project describes a map-based database of thousands of Scottish places and place-names connected with around 750 saints.

In Lanarkshire, that approach helps explain why wells belong in folklore as well as religious history. A name such as Ladywell does not only mark where water once flowed. It keeps the idea of a Marian landscape alive in a modern city. Motherwell does something similar on a larger scale, turning a local sacred-water memory into the name of a major Lanarkshire town.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.trove.scot/place/45037" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: trove.scot">[Trove Scot]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">trove.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Glasgow, Ladywell Street, Lady WellThe Merchants&#x27; House, in 1836, built a niche on the spot to mark the site of its exit from the brae. St Mungo’s Well and Glasgow’s Lady Well therefore sit naturally beside Motherwell, Stonehouse, Lanark and other Clydesdale sites when the subject is historic Lanarkshire folklore rather than present-day local authority boundaries.

## How Old Are These Traditions?

The answer varies from well to well. St Mungo’s shrine is very well attested as a medieval pilgrimage focus, and the cathedral’s Lower Church is securely tied to the saint’s tomb in official heritage interpretation. St Mungo’s Well itself is physically embedded in that medieval sacred space, but its earliest ritual story is less clearly documented than the tomb and shrine.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.historicenvironment.scot/visit/all/glasgow-cathedral/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: historicenvironment.scot">[Historic Environment Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">historicenvironment.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Historic Environment ScotlandGlasgow CathedralIt houses the tomb of St Mungo, the city&#x27;s founder, whose shrine once drew pilgrims from ac...</span></span></span>

## Why Holy Wells Still Matter in Lanarkshire

Lanarkshire’s holy wells still matter because they show how belief attaches to ordinary landscape features. A spring is practical before it is symbolic: people need water. But in these stories, water also becomes a threshold between health and sickness, town and countryside, saint and community, medieval devotion and modern memory.

St Mungo’s Well gives Glasgow a sacred origin point that can still be visited in the cathedral. Glasgow’s Lady Well shows how a Marian spring could survive as a marked urban remnant after the old water use had ended. Motherwell’s Lady Well shows how a lost or uncertain site can still shape identity through a town name. Stonehouse’s St Patrick’s Well shows how healing traditions can remain meaningful even when modern readers do not treat them as medical fact.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.aproxima.co.uk/the-well-overview" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: aproxima.co.uk">[aproxima.co.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">aproxima.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Aproxima The Well</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Aproxima The Well</span></span></span>

They also help balance the county’s folklore. Lanarkshire is often remembered through Glasgow’s urban growth, Clydeside industry, Covenanting history, witch records, old burgh customs and river-valley settlement. The wells add a quieter layer: sacred water under stone, a saint’s tomb below a cathedral, a Marian name on a modern street, a healing spring beside the Avon.

That is why these wells deserve more than a footnote. They are small places carrying large memories. In them, Lanarkshire’s folklore is not a fantasy world separate from history, but a way of reading the county’s real ground: where people drank, prayed, asked for healing, feared official disapproval, renamed places, and kept old associations alive long after the original rituals faded.
