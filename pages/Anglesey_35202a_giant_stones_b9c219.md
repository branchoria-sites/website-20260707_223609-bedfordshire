## Introduction

[Anglesey]({{ 'why-anglesey-feels-so-full-of-legends/' | relative_url }})’s giant stories often begin with something very solid: a mound, a chamber, a line of stones, or a dark passage that looks too old and too difficult to explain by ordinary means. The clearest example is Barclodiad y Gawres, the great Neolithic tomb above Porth Trecastell on the island’s south-west coast, whose name is usually rendered as “the Giantess’s Apronful”. In local legend, a giantess drops a load of stones from her apron, leaving the monument behind. Archaeology tells a different story: this is a late Neolithic passage tomb, built thousands of years before written history, with rare carved stones inside. The folklore matters because it shows how later people made sense of prehistoric remains: not by dating them, but by turning them into memorable acts of giant labour, accident and wonder.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cadw.gov.wales/visit/places-to-visit/barclodiad-y-gawres-chambered-tomb" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cadw.gov.wales">[Cadw]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cadw.gov.wales</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Cadw Barclodiad y Gawres Chambered Tomb &#124; Cadw</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Barclodiad y Gawres Chambered Tomb &#124; Cadw - gov.walesBarclodiad y Gawres (&#x27;The Giantess&#x27;s Apronful&#x27; in English) Among a series of sto...</span></span></span>

Bryn Celli Ddu, near Llanddaniel Fab, adds a second layer to the same Anglesey pattern. It is not chiefly known for a giantess story, but for the midsummer sunrise that sends light along its passage into the chamber. Together, Barclodiad y Gawres and Bryn Celli Ddu show why Anglesey’s ancient tombs became such powerful anchors for legend: they are visible, strange, carefully placed and still capable of producing awe in the present day.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cadw.gov.wales/visit/places-to-visit/bryn-celli-ddu-chambered-tomb" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cadw.gov.wales">[Cadw]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cadw.gov.wales</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Cadw Bryn Celli Ddu Chambered Tomb</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Bryn Celli Ddu Chambered Tomb - Cadw - gov.walesAs the sun rises on the summer solstice (the longest day of the year) shafts of light...</span></span></span>

## Barclodiad y Gawres and the giantess’s apron

Barclodiad y Gawres stands on a cliffside position above Porth Trecastell, also known as Cable Bay, between Rhosneigr and Aberffraw. Cadw describes it as a reconstructed earthen mound about 27 metres across, covering a passage about 7 metres long that leads into a cross-shaped chamber. Inside are carved stones with zig-zags, spirals and other designs, among the rarest examples of Neolithic art in Britain.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cadw.gov.wales/visit/places-to-visit/barclodiad-y-gawres-chambered-tomb" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cadw.gov.wales">[Cadw]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cadw.gov.wales</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Cadw Barclodiad y Gawres Chambered Tomb &#124; Cadw</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Barclodiad y Gawres Chambered Tomb &#124; Cadw - gov.walesBarclodiad y Gawres (&#x27;The Giantess&#x27;s Apronful&#x27; in English) Among a series of sto...</span></span></span>

The name is the folklore hook. “The Giantess’s Apronful” is not a technical archaeological description; it is a story-name. In one modern heritage retelling, a giant couple are carrying rocks north to build a new home. They ask a cobbler how far they are from Anglesey, not realising they have already arrived. Frightened, he tells them they are still miles away. The giantess, discouraged, drops the stones from her apron, and the tomb is left in the landscape.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.landoflegends.wales/location/barclodiad-y-gawres" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: landoflegends.wales">[Land of Legends]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">landoflegends.wales</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Land of Legends Barclodiad y Gawres Its name</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Land of LegendsBarclodiad y GawresIts name - Barclodiad y Gawres (&#x27;The Giantess&#x27; Apron&#x27;) - relates to a local story about a giant couple...</span></span></span>

That tale does not preserve a literal memory of Neolithic construction. It does something more typical of folklore: it gives an old, impressive, half-understood feature a humanly graspable cause. Why is there a great mound and a mass of stone above the bay? Because a giant woman dropped them. Why are the stones so large? Because they belonged to giant work. Why does the place feel older than ordinary memory? Because the tale moves it into a time when giant beings shaped the island.

The story also has a comic local cleverness. The cobbler survives not by strength but by quick speech. This is a common pattern in giant folklore: the giant explains the scale of the landscape, while the smaller human explains the triumph of wit. On Anglesey, that gives Barclodiad y Gawres a double identity. It is both a serious prehistoric tomb and a place where local storytelling domesticates the impossible weight of antiquity.

## What archaeology adds to the story

The archaeological evidence makes the folklore more interesting, not less. Barclodiad y Gawres is not just a pile of stones. It is a decorated cruciform passage grave, a type much more strongly associated with Ireland than with Wales. Visit Wales notes that its form is unusual in Anglesey because tombs of this kind are usually found in Ireland, while Cadw points out that comparable Neolithic carvings occur in Ireland and that the only other UK tomb with such megalithic art nearby is Bryn Celli Ddu.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.visitwales.com/attraction/historic-site/barclodiad-y-gawres-chambered-tomb-cadw-528051" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: visitwales.com">[Visit Wales]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">visitwales.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on visitwales.com.</span></span></span>

This Irish Sea connection matters for Anglesey folklore because it places the island in a wider western seaway world. The giantess tale is local, but the monument itself belongs to a much older network of building traditions, seaborne contact and shared symbolic forms. A reader standing at Barclodiad y Gawres is therefore looking at two kinds of inheritance at once: a Neolithic monument connected to Atlantic passage-tomb culture, and a later Welsh place-story that explains the stones through a giant woman’s dropped load.

Excavations in the twentieth century revealed carved stones with spirals, zig-zags, lozenges and chevrons. Visit Anglesey records that five carved stones were found during excavation and that a sixth carved stone was discovered in 2001. Cadw’s visitor information emphasises how rare these decorated stones are in a British context.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.visitanglesey.co.uk/en-gb/explore/barclodiad-y-gawres" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: visitanglesey.co.uk">[Visit Anglesey]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">visitanglesey.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on visitanglesey.co.uk.</span></span></span>

The tomb’s modern appearance also needs care. The mound visitors see today is a reconstruction, built to protect and present the monument after excavation. That does not make the place artificial in any simple sense: the ancient passage and decorated stones are real, but the neat mounded form is partly a heritage restoration. Folklore often thrives in just this sort of place, where ancient remains, later damage, excavation, reconstruction and visitor storytelling all overlap.

## Bryn Celli Ddu and the midsummer sun

Bryn Celli Ddu offers a different route from prehistoric tomb to modern wonder. Its name is commonly translated as “the Mound in the Dark Grove”. Cadw describes it as one of Anglesey’s most famous prehistoric landmarks and “two sites in one”: an earlier henge monument was later replaced by a chambered tomb beneath a mound. The tomb has a narrow passage leading into an octagonal chamber, where human bones, arrowheads and carved stones have been found.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cadw.gov.wales/visit/places-to-visit/bryn-celli-ddu-chambered-tomb" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cadw.gov.wales">[Cadw]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cadw.gov.wales</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Cadw Bryn Celli Ddu Chambered Tomb</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Bryn Celli Ddu Chambered Tomb - Cadw - gov.walesAs the sun rises on the summer solstice (the longest day of the year) shafts of light...</span></span></span>

Its most famous feature is the midsummer sunrise. Cadw states that at sunrise on the summer solstice, shafts of light shine directly down the passageway and illuminate the chamber. The Royal Commission on the Ancient and Historical Monuments of Wales gives the same basic account and explains that Bryn Celli Ddu is a late Neolithic passage grave built around 3,000 BC, excavated and partly restored in the 1920s by W. J. Hemp.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cadw.gov.wales/visit/places-to-visit/bryn-celli-ddu-chambered-tomb" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cadw.gov.wales">[Cadw]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cadw.gov.wales</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Cadw Bryn Celli Ddu Chambered Tomb</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Bryn Celli Ddu Chambered Tomb - Cadw - gov.walesAs the sun rises on the summer solstice (the longest day of the year) shafts of light...</span></span></span>

This is not a giantess tale in the same direct way as Barclodiad y Gawres. Its folklore value lies in the mechanism of awe. At Barclodiad, the question is “who could have carried these stones?” At Bryn Celli Ddu, the question is “who arranged stone, darkness and sunlight so precisely?” Both questions invite stories because both monuments feel intentional beyond ordinary daily life.

The midsummer alignment has also become part of the site’s living reputation. Cadw has referred to summer solstice celebrations at Bryn Celli Ddu, including closures during the Covid period because of the small nature of the site. More recent Cadw event material has also linked the monument with public archaeology, prehistory and folklore events. That shows how an ancient tomb can continue to gather seasonal meaning without anyone needing to claim an unbroken ritual tradition from the Neolithic to today.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cadw.gov.wales/about-us/news/bryn-celli-ddu-summer-solstice-gathering-cancelled" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cadw.gov.wales">[Cadw]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cadw.gov.wales</span><span class="citation-popover-title">bryn celli ddu summer solstice gathering cancelled</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">bryn celli ddu summer solstice gathering cancelled</span></span></span>

## The carved stones beneath the legends

The decorated stones at Barclodiad y Gawres and Bryn Celli Ddu are crucial because they stop the story becoming a simple contrast between “silly folklore” and “serious archaeology”. The monuments were never ordinary. Their builders invested labour in stone chambers, hidden spaces, carved surfaces, mound architecture and, at Bryn Celli Ddu, a solar relationship that still shapes the visitor imagination.

At Bryn Celli Ddu, the famous Pattern Stone is now held by Amgueddfa Cymru — Museum Wales. The museum describes it as a sandstone found in a Neolithic passage tomb near Menai Bridge, decorated by striking it with a tool. Its online catalogue adds an especially striking point: the builders deliberately hid it from view, suggesting that carving and placing the stone mattered even if it was not meant to be permanently visible.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://museum.wales/collections/online/object/6512880d-e714-305a-babe-768fd4dc4e0c/Bryn-celli-ddu-pattern-stone/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: museum.wales">[Museum Wales]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">museum.wales</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Wales Bryn-celli-ddu pattern stone</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wales Bryn-celli-ddu pattern stone</span></span></span>

The Royal Commission notes that the central pit at Bryn Celli Ddu contained the most richly decorated Neolithic carved stone in Wales, with the original now in National Museum Wales and a cast on site. Cadw’s Barclodiad y Gawres page, meanwhile, stresses the rarity of the carved stones there and their similarity to Irish Neolithic examples.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://rcahmw.gov.uk/angleseys-neolithic-tomb-with-a-solar-secret-here-comes-the-sun/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: rcahmw.gov.uk">[RCAHMW]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">rcahmw.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">angleseys neolithic tomb with a solar secret here comes the sun</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">angleseys neolithic tomb with a solar secret here comes the sun</span></span></span>

For folklore readers, this matters because later giant stories were not attached to blank or accidental landscapes. They gathered around places that already displayed unusual effort and meaning. A giantess with an apronful of stones is not a random invention floating free of place. It is a later imaginative answer to a genuine puzzle: how did people in the remote past create monuments that still feel powerful after five thousand years?

## Why ancient stones attract giant explanations

Giant folklore is one of the most natural ways for later communities to explain prehistoric stone monuments. Before modern archaeology, people did not have radiocarbon dates, excavation reports or typologies of passage graves. They had place-names, visible ruins and inherited stories. A massive capstone, a mound of boulders or a chambered tomb demanded an explanation, and giants supplied one that fitted the evidence seen by the eye: great stones must have been moved by great beings.

Anglesey’s version is especially effective because it turns construction into accident. The giantess does not carefully design a tomb; she drops stones from her apron. That kind of tale explains irregular heaps, cairns and ancient remains without needing a detailed building sequence. It also makes the landscape feel personal. The stone mound is no longer simply “prehistoric”; it is the trace of a dramatic moment.

Three mechanisms are at work:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **Scale becomes character.** Large stones imply large bodies. A tomb becomes the leftover work of a giantess rather than an anonymous ancient community.
* **Uncertainty becomes narrative.** Where archaeology sees a complex Neolithic monument, folklore supplies motive, journey, fear, deception and consequence.
* **Place becomes memorable.** A name like “The Giantess’s Apronful” is easier to remember and retell than a technical classification such as “decorated cruciform passage grave”.

</div>

This does not mean the folklore is useless as history. It tells us less about the Neolithic builders than about later relationships with the ancient landscape. It shows that Anglesey people, visitors and storytellers encountered these monuments as presences: old, strange, visible and in need of explanation.

## Old tradition, heritage retelling and modern wonder

A careful reading should separate three layers. The first is the Neolithic reality: these were built monuments, created by communities with their own beliefs, burial practices, ceremonies and relationships with land and sky. The second is later folklore: stories that explain ancient stones through giants, dropped loads and local wit. The third is modern heritage retelling: Cadw pages, tourism material, museum catalogues, guided tours and solstice events that shape how people now encounter the sites.

Barclodiad y Gawres sits strongly in the second and third layers. Its giantess name is central to how visitors remember it, but the modern public understanding of the tomb also depends on excavation, reconstruction and protected access to fragile carved stones. Cadw notes guided access on selected dates, reflecting the need to balance public fascination with conservation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cadw.gov.wales/barclodiad-y-gawres-guided-tours-0" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cadw.gov.wales">[Cadw]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cadw.gov.wales</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on gov.wales.</span></span></span>

Bryn Celli Ddu sits strongly in the first and third layers. The solar alignment is an archaeological and observational feature, but its public meaning today is shaped by modern solstice gatherings, museum interpretation and heritage storytelling. Steve Burrow’s scholarly work on the monument argues for a two-phase model of construction and places the tomb’s building between 3074 and 2956 cal BC, using radiocarbon dating, earlier excavation records and the demonstrated midsummer alignment.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/proceedings-of-the-prehistoric-society/article/bryn-celli-ddu-passage-tomb-anglesey-alignment-construction-date-and-ritual/6A95DFB4ED16F57FFCC6501466424DF1" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridge.org">[Cambridge University Press &amp; Assessment]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cambridge.org.</span></span></span>

The important point is continuity of attention, not simple continuity of belief. We cannot honestly say that modern solstice visitors are practising the same rites as Neolithic builders, or that the giantess tale preserves a direct prehistoric memory. What we can say is that these monuments have repeatedly attracted meaning. People keep returning to them because they make Anglesey’s deep past visible, walkable and story-shaped.

## Why these tombs matter to Anglesey’s identity

Anglesey’s folklore is not only about named characters such as saints, witches, giants or figures from medieval Welsh literature. It is also about the island’s unusually dense prehistoric landscape. Barclodiad y Gawres and Bryn Celli Ddu give that landscape two of its most memorable public images: the giantess’s dropped stones and the midsummer beam entering the dark chamber.

They matter because they keep Anglesey from being reduced to a scenic island with old ruins. They show it as a place where archaeology and legend speak to each other. The tombs are evidence of Neolithic skill and ritual life; the stories are evidence of later imagination, local explanation and the human need to make ancient remains intelligible.

For a visitor, the best way to understand them is not to choose between folklore and archaeology, but to let each answer a different question. Archaeology explains when the tombs were built, how they were structured, what was found inside and how they relate to other Atlantic monuments. Folklore explains why later people remembered them as giant work, why the names stuck, and why old stones still feel like active parts of Anglesey’s story.

Barclodiad y Gawres gives Anglesey its giantess with an apron full of stones. Bryn Celli Ddu gives it a chamber where the year’s longest-day sunrise still performs a brief act of illumination. Between them lies the mechanism behind much of the island’s ancient-stone folklore: when a monument is too old for ordinary memory but too visible to ignore, story rushes in to bridge the gap.
