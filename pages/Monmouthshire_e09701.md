## Which Monmouthshire are we talking about?

For folklore purposes, Monmouthshire needs careful geography. The historic county of Monmouthshire is larger than today’s Monmouthshire principal area. It includes places now administered separately, including Newport, Torfaen, most of Blaenau Gwent, and parts of Caerphilly and Cardiff east of the Rhymney, while the modern county created in 1996 covers roughly the eastern part, including Abergavenny, Caldicot, Chepstow, Monmouth and Usk. The historic county was one of the thirteen Welsh historic counties created from the Marcher lordships under the Laws in Wales Acts, and its legal and cultural status was long blurred by its position on the English border before being clearly settled within Wales in modern local government law.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monmouthshire_%28historic%29" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[wikipedia.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Monmouthshire (historic</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Monmouthshire (historic</span></span></span>



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/Monmouthshire_e09701-overview.webp" | relative_url }}" alt="Overview image for Where Monmouthshire&#x27;s Border Legends Gather" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

That matters because folklore does not obey council boundaries. A tale told at Chepstow may lean across the Wye into Gloucestershire; a Jack O’Kent story may belong as much to Herefordshire as to Monmouthshire; and Caerleon, now within Newport, remains central to the older county’s legendary geography. The Wikishire and Wikimedia historic-county mapping tradition treats Monmouthshire as one of the historic counties of Wales, and this is the most useful frame for a county folklore page because it follows older identities, parish histories and antiquarian collecting more closely than current administration.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://wikishire.co.uk/wiki/Monmouthshire" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wikishire.co.uk">[wikishire.co.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wikishire.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wikishire.co.uk.</span></span></span>

## Trellech: stones, wells and the making of a legendary village

Trellech is the clearest single folklore centre in Monmouthshire. The village name itself is tied to stone: Harold’s Stones, three large upright monoliths south of the village, are officially recorded as a Bronze Age stone alignment made of local conglomerate, probably dating from the later Neolithic or early Bronze Age. Coflein notes that their original purpose is uncertain, with ritual and astronomical interpretations both possible, and that geophysical work has suggested prehistoric and later features nearby.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://coflein.gov.uk/en/sites/221159" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: coflein.gov.uk">[Coflein]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">coflein.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on coflein.gov.uk.</span></span></span>

Folklore gives the stones a different kind of explanation. One tradition says they mark the graves of three chieftains killed in battle; another links them to Harold Godwinson’s campaign in Wales; a more playful border legend says Jack O’Kent hurled them there during a contest with the Devil. Visit Monmouthshire presents both the battle explanation and the Jack O’Kent version, while older folklore discussion in Margaret Eyre’s “Folk-Lore of the Wye Valley” treated Trellech as especially rich in “remains” and local battle-memory.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.visitmonmouthshire.com/things-to-do/harolds-stones-p2321381" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: visitmonmouthshire.com">[visitmonmouthshire.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">visitmonmouthshire.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Visit Monmouthshire Harold&#x27;s Stones</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Visit Monmouthshire Harold&#x27;s Stones</span></span></span>

The Virtuous Well, also at Trellech, shows how a place can shift from healing water to saintly association, wishing custom and fairy story. Visit Monmouthshire describes it as a holy or wishing well where a small metal object thrown into the water produced bubbles: many bubbles meant the wish would be granted quickly, fewer bubbles meant delay, and no bubbles meant failure. A related marriage divination custom said that young women counted the bubbles from a dropped pebble, one bubble for each month until marriage.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.visitmonmouthshire.com/things-to-do/the-virtuous-well-p2321371" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: visitmonmouthshire.com">[Visit Monmouthshire]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">visitmonmouthshire.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Visit Monmouthshire The Virtuous Well</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Visit Monmouthshire The Virtuous Well</span></span></span>

The well’s deeper history is more complicated than a simple “ancient holy well” label. The British Pilgrimage Trust presents it as St Anne’s Well, famous for healing and later used for wishing and fortune-telling, while holy-well researcher Tristan Gray Hulse has argued that the St Anne dedication and fairy associations are comparatively late layers attached to a medicinal well better known in earlier evidence as the Virtuous Well. That tension is useful: Trellech’s well is not less interesting because its stories changed. It is a good example of folklore as a living process, where healing reputation, church language, local romance, visitor offerings and modern spiritual practice can all gather around the same spring.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.britishpilgrimage.org/places/st-annes-well-virtuous-well-trellech" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: britishpilgrimage.org">[britishpilgrimage.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">britishpilgrimage.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on britishpilgrimage.org.</span></span></span>

## Jack O’Kent: the border trickster who throws landscapes around

Jack O’Kent is one of the great border figures of Monmouthshire folklore, though he belongs to the wider Welsh Marches rather than to Monmouthshire alone. He is usually a wizard, giant, clever cleric or folk hero who repeatedly outwits the Devil through verbal tricks and impossible bargains. Around Monmouthshire his stories are not just jokes; they explain the shape of the land.

The National Trust’s account of the Skirrid tells a classic version. Jack argues with the Devil over whether the Sugar Loaf is higher than the Malvern Hills. When Jack wins, the Devil tries to carry soil to the Malverns to make them higher, but his apron breaks over the Skirrid, forming the hill’s northern tump. Other versions connect Jack with the cleft of the Skirrid, the Sugar Loaf and the stones at Trellech, turning the uplands north of Abergavenny into a giant game-board.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nationaltrust.org.uk/visit/wales/sugar-loaf-skirrid-and-usk-valley/skirrid-history-and-legends" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nationaltrust.org.uk">[nationaltrust.org.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nationaltrust.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nationaltrust.org.uk.</span></span></span>

These tales matter because they are landscape folklore in a precise sense. They do not merely happen “somewhere in Wales”; they make local features memorable. A gap in a mountain becomes a heel-mark, a lump of earth becomes spilled soil, and a prehistoric stone alignment becomes the result of a throwing match. That is the same kind of explanatory storytelling found around standing stones and odd hills across Britain, but in Monmouthshire it takes on a border flavour: Welsh mountain, English hills, Devil, giant and trickster all meet within sight of one another.

Jack O’Kent also helps explain why Monmouthshire folklore often crosses county lines. Kentchurch, Herefordshire, is important to Jack’s wider legend, while Trellech and the Skirrid keep him inside Monmouthshire’s story-world. Rather than forcing him into one county, it is better to treat him as a Marches figure whose Monmouthshire tales are among the most vivid.

## Caerleon and Arthur: literary legend rooted in Roman stone

Caerleon’s Arthurian reputation is not a simple survival of oral folk memory. It is a blend of Roman remains, medieval literary imagination and later tourism. Geoffrey of Monmouth, the 12th-century writer associated with Monmouth, helped shape the medieval Arthurian tradition in his History of the Kings of Britain. People’s Collection Wales notes that Geoffrey identified Caerleon as Arthur’s court in that work, while Visit Wales explains that Caerleon’s Roman fortress and amphitheatre later became associated with Camelot and the Round Table, even though scholars continue to debate Arthur’s historicity and the geography of his legend.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.peoplescollection.wales/content/king-arthurs-round-table" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: peoplescollection.wales">[peoplescollection.wales]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">peoplescollection.wales</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on peoplescollection.wales.</span></span></span>

Caerleon’s power as a legendary site comes from visible ruins. Roman Isca, the fortress of the Second Augustan Legion, left an amphitheatre and military landscape impressive enough to invite medieval reimagining. Later writers and visitors could stand among Roman stone and imagine royal ceremony, battle, feasting and vanished grandeur. That makes Caerleon different from a purely oral ghost story: it is literary folklore, anchored to a real archaeological place and repeatedly revived by writers, guides and heritage interpretation.

Geoffrey himself is now part of Monmouthshire’s cultural folklore. Visit Monmouthshire calls him a major medieval storyteller and promotes a Geoffrey of Monmouth trail, showing how a writer who helped invent or reshape British legendary history has become a local heritage figure in his own right. The county can therefore claim not only Arthurian places, but one of the most influential makers of Arthurian Britain.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.visitmonmouthshire.com/inspire-me/about-monmouthshire/literary-legends/geoffrey-of-monmouth" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: visitmonmouthshire.com">[Visit Monmouthshire]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">visitmonmouthshire.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on visitmonmouthshire.com.</span></span></span>

## The Wye Valley, Tintern and Chepstow: border legends beside the river

The Wye Valley gives Monmouthshire some of its most atmospheric folklore settings. The river forms a natural corridor between Wales and England, with castles, abbeys, woods, cliffs and old roads close together. Tintern Abbey, founded in the 12th century, has long attracted romantic and supernatural storytelling because of its ruinous beauty and border position. One well-known local tradition places the Devil’s Pulpit on the Gloucestershire side of the Wye, looking across to Tintern; the myth says the Devil preached from the rock in an attempt to tempt the monks away from the abbey. Although the viewpoint is across the modern county and national boundary, the story belongs naturally to the Tintern-Wye legendary landscape. Visit the Forest of Dean &amp; Wye Valley<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.visitdeanwye.co.uk/blog/myths-legends-of-the-wye-valley" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: visitdeanwye.co.uk">[visitdeanwye.co.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">visitdeanwye.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on visitdeanwye.co.uk.</span></span></span>

Chepstow adds a different strand: revived seasonal custom. The Chepstow Wassail and Mari Lwyd brings Welsh horse-skull tradition and border wassailing together each January. Visit Monmouthshire describes it as an annual Monmouthshire event in the border town, with Morris groups, traditional beasts, music, singing and procession. This is modern folklore in a strong sense: not an untouched survival, but a living public performance that uses older forms to create present-day community identity.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.visitmonmouthshire.com/whats-on/chepstow-wassail-and-mari-lwyd-p2078181" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: visitmonmouthshire.com">[Visit Monmouthshire]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">visitmonmouthshire.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on visitmonmouthshire.com.</span></span></span>

The Wye also encourages “two-sided” storytelling. A Monmouthshire legend may be viewed from a Gloucestershire rock, retold by a Welsh tourism body, performed by English and Welsh dancers, or attached to a crossing point such as Chepstow Bridge. For a county whose history has often been argued over as Welsh, English or borderland, that is not a flaw. It is part of the folklore’s character.

## Mari Lwyd and winter visiting customs

The Mari Lwyd is one of the most recognisable Welsh winter customs: a decorated horse skull or horse head mounted on a pole, carried by a person hidden beneath a cloth, traditionally taken from house to house with singing and verbal contest. Amgueddfa Cymru describes it as a Christmas-season door-to-door wassailing custom, while Wales.com emphasises its eerie midwinter atmosphere and regional variations.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://museum.wales/articles/1187/Christmas-Traditions-The-Mari-Lwyd/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: museum.wales">[Museum Wales]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">museum.wales</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on museum.wales.</span></span></span>

Monmouthshire sits at the eastern edge of the Mari Lwyd’s historic and revived range. Folklore scholarship has generally associated the custom most strongly with Glamorgan, but recorded examples extended east into the Monmouthshire valleys and as far as Monmouth, and modern revival has made Chepstow one of the best-known public border performances. This is important because it prevents a common misunderstanding: the Mari Lwyd is not simply a museum-piece “ancient pagan rite”. It is a documented, changing folk custom, shaped by Christmas visiting, song, drink, household hospitality, local pride and modern revival.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mari_Lwyd" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[wikipedia.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Mari Lwyd</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Mari Lwyd</span></span></span>

For readers exploring Monmouthshire folklore today, Chepstow’s Mari Lwyd is one of the easiest traditions to see in action. It also demonstrates how folklore survives by adaptation. The old pattern of visiting houses becomes a town procession; local winter sociability becomes heritage performance; and the terrifying horse at the door becomes a shared emblem of Welsh-border identity.

## Clydach Gorge: fairies, witches and industrial memory

Clydach Gorge, in the north-west of Monmouthshire between Abergavenny and Brynmawr, is a wooded gorge shaped by the River Clydach and by industrial history. Visit Monmouthshire describes it as a hidden gem, but also notes that its fast-moving water powered ironworks, blast furnaces, quarries and mines during the Industrial Revolution. That mix of woodland, water, road, tunnel and industrial ruin makes it a natural setting for supernatural narrative.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.visitmonmouthshire.com/explore/brecon-beacons-national-park/clydach-gorge" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: visitmonmouthshire.com">[Visit Monmouthshire]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">visitmonmouthshire.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on visitmonmouthshire.com.</span></span></span>

Local and guide traditions connect the gorge with fairies, goblins, witches and spirits. The Abergavenny Chronicle reported a guided folklore walk in which the gorge was presented as a place whose “mystical properties” were linked to beliefs in fairies, goblins and witches, with even a local claim that Shakespeare drew inspiration there for A Midsummer Night’s Dream. That Shakespeare claim is best treated as local romance rather than firm literary history, but it shows how strongly the gorge invites enchantment.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.abergavennychronicle.com/news/fairies-witches-spirits-and-shakespeare-a-guide-to-the-folklore-of-the-clydach-gorge-71603" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: abergavennychronicle.com">[Abergavenny Chronicle]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">abergavennychronicle.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Abergavenny Chroniclea guide to the folklore of the Clydach Gorge</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Abergavenny Chroniclea guide to the folklore of the Clydach Gorge</span></span></span>

The most useful way to read Clydach Gorge folklore is not as proof of ancient fairy belief preserved unchanged, but as a meeting of landscape moods. Steep wooded sides, rushing water, transport routes, industrial remains and dangerous working history all make the place feel charged. In that sense, Clydach belongs with other Welsh gorge and valley traditions where fairies, witches and ghosts express the strangeness of difficult terrain and the memory of hard labour.

## Castles, ghosts and tourist haunting

Monmouthshire has many castles, so it is unsurprising that ghosts have gathered around them. Raglan Castle is the grandest example. Cadw calls it “the grandest castle ever built by Welshmen”, with Sir William ap Thomas and William Herbert central to its 15th-century development. Its later Civil War damage and ruined state make it a natural stage for ghost stories, especially stories of sorrowful figures, hidden books, lost rooms and presences in corridors.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cadw.gov.wales/visit/places-to-visit/raglan-castle" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cadw.gov.wales">[Cadw]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cadw.gov.wales</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Cadw Raglan Castle &#124; Cadw</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cadw Raglan Castle &#124; Cadw</span></span></span>

Caldicot Castle shows how haunting becomes part of visitor culture. Monmouthshire’s own tourism page describes the castle as a Norman foundation, later developed in royal hands and restored as a Victorian family home, and states that it is reputed to be haunted by a number of ghosts. The same page lists modern ghost-hunt events, making clear that the haunting tradition now operates partly as heritage entertainment.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.monmouthshire.gov.uk/places-to-visit/caldicot-castle" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: monmouthshire.gov.uk">[Monmouthshire]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">monmouthshire.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on monmouthshire.gov.uk.</span></span></span>

This does not make the ghost stories meaningless. Castle ghosts often preserve emotional readings of history: siege, abandonment, aristocratic decline, imprisonment, family tragedy, ruined libraries, dark towers. But they need a different evidential label from older collected oral tales. In Monmouthshire, many castle hauntings are best understood as modern or semi-modern legend attached to highly atmospheric historic sites, rather than as securely traceable medieval traditions.

## Fairies, wells and what is missing

Readers often expect every Welsh county to have abundant fairy hills, black dogs, mermaids, dragons and witch legends. Monmouthshire does have fairy and witch language around places such as Trellech and Clydach Gorge, and it shares the wider Welsh world of fairies, spectral hounds, saints, wells and otherworldly landscapes. But the county’s best-supported local folklore is more specific than a generic Welsh myth list: stones at Trellech, Jack O’Kent in the border hills, Arthurian Caerleon, the Virtuous Well, the Mari Lwyd, Wye Valley legends and castle ghosts.

Black-dog folklore is a good example of the need for caution. Spectral black dogs are widespread in British folklore, often appearing as omens, guardians or death-portents, but strong Monmouthshire-specific black-dog traditions are not as prominent in the readily accessible county evidence as Jack O’Kent, Trellech or the Mari Lwyd. It is better to say that Monmouthshire belongs to the wider British and Welsh supernatural-hound landscape than to invent a county “black dog” where the evidence is thin.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://tetzoo.com/blog/2022/8/13/legend-of-the-black-dog" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: tetzoo.com">[tetzoo.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">tetzoo.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Tetrapod Zoology Legend of the Black Dog</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Tetrapod Zoology Legend of the Black Dog</span></span></span>

The same caution applies to dragons and mermaids. The Welsh dragon is nationally powerful, and Monmouthshire’s Roman, Arthurian and Marcher history gives it plenty of mythic resonance, but the county’s distinctive folklore identity does not rest on one famous local dragon tale in the way that some places are defined by a single monster. Monmouthshire’s strongest “creatures” are more likely to be the Mari Lwyd horse, the Devil as Jack’s opponent, and the fairy presences around wells and wooded gorges.

## Who collected and preserved the traditions?

Monmouthshire folklore survives through several kinds of source. Antiquarian and folklore journals are crucial for older material. Margaret Eyre’s 1905 “Folk-Lore of the Wye Valley” is especially useful for Trellech and the Wye border, while Beatrix Wherry’s Monmouthshire notes, also published in Folklore in 1905, preserve local motifs such as underground music and cave stories. These sources are valuable because they record local speech and belief relatively early, though they also carry the assumptions and social attitudes of their period.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.jstor.org/stable/1253996" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: jstor.org">[JSTOR]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">jstor.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on jstor.org.</span></span></span>

Wales-wide collectors also shape how Monmouthshire is read. Marie Trevelyan’s Folk-lore and Folk-stories of Wales popularised Welsh supernatural material in the early 20th century, drawing on oral accounts, manuscripts, correspondence and earlier antiquarians. Modern scholars have treated her work as important but not unproblematic: some of her material is vivid and influential, while some claims need checking against stronger local evidence.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marie_Trevelyan" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[wikipedia.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Marie Trevelyan</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Marie Trevelyan</span></span></span>

Modern preservation is more public-facing. Cadw, Coflein, Visit Monmouthshire, Amgueddfa Cymru, local history groups, walking guides and revived custom groups all play a role. Coflein and Cadw help separate archaeological fact from legend at places such as Harold’s Stones and Raglan Castle; tourism pages keep legends visible to visitors; and events such as the Chepstow Mari Lwyd turn folklore into performance rather than simply text.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://coflein.gov.uk/en/sites/221159" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: coflein.gov.uk">[coflein.gov.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">coflein.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on coflein.gov.uk.</span></span></span>

## Why Monmouthshire folklore feels different

Monmouthshire’s folklore identity comes from crossing-points. It is a Welsh county on an English border; a historic county partly hidden beneath modern administrative names; a landscape of Roman fortress, medieval lordship, abbey ruin, industrial gorge, market town and mountain edge. Its stories often explain boundaries: between Wales and England, Christianity and older water-belief, archaeology and legend, literature and oral tale, winter death and New Year renewal.

Trellech turns prehistoric stone and spring water into local memory. Jack O’Kent makes mountains and standing stones into the punchline of a Devil-tricking contest. Caerleon shows how a Roman military site became part of Arthurian imagination. Chepstow’s Mari Lwyd shows a custom remade for a modern border town. Clydach Gorge shows how fairy and witch stories cling to places where natural beauty and industrial memory meet.

The county’s folklore is therefore not a single mythology but a layered map. Some layers are ancient monuments; some are medieval literary inventions; some are early modern and Victorian antiquarian interpretations; some are modern heritage performances. Monmouthshire is most rewarding when those layers are kept visible. That is where the county’s mystery really lies: not in pretending every tale is ancient, but in seeing how each generation found old stones, wells, ruins and roads ready to be storied again.
