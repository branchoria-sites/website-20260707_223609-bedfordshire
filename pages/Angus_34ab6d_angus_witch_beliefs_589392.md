## Introduction

Witches and magical beliefs in [Angus]({{ 'what-hidden-stories-shape-angus-folklore/' | relative_url }}) belong to a complicated history of fear, healing, accusation and memory. The county’s witch traditions are best understood not as stories of confirmed supernatural events, but as records of how communities explained illness, bad luck, conflict and unusual behaviour. During the Scottish witch-hunting period, people in Angus were caught up in wider national beliefs that magic could harm crops, livestock, families and individuals, while local healers, charmers and “wise” practitioners existed within the same cultural world.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://witches.hca.ed.ac.uk/about/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: witches.hca.ed.ac.uk">[witches.hca.ed.ac.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">witches.hca.ed.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">About the Survey of Scottish WitchcraftDuring the course of research into the local aspects of witchcraft and witch prosecution, we visit...</span></span></span>

Angus is particularly important because it preserves both sides of the story: the suffering caused by witch accusations, especially around Forfar and Brechin, and the older layers of folk belief involving charms, healing practices and magical explanations for misfortune. Modern retellings often focus on “witches” as mysterious figures, but the historical record shows a more human story of neighbours, disputes, illness and social pressures.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://angusfolklore.blogspot.com/2015/03/early-records-of-witchcraft.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: angusfolklore.blogspot.com">[angusfolklore.blogspot.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">angusfolklore.blogspot.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">early records of witchcraft</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">1 Apr 2015 — One of the earliest records of witchcraft in Angus comes from Arbroath and gives an insight into the motivation of those who...</span></span></span>

## Witchcraft accusations and historical records

### The Forfar witch trials and the memory of persecution

The most significant witchcraft episode associated with Angus is the Forfar witch-hunt of the 1660s. During this period, a number of local people were accused of witchcraft in a climate of intense Scottish persecution. The accusations included claims of causing illness, damaging crops and livestock, and taking part in meetings with the Devil. These allegations reflected the religious and social fears of seventeenth-century Scotland rather than evidence that the accused possessed supernatural powers.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://visitangus.com/discover-the-myths-and-legends-of-angus/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: visitangus.com">[Visit Angus]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">visitangus.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Visit Angus Angus Myths and Legends &#124; Visit Angus</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Visit AngusAngus Myths and Legends &#124; Visit AngusSeptember 16, 2012...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: September 16, 2012</span></span></span>

Forfar’s story also shows how witch accusations could spread through a community. Suspicion often began with a local disagreement, an unexplained event or a reputation for being unusual, and could grow into formal investigations involving church and civil authorities. Scotland’s witchcraft law operated from 1563 until 1736, and surviving records from courts, kirk sessions and government archives provide evidence of how accusations were processed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.scotlandspeople.gov.uk/help-and-support/guides/crime-and-criminals?msockid=1a43c73213ad6cef0eded1d2127e6d02" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: scotlandspeople.gov.uk">[Scotland&#x27;s People]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">scotlandspeople.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Scotland&#x27;s People Crime and Criminals &#124; Scotland&#x27;s People</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Scotland&#x27;s People Crime and Criminals &#124; Scotland&#x27;s People</span></span></span>

The memory of these events remains part of Angus’s local identity. The Meffan Museum and Art Gallery in Forfar includes displays connected with local history, including representations relating to the witch persecutions, while modern heritage writing about Angus increasingly treats the witch trials as a reminder of historical injustice rather than as a source of supernatural entertainment.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meffan_Institute" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Meffan Institute</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Meffan Institute</span></span></span>

### Brechin, Witchden and the physical landscape of memory

Brechin also carries memories connected with witch accusations. Local tradition associates Witchden Road with the place where accused witches were held or punished, linking the modern landscape with the darker history of early modern Scotland. Stories connected with the site have been preserved through local historical writing, including accounts of discoveries of ash, bones and other remains associated with the area’s grim past.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://angusfolklore.blogspot.com/2017/01/a-last-look-at-witches.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: angusfolklore.blogspot.com">[angusfolklore.blogspot.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">angusfolklore.blogspot.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Angus Folklore: A Last Look at Witches21 Jan 2017 — The guilty or suspected witches of the town were punished in a hollow named the Witch...</span></span></span>

These places matter because witch traditions are often attached to landscapes rather than only to written records. A road name, hollow, hill or field could preserve the memory of an event long after the original circumstances had faded. In Angus, such locations show how communities continued to negotiate the meaning of witchcraft accusations: as warnings from the past, stories of injustice, and reminders of how fear could shape everyday life.

## Healing, curses and local magical traditions

### Folk magic between medicine and superstition

Belief in magic in Angus was not limited to accusations of harmful witchcraft. Many traditional practices existed around protection, healing and attempts to control uncertainty. Across Scotland, people used charms, amulets and household rituals to guard against illness, injury and misfortune. These practices often existed alongside Christian belief and everyday medicine rather than replacing them completely.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nms.ac.uk/discover-catalogue/from-amulets-to-elf-bolts-10-scottish-charms" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nms.ac.uk">[National Museums Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nms.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Museums Scotland From amulets to elf bolts: 11 Scottish Charms</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">witchcraft was deeply rooted. A person who knew herbal remedies, prayers or protective customs could occupy an uncertain position in the community. The same knowledge that made someone valuable as a healer could also make them suspicious if illness continued or relationships broke down. This tension helps explain why historical “witch” cases cannot simply be divided into believers in magic and people who rejected it; many communities accepted some forms of magical practice while condemning others.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nms.ac.uk/discover-catalogue/from-amulets-to-elf-bolts-10-scottish-charms" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nms.ac.uk">[National Museums Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nms.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Museums Scotland From amulets to elf bolts: 11 Scottish Charms</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">witchcraft was deeply rooted. ### Charms and explanations for misfortune

Traditional magical beliefs often developed around problems that had no obvious explanation. A sick child, failing harvest, dying animal or sudden illness might be understood through ideas of curses, the evil eye or harmful supernatural influence. Charms offered a way for people to respond when ordinary solutions seemed insufficient.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nms.ac.uk/discover-catalogue/from-amulets-to-elf-bolts-10-scottish-charms" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nms.ac.uk">[National Museums Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nms.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Museums Scotland From amulets to elf bolts: 11 Scottish Charms</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">witchcraft was deeply rooted. We delve into eleven objects and charms that people used for centuries in hopes of curing their ailments an...</span></span></span>

In Angus, as elsewhere in Scotland, these beliefs formed part of a wider rural culture where landscape, religion, health and folklore overlapped. A charm or ritual was not necessarily viewed as entertainment or fantasy by the people who used it; it could be a serious response to the uncertainties of life. At the same time, later generations have often reshaped these practices into folklore, separating them from their original social setting.

## How witch stories are remembered today

Modern Angus witch traditions sit between history and legend. The documented trials of Forfar and other places are studied as examples of persecution and social fear, while stories about witches, magical places and old customs continue through local heritage projects, tourism and folklore writing.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://visitangus.com/discover-the-myths-and-legends-of-angus/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: visitangus.com">[Visit Angus]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">visitangus.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Visit Angus Angus Myths and Legends &#124; Visit Angus</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Visit AngusAngus Myths and Legends &#124; Visit AngusSeptember 16, 2012...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: September 16, 2012</span></span></span>

The challenge in retelling these stories is avoiding two extremes: treating accusations as proof of supernatural activity, or dismissing the traditions as meaningless superstition. The records reveal real experiences of accusation, imprisonment and execution, while the surviving folklore reveals how Angus communities tried to understand danger, healing and the unknown.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.scotlandspeople.gov.uk/help-and-support/guides/crime-and-criminals?msockid=1a43c73213ad6cef0eded1d2127e6d02" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: scotlandspeople.gov.uk">[Scotland&#x27;s People]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">scotlandspeople.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Scotland&#x27;s People Crime and Criminals &#124; Scotland&#x27;s People</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Scotland&#x27;s People Crime and Criminals &#124; Scotland&#x27;s People</span></span></span>
