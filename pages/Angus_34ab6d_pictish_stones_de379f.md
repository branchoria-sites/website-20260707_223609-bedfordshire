## Introduction

[Angus]({{ 'what-hidden-stories-shape-angus-folklore/' | relative_url }})’s Pictish stones are among the clearest places where archaeology and local imagination meet. These carved monuments, created roughly between the early medieval centuries of the first millennium AD, preserve evidence of Pictish identity, belief, art and power — while their unanswered questions have allowed later generations to attach new meanings to the landscape around them. Sites such as Aberlemno, St Vigeans and Eassie are not “mystery stones” in the sense of lacking evidence; they are carefully studied archaeological monuments whose surviving carvings continue to inspire stories because so much of their original symbolism remains uncertain.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.historicenvironment.scot/visit/all/aberlemno-sculptured-stones/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: historicenvironment.scot">[Historic Environment Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">historicenvironment.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Historic Environment ScotlandAberlemno Sculptured StonesThere are four stones with Pictish carvings in the village of Aberlemno, variousl...</span></span></span>

For people exploring Angus’s heritage, these stones offer something unusual: a chance to stand in places where ancient craftsmanship, religious change, landscape memory and later folklore overlap. ## Why Angus’s ancient stones still fascinate

Pictish stones are some of the most distinctive monuments of early medieval Scotland. They were carved with symbols, animals, warriors, crosses and other designs, but many of the meanings behind the imagery are still debated. Archaeologists can identify repeated motifs such as the serpent, double disc and z-rod, mirror and comb, and various animal figures, yet there is no surviving written explanation from the Picts themselves that provides a complete key to their visual language.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.historicenvironment.scot/visit/all/aberlemno-sculptured-stones/history-and-stories/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: historicenvironment.scot">[Historic Environment Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">historicenvironment.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Historic Environment ScotlandHistory and stories &#124; Aberlemno Sculptured StonesJudging from the prehistoric cup marks on its back, this st...</span></span></span>

This uncertainty has shaped how people have understood the stones over time. A carved monument standing beside an old road or within a churchyard naturally becomes part of the story of a place. Even when modern archaeology explains its date and construction, the stone still carries layers of memory: who noticed it, who protected it, what stories grew around it, and why it remained important.

In Angus, this relationship between monument and landscape is especially strong because many Pictish sites survive close to older settlement areas, religious centres and farming landscapes. The stones are not isolated museum objects; they remain connected to the places where generations of people encountered them.

## Aberlemno, St Vigeans and Eassie stone sites

### Aberlemno: symbols beside the old road

The Aberlemno Sculptured Stones are among Angus’s best-known Pictish monuments. Four carved stones survive at the site, dating broadly from around AD 500 to 800. They include symbol stones and cross-slabs, showing the transition between older Pictish imagery and Christian artistic traditions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.historicenvironment.scot/visit/all/aberlemno-sculptured-stones/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: historicenvironment.scot">[Historic Environment Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">historicenvironment.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Historic Environment ScotlandAberlemno Sculptured StonesThere are four stones with Pictish carvings in the village of Aberlemno, variousl...</span></span></span>

The roadside stones are particularly powerful because they remain in the landscape rather than being removed entirely into a collection. One stone appears to have reused an earlier prehistoric standing stone, while its carvings include familiar Pictish symbols such as a serpent, double disc and z-rod, and mirror and comb. This combination of an ancient standing place with later carved meaning demonstrates how landscapes could be reused and reinterpreted across centuries.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.historicenvironment.scot/visit/all/aberlemno-sculptured-stones/history-and-stories/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: historicenvironment.scot">[Historic Environment Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">historicenvironment.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Historic Environment ScotlandHistory and stories &#124; Aberlemno Sculptured StonesJudging from the prehistoric cup marks on its back, this st...</span></span></span>

The churchyard cross-slab at Aberlemno is also important because it combines Christian imagery with scenes interpreted as possibly representing warfare. Some interpretations have connected its battle imagery with the Battle of Dunnichen in AD 685, although the exact meaning of the scene remains debated. The stone’s fascination comes from this mixture of evidence and uncertainty: it records a world of belief and conflict without telling a simple story in modern words.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.historicenvironment.scot/visit-a-place/places/aberlemno-sculptured-stones/history/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: historicenvironment.scot">[Historic Environment Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">historicenvironment.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on historicenvironment.scot.</span></span></span>

### St Vigeans: a religious landscape carved in stone

Near Arbroath, St Vigeans preserves one of Angus’s richest collections of Pictish sculpture. The site contains 38 carved stones, many associated with the church mound where they were once placed. The quantity and quality of the carvings suggest that St Vigeans was an important religious centre during the early medieval period.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.historicenvironment.scot/visit/all/st-vigeans-sculptured-stones-museum/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: historicenvironment.scot">[Historic Environment Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">historicenvironment.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Historic Environment Scotland St Vigeans Sculptured Stones &#124; Historic Scotland</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Historic Environment Scotland St Vigeans Sculptured Stones &#124; Historic Scotland</span></span></span>

The famous Drosten Stone shows how Angus’s stones can reveal a changing religious landscape. It combines Christian imagery with Pictish symbols and includes an inscription using Roman letters, reflecting a society where local traditions and wider Christian influences existed together.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.historicenvironment.scot/visit/all/st-vigeans-sculptured-stones-museum/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: historicenvironment.scot">[Historic Environment Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">historicenvironment.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Historic Environment Scotland St Vigeans Sculptured Stones &#124; Historic Scotland</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Historic Environment Scotland St Vigeans Sculptured Stones &#124; Historic Scotland</span></span></span>

For folklore and landscape memory, St Vigeans is significant because the stones were not simply discovered as forgotten objects. They were embedded in the life of a sacred place. Later communities encountered them through the churchyard, village and local traditions, allowing the stones to remain part of Angus’s living heritage rather than becoming only archaeological remains.

### Eassie: an ancient monument in a changing countryside

The Eassie Sculptured Stone, near Glamis, is another major Angus example. The carved sandstone slab survives within the ruins of the old church at Eassie and preserves Pictish designs including human and symbolic imagery.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.historicenvironment.scot/visit/all/eassie-sculptured-stone/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: historicenvironment.scot">[Historic Environment Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">historicenvironment.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Historic Environment Scotland Eassie Sculptured Stone &#124; Historic Scotland</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Historic Environment Scotland Eassie Sculptured Stone &#124; Historic Scotland</span></span></span>

Its setting illustrates a recurring pattern in Angus: ancient monuments often remain close to later religious sites. This does not mean that every medieval or modern tradition preserves an unchanged memory of the Picts, but it shows how certain locations continued to be treated as meaningful places. A landscape already marked by an old monument could gather new stories, associations and traditions over many generations.

## Symbols, mysteries and folk interpretations

The fascination of Pictish stones comes partly from the gap between what is visible and what is known. The carvings are deliberate and repeated, suggesting that the symbols communicated important ideas, identities or relationships. However, modern interpretations must remain cautious because the original meanings are not fully recoverable.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.aberdeenshire.gov.uk/leisure-sport-and-culture/archaeology/sites-to-visit/pictish-symbol-stones/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: aberdeenshire.gov.uk">[Aberdeenshire Council]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">aberdeenshire.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Aberdeenshire Council Pictish Symbol Stones</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Aberdeenshire CouncilPictish Symbol Stones - Aberdeenshire Council...</span></span></span>

Several themes have encouraged later imagination:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **Animals and strange creatures:** Pictish carvings include unusual beasts that have attracted speculation, especially where the original symbolic meaning is unknown.
* **Warriors and battles:** Scenes of armed figures connect stones with ideas of ancestry, power and memory.
* **Christian imagery alongside older symbols:** Crosses and biblical designs appear alongside earlier Pictish motifs, showing cultural change rather than a simple replacement of one belief system by another.
* **Ancient stones in open landscapes:** A carved stone beside a road, field or church naturally becomes a landmark around which stories and local identity develop.

</div>

Older communities often explained unusual monuments through the traditions available to them. Across Scotland, standing stones and ancient places could become associated with giants, supernatural beings, saints or forgotten peoples. In Angus, the Pictish stones occupy a similar imaginative space: their real history is archaeological, but their continued presence invites storytelling.

The important distinction is that these later meanings are not evidence of what the Picts themselves believed. They are evidence of how later generations responded to a landscape filled with visible reminders of a distant past.

## Archaeology, memory and the visitor experience

Today, Angus’s Pictish stones are valued because they allow visitors to experience history in place. A museum display can explain carving techniques, dates and interpretations, but seeing a stone in its landscape changes the encounter. The surrounding fields, churchyards and roads become part of the meaning.

Conservation also plays an important role because these monuments are fragile.
