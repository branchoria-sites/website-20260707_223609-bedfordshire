## Introduction

The best way to understand Inverness-shire folklore is not as a single “mythology”, but as a map of places where story clings to landscape. Some traditions are old oral material recorded by collectors; some are medieval saintly legend; some are antiquarian interpretation; some are modern tourism and media retelling. The county’s identity comes from the way all these layers meet in recognisable places: a loch, a hill, a well, a castle, a cairn, a sea stack, or a road through the glens.

## What counts as Inverness-shire folklore?

Historic Inverness-shire is not the same thing as today’s Highland Council area. The historic county stretched across a huge part of the Highlands and islands: the mainland Great Glen and surrounding districts, Skye and Raasay, several Inner Hebridean islands, most of the Outer Hebrides apart from Lewis, and St Kilda far out in the Atlantic. Wikishire notes the county’s border with Ross-shire in the Outer Hebrides as the line between Harris and Lewis, while Scotland’s People records Inverness county as a historic county whose local-government role ended in 1975.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://wikishire.co.uk/wiki/Inverness-shire" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wikishire.co.uk">[Wikishire]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wikishire.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Inverness shire</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Inverness shire</span></span></span>

That matters for folklore because many famous “Highland” or “Hebridean” traditions fall within Inverness-shire when the historic county map is used. A page on Inverness-shire therefore has to hold together mainland Inverness and Loch Ness with Skye, Harris, North Uist, South Uist, Benbecula and St Kilda, while not pretending that every tale from the modern Highlands belongs here. The centre of gravity is the old county: its lochs, glens, islands, ferry routes, clan territories, Gaelic-speaking communities and visitor landmarks.

The county’s folklore survives in several kinds of evidence. Medieval texts give saintly miracle stories; early modern court records preserve witchcraft accusations; nineteenth- and twentieth-century collectors recorded Gaelic charms, tales and customs; museums and archives now present oral history; and tourism bodies retell a smaller set of famous legends for visitors. Tobar an Dualchais/Kist o Riches is especially important for later oral tradition, because it gives public access to recordings of songs, stories, music, history and poetry recorded across Scotland and the Scottish diaspora from the 1930s onwards.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.tobarandualchais.co.uk/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: tobarandualchais.co.uk">[Tobar an Dualchais]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">tobarandualchais.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on tobarandualchais.co.uk.</span></span></span>

## Loch Ness: monster, saint and media legend

Loch Ness is Inverness-shire’s best-known folklore landscape, but the familiar “Nessie” story is not one simple ancient tale. It has at least three layers: the early saintly story of Columba and a water beast near the River Ness, older Highland water-monster motifs, and the modern press-driven monster legend that began in the twentieth century.

The early written anchor is Adomnán’s *Life of St Columba*, composed around AD 700, which tells how Columba confronted a dangerous water creature near the River Ness. The University of the Highlands and Islands summarises this as one of the earliest Highland monster accounts and stresses that it appears in a hagiography: a saint’s life written to show Columba’s spiritual power, not a modern eyewitness report.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.uhi.ac.uk/en/research-enterprise/cultural/institute-for-northern-studies/blogs--exhibitions/old-mimirs-well-articles/columba%2C-nessie%2C-and-the-deadly-loathsome-little-creatures/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: uhi.ac.uk">[UHI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">uhi.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">columba, nessie, and the deadly loathsome little creatures</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">columba, nessie, and the deadly loathsome little creatures</span></span></span>

The modern Loch Ness Monster, however, belongs above all to 1933. Am Baile identifies the Inverness Courier article of 2 May 1933 as the first newspaper report concerning a sighting of the Loch Ness Monster, while later summaries of the press episode note that the story quickly became a media phenomenon.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ambaile.org.uk/asset/1755/1/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ambaile.org.uk">[Am Baile]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ambaile.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on ambaile.org.uk.</span></span></span>

That distinction matters. Loch Ness certainly sits in a wider Highland world of water-beast and loch traditions, but the global image of a long-necked creature, disputed photographs, sonar searches and “Nessie hunting” is a modern legend. It is folklore too, but folklore of newspapers, tourism, popular science, hoaxes and mass imagination as much as folklore of hearthside storytelling.

For Inverness-shire identity, Nessie has become both blessing and distortion. The monster brings worldwide attention to the county’s landscape, especially Drumnadrochit, Urquhart Castle and the Great Glen. Yet it can also flatten local tradition into one celebrity creature, when the county’s older supernatural culture includes saints, fairies, death omens, healing rites, witchcraft beliefs and clan legends.

## Tomnahurich: Inverness’s fairy hill

On the edge of Inverness, Tomnahurich is one of the county’s most accessible fairy landscapes. It is now a cemetery, but local storytelling has long treated the hill as a place where the fairy world touches the human one. Discover Highlands and Islands describes it as a steep hill on the south-western edge of the city, now surrounded and topped by a Victorian cemetery, and presents it as one of northern Scotland’s prime fairy locations.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://discoverhighlandsandislands.scot/en/trip-idea/the-fairy-hill-of-inverness" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: discoverhighlandsandislands.scot">[Discover Highlands and Islands]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">discoverhighlandsandislands.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on discoverhighlandsandislands.scot.</span></span></span>

The most famous tale is usually called the Fiddlers of Tomnahurich. In its common modern form, two travelling musicians are invited inside the hill to play for a fairy gathering. They think they have been there only a short while, but when they return to Inverness, far more time has passed. This is a classic fairy-time motif: the fairy realm is not just another place, but a different order of time, payment, hospitality and danger. Map of Stories presents it as a well-known Highland folktale in which young fiddlers are drawn into trouble after agreeing to play at a fairy event.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://mapofstories.scot/the-story-of-the-fiddlers-of-tomnahurich-english/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: mapofstories.scot">[mapofstories.scot]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">mapofstories.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on mapofstories.scot.</span></span></span>

Tomnahurich also shows how place-name, folklore and later interpretation can become tangled. The hill is widely known in English as the Fairy Hill, but the Gaelic place-name is more accurately explained as “hill of the yew wood”, not literally “hill of the fairies”. High Life Highland’s Am Baile archive notes both the yew-tree meaning and the popular Fairy Hill association.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ambaile.org.uk/asset/21814/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ambaile.org.uk">[Am Baile]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ambaile.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Am Baile Tomnahurich from the Canal</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Am Baile Tomnahurich from the Canal</span></span></span>

That makes Tomnahurich a useful test case. The folklore is real as a local tradition, but the “fairy” meaning of the name should not be overstated. The hill matters because stories have gathered there, not because every later English label is an exact translation of an ancient belief.

## Skye, Dunvegan and the Fairy Flag

Because historic Inverness-shire includes Skye, the Fairy Flag of Dunvegan belongs within the county’s folklore map. Dunvegan Castle describes the flag, known as Am Bratach Sith, as one of Clan MacLeod’s most treasured possessions, probably woven of silk in the eastern Mediterranean world and traditionally believed to have miraculous powers when unfurled in battle.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.dunvegancastle.com/castle/fairy-flag/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: dunvegancastle.com">[Dunvegan Castle]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">dunvegancastle.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on dunvegancastle.com.</span></span></span>

The power of the Fairy Flag legend lies in the meeting of object and story. Unlike many fairy tales attached only to a place, this one is attached to a fragile physical heirloom displayed in a clan castle. Traditions variously say it was given by a fairy lover, left for an infant chieftain, or granted to protect the clan in times of peril. The flag is said to have helped in battle, protected the clan, cured cattle or brought prosperity, depending on the version.

For readers, the key point is that the Fairy Flag is not simply “proof” of fairies, nor merely a decorative tourist tale. It is a clan relic around which stories of ancestry, danger, legitimacy and protection have crystallised. Its material history and its legendary history do not cancel each other out. Instead, they show how Highland folklore often works: a real object becomes more powerful because generations have told stories about what it means.

Skye’s wider fairy reputation is also part of modern Inverness-shire folklore. The Fairy Glen near Uig, the Fairy Bridge and other named landscapes are now major visitor sites, though not all have equally deep attested oral traditions. IsleofSkye.com describes the Fairy Glen as a strange landslip-created landscape above Uig, while travel writing often layers the place with “myth and wonder”.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.isleofskye.com/skye-guide/skye-places/fairy-glen" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: isleofskye.com">[Isle of Skye]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">isleofskye.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on isleofskye.com.</span></span></span>

## Holy wells, cloots and healing customs

Inverness-shire’s folklore is not only about dramatic creatures. Some of its most persistent traditions are practical: healing, protection, blessing and the management of misfortune. The Munlochy Clootie Well, on the Black Isle near Inverness, is one of the clearest surviving examples.

Forestry and Land Scotland explains the custom plainly: a cloot is a rag dipped in the well and tied to a tree, with the belief that sickness or an ailment will fade as the cloth disintegrates. It also places clootie wells within wider Celtic healing traditions and asks visitors to use small biodegradable cloths rather than leaving damaging material behind.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://forestryandland.gov.scot/visit/destinations/munlochy-clootie-well" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: forestryandland.gov.scot">[Forestry and Land Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">forestryandland.gov.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on gov.scot.</span></span></span>

This is folklore as action rather than story. The visitor does not merely hear a tale; they perform a gesture. The cloth becomes a visible sign of hope, illness, prayer, transfer or offering. Because modern visitors continue to leave cloots, the site is not only an antiquarian survival but a living, if contested, tradition.

The Inverness area also has a wider pattern of healing wells. Local accounts list springs associated with cures for cholera, skin conditions, weak eyes and general ailments, including wells near Dochgarroch, Bunchrew, Clachnaharry and Culloden. These traditions should be treated as belief history rather than medical advice, but they show how water, saints, coins, washing and bodily cure were once closely linked in the local imagination.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inverness" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wikipedia.org.</span></span></span>

The Munlochy well also raises a modern ethical question: what happens when a healing custom becomes environmental litter? That tension is now part of the folklore itself. A practice rooted in decay and disappearance changes when synthetic fabrics, plastic objects and mass tourism enter the ritual landscape.

## Stones, cairns and prehistoric mystery near Inverness

Prehistoric monuments often attract supernatural explanation because they are ancient, visible and not fully explained by written records. Near Inverness, Clava Cairns is the strongest example. Historic Environment Scotland describes the site as about 4,000 years old, built to house the dead, and part of a sacred landscape that remained significant for millennia.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.historicenvironment.scot/visit/all/clava-cairns/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: historicenvironment.scot">[Historic Environment Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">historicenvironment.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on historicenvironment.scot.</span></span></span>

The cairns are not “folklore” in the same way as a fairy tale or ghost story. They are archaeological monuments. Yet they feed folklore because they invite questions: who built them, why were they aligned as they were, what rituals took place there, and why do standing stones feel so charged to later visitors? Historic Environment Scotland notes that the Clava Cairns are the type-site for around fifty similar cairns found only in the Moray Firth and Inverness region, combining ring cairns, passage graves and stone circles in a distinctive local form.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.historicenvironment.scot/visit/all/clava-cairns/history-and-stories/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: historicenvironment.scot">[Historic Environment Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">historicenvironment.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on historicenvironment.scot.</span></span></span>

Modern popular culture has added another layer. VisitScotland points out that many visitors now recognise Clava Cairns through *Outlander*, where the idea of standing stones as thresholds into another time has become a powerful tourist association.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.visitscotland.com/things-to-do/attractions/historic/standing-stones" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: visitscotland.com">[VisitScotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">visitscotland.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on visitscotland.com.</span></span></span>

That does not mean Bronze Age Inverness-shire people believed in television-style time travel. It means modern folklore can attach new imaginative meanings to old places. The responsible distinction is simple: Clava Cairns is a real prehistoric burial and ritual landscape; later supernatural interpretations are retellings, responses and fictional overlays.

## Witches, fairies and the danger of belief

Inverness-shire’s witchcraft traditions are darker because they are tied to accusation, law and punishment. The Survey of Scottish Witchcraft, created by University of Edinburgh researchers, records nearly 4,000 people known to have been accused of witchcraft in early modern Scotland.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://witches.hca.ed.ac.uk/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: witches.hca.ed.ac.uk">[witches.hca.ed.ac.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">witches.hca.ed.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on ed.ac.uk.</span></span></span>

One important Inverness case is Isobel Duff, tried for witchcraft at Inverness on 17 July 1662. A modern edition of the trial proceedings highlights the case as a formal legal record, not a fireside legend, and uses it to examine gender expectations within the Scottish witch-hunt.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.euppublishing.com/doi/10.3366/shr.2022.0549" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: euppublishing.com">[EU Publishing]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">euppublishing.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on euppublishing.com.</span></span></span>

Just beyond the strict historic-county focus, the famous Isobel Gowdie confessions at Auldearn near Nairn show why neighbouring material is sometimes relevant. Gowdie’s 1662 confessions include fairy encounters, charms, animal transformation, elf arrows and meetings with the fairy queen and king. The case is Nairnshire rather than Inverness-shire, but it helps interpret the shared Moray Firth belief-world in which witchcraft, fairies, illness, cattle, ministers and local grudges could become dangerously entangled.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isobel_Gowdie" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Isobel Gowdie</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Isobel Gowdie</span></span></span>

The caution for modern readers is important. These records are evidence that people were accused, interrogated and sometimes condemned; they are not evidence that the alleged magic happened. They do, however, reveal what communities feared: spoiled milk, sick children, damaged crops, uncanny animals, fairy contact, curses and women believed to possess hidden power.

## Gaelic collectors and the survival of oral tradition

Much of what readers now call Highland folklore survives because collectors, ministers, excisemen, scholars and later sound archivists wrote it down or recorded it. Alexander Carmichael’s *Carmina Gadelica* is one of the best-known examples. DASG describes it as a six-volume compilation of blessings, chants, prayers and incantations collected throughout the Highlands and Islands in the second half of the nineteenth century.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://dasg.ac.uk/corpus/textmeta.php?text=59004" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: dasg.ac.uk">[dasg.ac.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">dasg.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Carmina Gadelica</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Carmina Gadelica</span></span></span>

Carmichael’s work matters for Inverness-shire because so much of the historic county lay inside Gaelic-speaking regions where blessing, charm, seasonal practice and supernatural belief overlapped. At the same time, modern readers should know that *Carmina Gadelica* is not a neutral audio transcript. It is a shaped literary and devotional collection, admired for preserving material but also debated because Carmichael edited and reworked some of what he gathered.

John Gregorson Campbell’s *Superstitions of the Highlands and Islands of Scotland* is another major source for fairy belief, second sight, household spirits and supernatural customs. Project Gutenberg presents the work as a late nineteenth-century collection rooted in Highland and island oral tradition, especially around beliefs concerning fairies.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/61730" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on gutenberg.org.</span></span></span>

The twentieth century changed the evidence base. Sound archives allow listeners to hear tradition bearers rather than only reading polished literary versions. ## St Kilda and the folklore of extreme place

St Kilda, historically within Inverness-shire through the Outer Hebridean county structure, adds a different kind of folklore: not fairy glamour, but the traditions of an isolated community living with cliffs, seabirds, risk and ritual. The National Trust for Scotland describes a community there for at least 4,000 years, sustained by gannets, fulmars and puffins for food, feathers and oil, until the final 36 residents left in 1930.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nts.org.uk/visit/places/st-kilda" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nts.org.uk">[National Trust for Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nts.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nts.org.uk.</span></span></span>

The folklore of St Kilda is inseparable from its way of life. Fowling skills, communal decision-making, marriage tests, island memory and stories of the surrounding sea all became part of how outsiders understood the archipelago. National Records of Scotland notes that St Kilda is richly documented in archives ranging from early eighteenth-century missionary activity to Scottish Office files about the final evacuation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nrscotland.gov.uk/learning-and-events/all-features-and-articles/stories-from-st-kilda/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nrscotland.gov.uk">[National Records of Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nrscotland.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">stories from st kilda</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">stories from st kilda</span></span></span>

One well-known tradition concerns the “Mistress Stone”, a dangerous opening in the rocks where young men were said to prove themselves on the cliffs. Another concerns St Kilda’s daily “parliament”, the morning meeting of adult men in the village street to decide the day’s work. These are not supernatural in the fairy-tale sense, but they are folk custom: repeated, meaningful actions by which a community explained courage, adulthood, labour and belonging.

St Kilda also shows how romantic retelling can distort hard lives. The island is often imagined as a lost world of purity and endurance. The better reading is more human: a community with its own traditions, skills and stories, but also one shaped by illness, isolation, economic pressure, religious change and outside interference.

## What Inverness-shire folklore means today

Inverness-shire folklore still matters because it gives the county’s landscapes a second life. Loch Ness is not only deep water; it is a stage for monster belief, scepticism and global media. Tomnahurich is not only a cemetery hill; it is a fairy mound in city memory. Dunvegan Castle is not only a clan seat; it houses a flag around which stories of fairy protection have gathered. Munlochy is not only woodland; it is a living healing site. Clava Cairns is not only archaeology; it is a place where modern visitors negotiate the boundary between ancient ritual, fiction and wonder.

The strongest pattern across the county is the attachment of story to threshold places. Hills open into fairy halls. Wells transfer illness. Stones mark the dead and invite later myth. Lochs hide creatures. Islands preserve customs at the edge of the map. Castles turn cloth into clan destiny. These are not random legends scattered across a large county; they are ways of making sense of difficult terrain, uncertain weather, dangerous water, illness, death, ancestry and belonging.

A grounded reader should avoid two mistakes. The first is dismissing everything as “just superstition”, which misses the social memory, oral artistry and place-knowledge embedded in the tales. The second is treating every modern retelling as ancient fact, which blurs the difference between oral tradition, court record, archaeology, literary shaping and tourism. Inverness-shire is most interesting when those layers are kept visible.

## Places and traditions to remember

For a first encounter with Inverness-shire folklore, a few anchors carry most of the county’s story:

**Loch Ness and the River Ness** show the difference between medieval saintly water-beast narrative and the modern monster legend launched by twentieth-century newspapers.

**Tomnahurich in Inverness** preserves the fairy-hill motif in a place still visible in the city landscape.

**Dunvegan on Skye** connects fairy belief to clan memory through the Fairy Flag, a real heirloom with a legendary biography.

**Munlochy Clootie Well** keeps alive a healing custom in which cloth, water, illness and offering meet.

**Clava Cairns near Culloden** shows how prehistoric ritual landscapes become magnets for later mystery, fiction and visitor imagination.

**St Kilda** widens the county’s folklore beyond monsters and fairies into communal custom, oral memory and the traditions of life at the Atlantic edge.

Together, these places make Inverness-shire one of the most folklore-rich historic counties in the UK project: a county where the supernatural is not confined to one famous monster, but spread through roads, wells, hills, stones, castles, islands and remembered voices.
