## Introduction

[Norfolk]({{ 'why-norfolks-legends-still-haunt-the/' | relative_url }})’s Lantern Men are the Broads’ most dangerous version of the will-o’-the-wisp: strange moving lights seen over marsh, reed bed and wet meadow after dark, said to lure travellers off safe ground and into water or sucking mud. In the stories, the light is not merely a natural flicker. It may belong to a small figure, a ghostly man, or a malicious marsh spirit, and the warning is always practical as well as supernatural: do not follow lights across the marsh at night. The best-known Norfolk case is Joseph Bexfield, a wherryman associated with Thurlton, who drowned in 1809 and whose death was later folded into Lantern Man tradition. His grave records the human fact of the drowning; folklore supplies the eerie explanation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://norfolkfolkloresociety.substack.com/p/norfolk-folklore-society-february-c2d" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: norfolkfolkloresociety.substack.com">[norfolkfolkloresociety.substack.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">norfolkfolkloresociety.substack.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">norfolk folklore society february c2d</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">White Horse Inn at Thurlton Staithe to retrieve a parcel for her... The tale matters because it turns the Broads’ real dangers into memorable story. Fog, darkness, flooded peat workings, hidden channels, reed beds, mud and tidal rivers all become part of a warning system. In Norfolk folklore, the Lantern Men are not decorative fairies. They are the marsh itself made watchful, deceptive and hostile.

## Marsh Lights and Will-o’-the-Wisp Traditions

The Lantern Men belong to a wider family of marsh-light traditions usually called will-o’-the-wisp, Jack o’Lantern, ignis fatuus or “foolish fire”. Across Britain and Europe, these lights are described as flickering flames or lantern-like glows seen over bogs, fens, swamps and graveyards, especially by travellers at night. Folklore commonly says they mislead people from the path; scientific explanations have often pointed to gases, phosphorescence, bioluminescence or other low-light phenomena associated with decaying organic matter and wet ground.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Will-o%27-the-wisp" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wikipedia.org.</span></span></span>

Norfolk’s version is sharper than the generic image of a wandering flame. Local retellings often make the light personal. It is carried by “Lantern Men” who stand or move in the darkness, swinging blue-green lights over the marsh, tempting the observer to leave the safe way and walk towards them. This is why the tradition feels so well suited to the Broads: the danger is not just that a person sees something odd, but that they misread it as a human light, a waymarker, a boatman, a cottage lamp or someone in need.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.theshoebox.org.uk/the-shoebox-experiences/norfolk-a-land-of-folklore/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theshoebox.org.uk">[The Shoebox Enterprises]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theshoebox.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Shoebox Enterprises Norfolk, A Land of Folklore</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Shoebox Enterprises Norfolk, A Land of Folklore</span></span></span>

The Broads version also overlaps with East Anglian Fenland belief beyond Norfolk. In older East Norfolk material, “Will of the Wisp”, “Jack of Lanterns”, “Lantern Jack” and “Lantern Man” are treated as closely related names. One 19th-century East Anglian passage says the Lantern Man was believed to haunt the “hundred and one fens and broads”, follow wayfarers home, light up windows from outside and grow angry if someone crossed the meadows at night with a lantern. That detail is important: the Lantern Man is not only a light that leads people away. He polices the night landscape and punishes those who presume they can carry their own light through it.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/stream/historyofeastang00hood/historyofeastang00hood_djvu.txt" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on archive.org.</span></span></span>

This is where the folklore becomes a mechanism rather than a monster story. The tale teaches a set of behaviours: avoid marsh crossings after dark; distrust lights that are not where a light should be; do not whistle, mock or challenge what you see; and do not assume local skill makes you safe. Those rules are supernatural in form, but they are rooted in real environmental risk.

## Joseph Bexfield and Thurlton Marsh

The most memorable Norfolk Lantern Man story centres on Joseph Bexfield of Thurlton. The core of the tale is simple. Bexfield was a wherryman, one of the men who worked the sailing cargo boats of the Broads. One August night in 1809, after drinking with other boatmen at the White Horse Inn near Thurlton Staithe, he remembered a parcel or errand connected with his wife and decided to return across the marsh towards his wherry. His companions warned him not to go because the Lantern Men were abroad. He went anyway, and he did not return alive.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.countryfile.com/people/historical-figures/terrifying-lantern-men" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: countryfile.com">[countryfile.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">countryfile.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The terrifying tale of the Lantern Men, evil pixies who</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The terrifying tale of the Lantern Men, evil pixies who</span></span></span>

The historical anchor is his death, not proof of the supernatural event. A photographed record of Joseph Bexfield’s grave at Thurlton identifies him as a wherryman who drowned on 11 August 1809, aged 38, leaving a widow and two children. Modern folklore accounts preserve the verse said to have been on the headstone, mourning the loss of a “Father kind and Husband dear”. That grave gives the story weight: even if the Lantern Men are legendary, the drowned man was not invented from nothing.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.geograph.org.uk/photo/2985219" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: geograph.org.uk">[Geograph]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">geograph.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Joseph Bexfield&#x27;s grave, Thurlton © Evelyn Simak9 Jun 2012 — The headstone marks the grave of wherry man Joseph Bexfield, who dro...</span></span></span>

The wherryman detail matters. Wherries were the working boats of the Norfolk and Suffolk Broads, designed for shallow waters and narrow channels, with a distinctive high-peaked sail and mast set well forward. Trading wherries developed from the early modern period and by the 19th century were central to moving goods through Broadland. Bexfield was therefore not imagined as a foolish outsider. He belonged to the water. In folklore terms, that makes the warning more severe: if even a skilled wherryman could be lost on a misty marsh night, ordinary travellers had even less reason to trust their judgement.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.wherryyachtcharter.org/history-of-the-norfolk-wherries" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wherryyachtcharter.org">[wherryyachtcharter.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wherryyachtcharter.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wherryyachtcharter.org.</span></span></span>

Later retellings vary in detail. Some say his body was found downstream; some place the recovery after a search lasting several days; some say his ghost still appears in August, trying to find the way home. The safest reading is that a real drowning became a local cautionary tale, and that the Lantern Men supplied a culturally familiar explanation for a death that felt shocking because of Bexfield’s experience on the waterways.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://crimereads.com/why-marshes-capture-our-imaginations-and-inspire-some-of-our-most-unsettling-folklore/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: crimereads.com">[crimereads.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">crimereads.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on crimereads.com.</span></span></span>

## Why the Broads Made the Story Believable

The Broads are not simply a scenic background for the Lantern Men. They are the reason the tradition works. The modern Broads landscape was shaped by medieval peat digging; the abandoned pits flooded by the 14th century, creating shallow lakes, waterways, reed beds and wetland habitats. The Broads Authority describes this as a partly man-made wetland, not a set of natural lakes. That history gives the area a strange double character: it is natural-looking, but also cut, drained, flooded and managed by human labour over centuries.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.broads-authority.gov.uk/learning/for-students/history" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: broads-authority.gov.uk">[Broads Authority]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">broads-authority.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on broads-authority.gov.uk.</span></span></span>

Such a landscape is full of thresholds. A path may run beside water; firm-looking ground may soften; mist may erase distance; a light may seem near when it is not. Reed beds and dykes break sightlines. Rivers such as the Yare connect inland Norfolk to Great Yarmouth and the coast, so the Broads are both a working route and a place where a wrong step can isolate a person quickly. The Lantern Men compress all of that into one image: a light that looks like guidance but is actually danger.

The setting also explains why the tale is especially Norfolk in feeling even though will-o’-the-wisp traditions are widespread. Norfolk’s folklore repeatedly attaches supernatural warning to landscape: [Black Shuck]({{ 'black-shuck/' | relative_url }}) on roads and churchyards, ghost lights over marsh, drowned figures by rivers, and strange presences in places where travel becomes uncertain. The Lantern Men are the Broads’ answer to the same question: what does danger look like before it becomes visible as danger?

## How Old and Well-Attested Is the Tradition?

The Lantern Men are best treated as a layered tradition. The broad motif of misleading marsh lights is old and widespread, but the specific Norfolk form is clearest in later antiquarian, local-history and modern folklore retellings. The 19th-century East Anglian reference to “Jack of Lanterns”, “Lantern Jack” and “Lantern Man” in East Norfolk is valuable because it shows that the figure was not purely a recent internet invention. It also records behavioural details: the Lantern Man follows people home, lights windows from outside and attacks or abuses a traveller’s own lantern.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/stream/historyofeastang00hood/historyofeastang00hood_djvu.txt" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on archive.org.</span></span></span>

Joseph Bexfield’s drowning gives the Norfolk branch a named case with a date, place and grave. However, the surviving evidence does not prove that people in 1809 immediately explained his death in exactly the form now retold. The grave attests the drowning; later folklore sources and local retellings attach the Lantern Men to it. That distinction matters because it keeps the article honest without draining the story of meaning. Folklore often works by fastening older motifs to remembered deaths, paths, pubs, churches and watersides.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.geograph.org.uk/photo/2985219" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: geograph.org.uk">[geograph.org.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">geograph.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Joseph Bexfield&#x27;s grave, Thurlton © Evelyn Simak9 Jun 2012 — The headstone marks the grave of wherry man Joseph Bexfield, who dro...</span></span></span>

The tradition is now preserved by local folklore writers, Norfolk heritage storytelling, national-park visitor material and modern popular culture. The National Parks website presents the Broads’ Jack o’Lantern stories as part of the area’s living folklore and specifically mentions Bexfield’s 1809 drowning at Thurlton Marsh. Norfolk Folklore Society retellings keep the warning formula alive: whatever name is used for the light, one thing is certain — do not follow it into the marsh.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nationalparks.uk/2024/09/30/discover-folklore-of-the-broads/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nationalparks.uk">[National Parks]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nationalparks.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Parks Discover: Folklore of the Broads</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Parks Discover: Folklore of the Broads</span></span></span>

## What the Lantern Men Were Warning About

The Lantern Men are frightening because they turn a survival rule into a story people remember. A plain warning — “do not cross the marsh in fog after dark” — is useful but forgettable. A tale of malicious lights that lure even confident boatmen to their deaths is harder to ignore.

The warning has several layers:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--caution" markdown="1">

* **Environmental danger:** marshes, wet meadows, reed beds and river margins can conceal water, mud and sudden drops, especially in darkness or mist.
* **False guidance:** a moving light may look like a person, boat, house or safe route, but in folklore it leads away from safety.
* **Overconfidence:** Bexfield’s role as a wherryman makes the story a warning against trusting familiarity too much.
* **Night behaviour:** whistling, mocking, carrying a lantern across the meadows or chasing a light all become risky acts in the story-world.
* **Respect for place:** the tale says the Broads are not passive scenery. They must be read carefully, and sometimes avoided.

</div>

This is why the Lantern Men sit so naturally beside other Norfolk legends without becoming interchangeable with them. Black Shuck warns on lanes, coasts and church paths; the Lantern Men warn from marsh and water. Both traditions make the county’s geography feel morally charged, but the Lantern Men are specifically about being drawn off course.

## The Broads as a Threshold Landscape

The Broads are often enjoyed today as a place of boating holidays, wildlife, riverside pubs and wide skies. The Lantern Men preserve an older and darker memory of the same environment: the working marsh at night, crossed by people who knew the waterways but still had to reckon with weather, water level, poor visibility and isolation. That older memory is not cancelled by tourism. It gives the modern landscape depth.

In folklore terms, the Broads are a threshold landscape: neither fully land nor fully water, neither wild nor untouched, neither inland nor coastal. Medieval peat workings became flooded lakes; trading routes became leisure waterways; working wherries became museum pieces and heritage symbols. The Lantern Men belong to that in-between quality. They appear where categories blur: light and darkness, path and water, guide and trap, human figure and natural phenomenon.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.broads-authority.gov.uk/learning/for-students/history" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: broads-authority.gov.uk">[Broads Authority]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">broads-authority.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on broads-authority.gov.uk.</span></span></span>

Modern retellings keep returning to Bexfield because his story gives the tradition a human centre. Without him, the Lantern Men might be only a class of ghost lights. With him, they become a Norfolk cautionary tale about a named man, a named village, a vanished inn, a working boat culture and a dangerous walk across marsh in August 1809. The supernatural claim remains a tradition, not a fact; the emotional truth is that the Broads could kill, and local storytelling found a way to make that danger visible.

## How the Story Is Retold Today

Today the Lantern Men appear in heritage writing, folklore blogs, walking-and-visitor material, fiction and local mystery culture. The Broads’ official and semi-official visitor ecosystem presents Jack o’Lanterns and marsh lights as part of the area’s folklore, while writers continue to retell Bexfield’s death as one of the county’s most atmospheric legends. Modern crime and historical fiction have also borrowed the image because it is instantly legible: a lonely wetland, a bobbing light, and the fatal mistake of following it.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nationalparks.uk/2024/09/30/discover-folklore-of-the-broads/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nationalparks.uk">[nationalparks.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nationalparks.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Parks Discover: Folklore of the Broads</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Parks Discover: Folklore of the Broads</span></span></span>

That popularity can blur the evidence. Some online versions make the Lantern Men sound like a fully fixed race of evil pixies; others present them as ghosts, marsh spirits, fairy lights, death lights or the souls of the drowned. The older material is looser. It shows a cluster of names and behaviours around mysterious lights in East Norfolk and the wider East Anglian wetlands. The most grounded way to understand the tradition is not to force a single monster profile, but to see the Lantern Men as Norfolk’s local shape for a wider marsh-light fear.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/stream/historyofeastang00hood/historyofeastang00hood_djvu.txt" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[archive.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on archive.org.</span></span></span>

Their lasting power comes from that flexibility. They can be explained as marsh gas, remembered as fairies, feared as ghosts, used in fiction, or interpreted as safety folklore. Yet the core message hardly changes: the Broads are beautiful, but they are not harmless; not every light is a guide; and in Norfolk’s marsh country, the path you do not leave may be the thing that saves you.
