## Introduction

[Anglesey]({{ 'why-anglesey-feels-so-full-of-legends/' | relative_url }})’s reputation as the island of Druids rests on one of the most dramatic passages in Roman writing about Britain. In AD 60 or 61, the Roman governor Suetonius Paulinus attacked Mona, the island generally identified with Anglesey, and Tacitus described a terrifying shore scene of armed Britons, torch-bearing women and Druids calling down curses before the Roman army broke through and cut down sacred groves. The account is not folklore in the simple sense of an old fireside tale: it is a Roman conquest story, later absorbed into Welsh historical imagination, antiquarian writing, tourism and local identity. Its power lies in that mixture. Tacitus gives Anglesey a rare ancient spotlight, archaeology adds real evidence for Iron Age ritual activity, and later writers turned Mona into one of the most mythic landscapes in Wales.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.poetryintranslation.com/PITBR/Latin/AnnalsBookXIV-1to39.php" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: poetryintranslation.com">[Poetry In Translation]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">poetryintranslation.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Poetry In Translation Tacitus, Publius Cornelius (c.56–c.120</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Poetry In TranslationTacitus, Publius Cornelius (c.56–c.120) - The Annals: Book XIV, I-XXXIX...</span></span></span>

The careful answer is this: Anglesey was probably an important late Iron Age religious and political landscape, and Roman writers did associate Mona with Druids, resistance and sacred groves. But the famous invasion scene is filtered through Roman literary habits, imperial propaganda and Tacitus’s own dramatic purpose. It should be read as a powerful historic memory and identity-making legend, not as a straightforward eyewitness photograph of “the last Druids of Britain”.

## Tacitus and the Roman invasion story

Tacitus is the key source for the Roman attack legend. In his account of the year AD 61, Suetonius Paulinus is campaigning in Britain and prepares to attack Mona, described as densely inhabited and a refuge for fugitives. Tacitus says the Romans used flat-bottomed boats for the infantry, while cavalry either waded or swam their horses through deeper water. This is already enough to make the story memorable: the Roman army is crossing a difficult tidal frontier into an island portrayed as both militarily and spiritually dangerous.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.poetryintranslation.com/PITBR/Latin/AnnalsBookXIV-1to39.php" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: poetryintranslation.com">[Poetry In Translation]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">poetryintranslation.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Poetry In Translation Tacitus, Publius Cornelius (c.56–c.120</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Poetry In TranslationTacitus, Publius Cornelius (c.56–c.120) - The Annals: Book XIV, I-XXXIX...</span></span></span>

The famous scene comes when the Romans reach the shore. Tacitus describes the opposing force as a mass of armed men, with women moving among them in black clothing, loose hair and torches, while Druids raise their hands and pour out curses. The Roman soldiers are said to freeze in fear until their commander and one another urge them on; then they advance, kill the defenders and burn the enemy with their own flames. After the victory, Tacitus says a garrison was imposed and groves connected with what he calls savage rites were destroyed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.poetryintranslation.com/PITBR/Latin/AnnalsBookXIV-1to39.php" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: poetryintranslation.com">[Poetry In Translation]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">poetryintranslation.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Poetry In Translation Tacitus, Publius Cornelius (c.56–c.120</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Poetry In TranslationTacitus, Publius Cornelius (c.56–c.120) - The Annals: Book XIV, I-XXXIX...</span></span></span>

For a folklore reader, the most important point is not simply “did it happen exactly like that?” but why the passage became so durable. It gives Anglesey a complete legendary image in a few lines: a sacred island, a hostile sea crossing, women like avenging spirits, Druids calling to the heavens, sacred woods felled by conquerors, and the sudden interruption of victory by the Boudican revolt elsewhere in Britain. Tacitus’s next sentence says that news arrived of a revolt in the rest of the province, linking Mona directly with the wider crisis of Roman Britain.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.poetryintranslation.com/PITBR/Latin/AnnalsBookXIV-1to39.php" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: poetryintranslation.com">[Poetry In Translation]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">poetryintranslation.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Poetry In Translation Tacitus, Publius Cornelius (c.56–c.120</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Poetry In TranslationTacitus, Publius Cornelius (c.56–c.120) - The Annals: Book XIV, I-XXXIX...</span></span></span>

That timing matters. The attack on Mona was not the clean ending of a campaign. According to Tacitus, Suetonius had to leave the island operation because Boudica’s revolt had erupted in eastern Britain. In legendary terms, that gives the story a strange double movement: Rome appears to smash the Druidic stronghold, but the province almost explodes behind it. Anglesey becomes, in later imagination, not just a place conquered by Rome but a place whose resistance belongs to the same charged moment as one of Britain’s most famous anti-Roman uprisings.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.poetryintranslation.com/PITBR/Latin/TacitusAgricola.php" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: poetryintranslation.com">[Poetry In Translation]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">poetryintranslation.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Poetry In Translation Tacitus, Publius Cornelius (c.56–c.120</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Poetry In TranslationTacitus, Publius Cornelius (c.56–c.120) - The Agricola...</span></span></span>

## How much of the Druid scene can we trust?

Tacitus is indispensable, but he is not a neutral local reporter. He was a Roman senator writing imperial history, and his account of Druids, women and “savage rites” belongs to a Roman literary world that often portrayed conquered peoples as frightening, exotic or morally extreme. That does not mean the whole episode is invented. It does mean the details need weighing carefully.

Several parts of the story are historically plausible. Mona was strategically important: an island off north-west Wales, difficult to attack without careful planning, and close to territories resisting Roman rule. Tacitus also separately says in the *Agricola* that Suetonius advanced towards Mona because it harboured rebel forces. The idea of the island as a refuge therefore comes from more than one Tacitean passage, though both are still from the same Roman authorial tradition.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.poetryintranslation.com/PITBR/Latin/TacitusAgricola.php" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: poetryintranslation.com">[Poetry In Translation]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">poetryintranslation.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Poetry In Translation Tacitus, Publius Cornelius (c.56–c.120</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Poetry In TranslationTacitus, Publius Cornelius (c.56–c.120) - The Agricola...</span></span></span>

Other parts are harder to verify. We do not have a British Druid’s account of the event. We do not know exactly where Suetonius crossed, where the defenders stood, what the sacred groves were like, or whether the women on the shore were priestesses, relatives, ritual performers, warriors, mourners, or a Romanised literary image of barbarian frenzy. Tacitus’s description of human sacrifice and entrail divination also fits wider Roman accusations against Druids and other enemies. It may preserve something real about Iron Age ritual violence, but it is written in the language of Roman moral disgust.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.poetryintranslation.com/PITBR/Latin/AnnalsBookXIV-1to39.php" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: poetryintranslation.com">[Poetry In Translation]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">poetryintranslation.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Poetry In Translation Tacitus, Publius Cornelius (c.56–c.120</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Poetry In TranslationTacitus, Publius Cornelius (c.56–c.120) - The Annals: Book XIV, I-XXXIX...</span></span></span>

The best reading is therefore balanced. Tacitus gives strong evidence that Roman elite memory associated Mona with Druids, resistance and sacred places. He does not prove every later claim made about Anglesey’s Druid temples, nor does he allow modern readers to map the battle scene neatly onto every prehistoric monument on the island. The legend is powerful because it sits at the edge between history and myth: enough ancient testimony to matter, enough uncertainty to invite centuries of retelling.

## The second Roman attack and the unfinished conquest

The Mona story did not end with Suetonius. Tacitus’s *Agricola* describes a later operation under Gnaeus Julius Agricola, usually placed in AD 77 or 78, after the Ordovices of north Wales had attacked Roman forces. Agricola defeated them and then decided to reduce Mona, “from whose conquest Paulinus had been recalled” by the wider rebellion. This second account is less theatrical than the Druid scene, but it matters because it shows that the first attack had not settled the island permanently.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.poetryintranslation.com/PITBR/Latin/TacitusAgricola.php" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: poetryintranslation.com">[Poetry In Translation]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">poetryintranslation.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Poetry In Translation Tacitus, Publius Cornelius (c.56–c.120</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Poetry In TranslationTacitus, Publius Cornelius (c.56–c.120) - The Agricola...</span></span></span>

Agricola’s crossing is a different kind of legend: not torches and curses, but tactical surprise. Tacitus says Agricola lacked a fleet, so he chose auxiliaries who knew the shallows and could manage themselves, their weapons and their horses in the crossing. The defenders, expecting ships and a formal sea assault, were astonished by the sudden attack and surrendered the island.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.poetryintranslation.com/PITBR/Latin/TacitusAgricola.php" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: poetryintranslation.com">[Poetry In Translation]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">poetryintranslation.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Poetry In Translation Tacitus, Publius Cornelius (c.56–c.120</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Poetry In TranslationTacitus, Publius Cornelius (c.56–c.120) - The Agricola...</span></span></span>

This second Mona episode is often overshadowed by the more famous Druid attack, but it changes the story in an important way. It suggests that Anglesey’s Roman conquest was a process, not a single cinematic massacre. In public memory, Suetonius’s attack supplies the mythic image; Agricola’s campaign supplies the consolidation. Together they make Mona a place where Rome had to come back.

Modern archaeology also supports the idea that Roman Anglesey was not simply erased or transformed overnight. Research summarised in *Britannia* discusses Roman-period evidence from Tai Cochion near the Menai Strait, a fortlet at Cemlyn Bay and a watchtower at Pen Bryn-yr-Eglwys, giving a more grounded picture of military control, coastal watching and settlement after the conquest.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/britannia/article/roman-anglesey-recent-discoveries/D3479D01FBE7952116F7E31DE182618F" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridge.org">[Cambridge University Press &amp; Assessment]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cambridge.org.</span></span></span> These sites do not prove Tacitus’s Druid scene, but they help move the story from pure literary drama into a real occupied island landscape.

## Llyn Cerrig Bach and ritual archaeology

If Tacitus gives Anglesey its Roman attack legend, Llyn Cerrig Bach gives it some of its strongest archaeological texture. The site, near Valley in western Anglesey, produced one of the great Iron Age metalwork deposits in Wales. Museum Wales describes chariots, weapons, tools and decorated metalwork being placed between about 300 BC and AD 100 into a small lake, from a causeway or island.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://museum.wales/blog/1343/Ancient-druids-of-Wales/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: museum.wales">[Museum Wales]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">museum.wales</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Wales Ancient druids of Wales &#124; Museum Wales</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Ancient druids of Wales &#124; Museum Wales...</span></span></span> Cadw teaching material similarly notes more than 150 recovered items, including swords, spearheads, cauldrons, slave chains, blacksmiths’ tongs and a decorated bronze plaque, interpreted as possible offerings to the gods.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cadw.gov.wales/sites/default/files/2019-05/151214-prehistoricresource-eng.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cadw.gov.wales">[Cadw]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cadw.gov.wales</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Cadw Prehistoric Wales Teacher Information Pack</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cadw Prehistoric Wales Teacher Information Pack</span></span></span>

This is the crucial distinction: Llyn Cerrig Bach is not “the battlefield” named by Tacitus, and it is not direct proof that Druids stood there during the Roman attack. What it does show is that Anglesey had a major late Iron Age watery ritual site in roughly the period that matters for the Roman story. That makes the island’s religious reputation far more than a romantic invention, even if the word “Druid” cannot be attached to every object with certainty.

The character of the finds is especially suggestive. Museum Wales material notes that items such as military equipment, a trumpet and gang chains have raised the question of whether Llyn Cerrig Bach could have been a major focus of Druidic ritual, while also explaining that reassessment points towards repeated deposits over time rather than one single dramatic dumping event.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://museum.wales/media/8536/iron_age_text.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: museum.wales">[Museum Wales]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">museum.wales</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Wales Iron Age Teachers&#x27; Pack Text</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wales Iron Age Teachers&#x27; Pack Text</span></span></span> This matters because folklore often likes one great moment: the last stand, the final sacrifice, the treasures hurled into the lake before Rome arrives. The archaeology points to something slower and stranger: repeated acts of offering, memory and display across generations.

The finds also complicate the old picture of Druids as only white-robed sages wandering among oaks. The material world around late Iron Age ritual could include weapons, vehicle fittings, tools, chains, decorated bronze and deliberately damaged objects. Museum Wales notes that some swords from Llyn Cerrig Bach appear to have been deliberately bent, a practice sometimes interpreted as the “killing” of weapons before deposition.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://museum.wales/media/8536/iron_age_text.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: museum.wales">[Museum Wales]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">museum.wales</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Wales Iron Age Teachers&#x27; Pack Text</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wales Iron Age Teachers&#x27; Pack Text</span></span></span> For a reader standing in Anglesey today, that gives the Mona legend a sharper landscape feel: not just misty groves, but metal, water, violence, craft and offerings.

## Why later writers made Mona the Druid island

Anglesey’s Druidic identity did not survive only because of Tacitus. It was repeatedly remade by antiquarians, Welsh cultural revival, museum interpretation and popular history. One of the most important figures was Henry Rowlands, an Anglesey antiquary whose *Mona Antiqua Restaurata* was published in 1723. The Dictionary of Welsh Biography says the work listed the island’s relics and tried to prove that Anglesey was the chief seat of the Druids.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://biography.wales/article/s-ROWL-HEN-1655.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: biography.wales">[Welsh Biography]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">biography.wales</span><span class="citation-popover-title">s ROWL HEN 1655</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">s ROWL HEN 1655</span></span></span> Museum Wales also identifies the book by its revealing subtitle: an archaeological discourse on the antiquities, natural and historical, of Anglesey as “the Antient Seat of the British Druids.”<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://museum.wales/blog/2017-08-04/Mona-Antiqua-Restaurata-by-Henry-Rowlands/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: museum.wales">[Museum Wales]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">museum.wales</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Wales Mona Antiqua Restaurata by Henry Rowlands &#124; Museum Wales</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wales Mona Antiqua Restaurata by Henry Rowlands &#124; Museum Wales</span></span></span>

Rowlands matters because he helped turn scattered ancient remains into a Druidic map. In the early eighteenth century, many prehistoric monuments were still poorly dated. Stone circles, chambered tombs and standing stones were often drawn into Druid theories, even when modern archaeology would place some of them thousands of years before the historically attested Druids. Museum Wales makes this correction clearly: earlier writers associated megalithic monuments with Druid temples and altars, but nineteenth-century archaeological advances showed that monuments such as Stonehenge, and by extension many comparable prehistoric monuments, were much older than the first historical references to Druids.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://museum.wales/articles/1016/Ancient-druids-of-Wales/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: museum.wales">[Museum Wales]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">museum.wales</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Ancient druids of Wales... The alleged Druidical Temple of Tre&#x27;r Dryw (Anglesey) - prepared by the Revd Henry Rowlands (1723). Druidic ce...</span></span></span>

This does not make the antiquarian tradition worthless. It is part of the folklore history of Anglesey itself. Rowlands and later writers shaped how people imagined the island: as a sacred archive, a place where every ancient stone might be a Druidic remnant, every grove a lost sanctuary, every old name a clue. Modern readers need to separate that imaginative layer from prehistoric dating, but they should not ignore it. The “Druidic Mona” known to visitors today is partly Iron Age religion, partly Roman fear, partly eighteenth-century scholarship, and partly Welsh mythic self-fashioning.

## What the legend attaches to in the Anglesey landscape

The Roman attack legend is unusually place-bound, even when its exact locations remain uncertain. It attaches first to the Menai Strait, the tidal border between mainland north Wales and Anglesey. Tacitus’s descriptions of crossings, boats, shallows, swimming horses and surprised defenders make the strait more than a strip of water: it becomes the threshold between Roman Britain and sacred Mona.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.poetryintranslation.com/PITBR/Latin/AnnalsBookXIV-1to39.php" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: poetryintranslation.com">[Poetry In Translation]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">poetryintranslation.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Poetry In Translation Tacitus, Publius Cornelius (c.56–c.120</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Poetry In TranslationTacitus, Publius Cornelius (c.56–c.120) - The Annals: Book XIV, I-XXXIX...</span></span></span>

It also attaches to imagined groves. Tacitus says sacred groves were cut down after the Roman victory, but no particular surviving woodland can be securely identified as “the” grove from the account. This is where folklore and heritage imagination often fill the gap. The lost grove becomes a symbol rather than a mappable site: the place where old religion, native resistance and Roman violence meet.

Llyn Cerrig Bach gives the story a different kind of anchor: not the grove of Tacitus, but a real lake deposit with Iron Age ritual significance. Because Museum Wales explicitly notes that Tacitus’s account has led some people to infer a Druidic connection for Llyn Cerrig Bach, the site stands at the boundary between archaeology and legend.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://museum.wales/blog/1343/Ancient-druids-of-Wales/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: museum.wales">[Museum Wales]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">museum.wales</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Wales Ancient druids of Wales &#124; Museum Wales</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Ancient druids of Wales &#124; Museum Wales...</span></span></span> It is not a simple illustration of the Roman battle, but it is one of the strongest reasons Anglesey’s Druidic reputation continues to feel materially grounded.

Finally, the legend attaches to the island as a whole. Anglesey is not just a backdrop. Its separateness, sea crossings, ancient monuments and later Welsh name recognition all help Mona function as a mythic island: close enough to be invaded, distant enough to feel like a last refuge. That is why the story works so well in county folklore. It turns geography into drama.

## What is old tradition, and what is later retelling?

A useful way to read Druidic Mona is to separate the layers without stripping away the atmosphere.

The oldest surviving layer is Roman literary testimony. Tacitus gives the first major account of the attack on Mona and the destruction of sacred groves, then later describes Agricola’s renewed conquest of the island. These are ancient sources, but they are Roman elite texts with political and literary purposes.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.poetryintranslation.com/PITBR/Latin/AnnalsBookXIV-1to39.php" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: poetryintranslation.com">[Poetry In Translation]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">poetryintranslation.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Poetry In Translation Tacitus, Publius Cornelius (c.56–c.120</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Poetry In TranslationTacitus, Publius Cornelius (c.56–c.120) - The Annals: Book XIV, I-XXXIX...</span></span></span>

The archaeological layer is material rather than narrative. Llyn Cerrig Bach shows that Anglesey had important Iron Age ritual deposition, with high-status metalwork and repeated offerings over time. It supports the idea of a religiously significant island landscape, but it does not name Druids or confirm Tacitus’s set-piece beach scene.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://museum.wales/blog/1343/Ancient-druids-of-Wales/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: museum.wales">[Museum Wales]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">museum.wales</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Wales Ancient druids of Wales &#124; Museum Wales</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Ancient druids of Wales &#124; Museum Wales...</span></span></span>

The antiquarian layer belongs especially to the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, when writers such as Henry Rowlands tried to identify Anglesey as the chief seat of the Druids and interpreted monuments through that lens. This preserved and popularised Mona’s Druidic fame, but it also encouraged overconfident identifications that modern archaeology would treat cautiously.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://biography.wales/article/s-ROWL-HEN-1655.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: biography.wales">[Welsh Biography]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">biography.wales</span><span class="citation-popover-title">s ROWL HEN 1655</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">s ROWL HEN 1655</span></span></span>

The modern folklore and tourism layer is the one many visitors meet first. It retells Anglesey as an island of Druids, Roman soldiers, sacred lakes, ancient stones and lost rites. At its best, this retelling is not fake history; it is a living way of organising real but uneven evidence into a memorable landscape story. At its weakest, it flattens all ancient monuments into “Druid temples” and turns Tacitus’s hostile Roman prose into unquestioned local fact.

## Why Druidic Mona still matters to Anglesey

Druidic Mona matters because it gives Anglesey one of the strongest origin stories in Welsh legendary geography. Many counties have haunted houses, fairy hills or saintly wells; Anglesey has those too, but it also has a Roman-era myth of national scale. The island becomes a place where Britain’s old religion is imagined facing empire at the water’s edge.

The story also matters because it teaches readers how folklore and history blend. The Roman attack was a historical campaign, but the version that lives in public imagination is shaped by Tacitus’s drama, archaeological discoveries, antiquarian enthusiasm and modern heritage storytelling. That makes it a perfect Anglesey legend: not wholly invented, not wholly recoverable, and deeply attached to place.

It also gives the island a bridge between different kinds of local tradition. Druidic Mona sits naturally beside Anglesey’s chambered tomb legends, standing stones, holy wells and saintly landscapes, but it should not be collapsed into them. A Neolithic tomb is not automatically Druidic; a later holy well is not part of the Roman attack story; a modern bardic ceremony is not direct Iron Age survival. Yet all these traditions show how Anglesey has repeatedly been read as sacred ground.

The most honest version of the legend is also the most interesting. Anglesey was not simply “the island where the Druids were wiped out”, as a dramatic tourist slogan might suggest. It was a complex Iron Age island, a refuge and target in Roman military writing, a place with striking ritual deposits, an object of antiquarian fascination, and a continuing symbol of Welsh depth, resistance and mystery. That layered Mona is far richer than the simplified myth.

      .map(function (button) { return button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button'); })
      .filter(function (marketId) { return !!markets[marketId]; });
  }
  function applyMarket(section, marketId, persist) {
    var available = availableMarkets(section);
    if (available.indexOf(marketId)< 0) marketId = available[0] || defaultMarket;
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-localized-link]')).forEach(function (link) {
      var query = link.getAttribute('data-ebay-query') || '';
      var reference = link.getAttribute('data-ebay-reference') || '';
      link.setAttribute('href', buildUrl(query, reference, marketId));
    });
    var select = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-select]');
    if (select && available.indexOf(marketId) >= 0) select.value = marketId;
    var selectedText = marketId;
    if (select && select.selectedIndex >= 0) selectedText = select.options[select.selectedIndex].text;
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-selected-market-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = selectedText;
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-selected-market-flag], [data-ebay-trigger-market-flag]')).forEach(function (flag) {
      flag.className = flagClass(marketId);
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-trigger-market-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = selectedText;
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-domain-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = domainLabel(marketId);
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-option]')).forEach(function (button) {
      button.setAttribute('aria-selected', button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-option') === marketId ? 'true' : 'false');
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      var active = button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button') === marketId;
      button.setAttribute('aria-pressed', active ? 'true' : 'false');
    });
    section.setAttribute('data-ebay-selected-market', marketId);
    if (persist) {
      try { if (window.localStorage) localStorage.setItem('phoenix-ebay-market', marketId); } catch (err) {}
    }
    closeMenu(section);
  }
  sections.forEach(function (section) {
    if (section.getAttribute('data-ebay-localized-init') === '1') return;
    section.setAttribute('data-ebay-localized-init', '1');
    var available = availableMarkets(section);
    var marketId = defaultMarket;
    if (window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation && window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation.inferMarket) {
      marketId = window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation.inferMarket(available, {
        storageKey: 'phoenix-ebay-market',
        defaultMarket: defaultMarket
      });
    } else if (available.indexOf(defaultMarket)< 0) {
      marketId = available[0] || defaultMarket;
    }
    var select = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-select]');
    if (select) {
      select.addEventListener('change', function () {
        applyMarket(section, select.value, true);
      });
    }
    var trigger = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-trigger]');
    var menu = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-menu]');
    if (trigger && menu) {
      trigger.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
        event.stopPropagation();
        var expanded = trigger.getAttribute('aria-expanded') === 'true';
        menu.hidden = expanded;
        trigger.setAttribute('aria-expanded', expanded ? 'false' : 'true');
        var picker = trigger.closest('.fr-ebay-market-picker');
        if (picker) picker.classList.toggle('fr-ebay-market-picker--open', !expanded);
      });
      Array.prototype.slice.call(menu.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-option]')).forEach(function (button) {
        button.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
          event.stopPropagation();
          applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-option'), true);
        });
      });
      document.addEventListener('click', function () { closeMenu(section); });
    }
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      button.addEventListener('click', function () {
        applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button'), true);
      });
    });
    applyMarket(section, marketId, false);
  });
})();
