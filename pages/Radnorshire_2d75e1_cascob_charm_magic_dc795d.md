## Introduction

The Cascob Charm is a surviving written protection charm associated with St Michael’s church at Llanfihangel Cascob, a small settlement on the eastern edge of Radnor Forest in historic [Radnorshire]({{ 'where-radnorshires-old-stories-still/' | relative_url }}). It matters because it turns a broad idea — “folk magic in Wales” — into a named local object: a paper charm made for a woman called Elizabeth Lloyd, invoking Christian protection while also using occult-style formulae such as “ABRACADABRA”. Local retellings usually present it as a charm against witchcraft and evil influence, now framed inside the church where it is connected with Cascob’s wider landscape of St Michael dedications, dragon legend, churchyard memory and everyday rural fears.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.radnorfforest.co.uk/news-views/legands" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: radnorfforest.co.uk">[Friends of Radnor Forest]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">radnorfforest.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Friends of Radnor Forest Legend and Lore of the Radnor Forest</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Friends of Radnor Forest Legend and Lore of the Radnor Forest</span></span></span>



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/Radnorshire_2d75e1_cascob_charm_magic_dc795d-overview.webp" | relative_url }}" alt="Overview image for Cascob Charm" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

The evidence is intriguing but not perfectly neat. Popular accounts have dated the charm variously to the seventeenth or eighteenth century, while recent research presented by Judith Tulfer notes that Elizabeth Lloyd has not been securely identified and that the charm was probably cast by James Morris, a Welsh cunning man active between 1860 and 1892.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.womensarchivewales.org/getfile/documents/Pressentations/Conference%202024/judith-tulfer-agency.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: womensarchivewales.org">[Women’s Archive Wales]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">womensarchivewales.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">judith tulfer agency</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">judith tulfer agency</span></span></span> That uncertainty is part of the story. The Cascob Charm is not best understood as a theatrical “spell” in the modern fantasy sense, but as a fragile piece of practical protection: paper, words, names, holy powers and fear of witchcraft brought together for a specific person in a specific Radnorshire parish.

## The charm at Llanfihangel Cascob

Cascob itself is small, remote and well suited to a folklore page precisely because it is not a grand tourist centre. The Clwyd-Powys Archaeological Trust describes Cascob as lying towards the head of a remote valley on the eastern edge of Radnor Forest, with Cascob Brook running eastwards towards the Lugg and Presteigne around 8 km to the east.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://heneb.org.uk/archive/cpat/ycom/radnor/cascob.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: heneb.org.uk">[Heneb]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">heneb.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Heneb Microsoft Word</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Heneb Microsoft Word</span></span></span> The same survey notes that, 150 years before its report, the settlement consisted of little more than a church, farm and single cottage, with little subsequent change.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://heneb.org.uk/archive/cpat/ycom/radnor/cascob.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: heneb.org.uk">[Heneb]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">heneb.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Heneb Microsoft Word</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Heneb Microsoft Word</span></span></span>

That sparse setting gives the charm much of its force. It is not attached to a city occult circle or a famous magical manuscript, but to a rural church in a border county where churchyard, farm, stream and upland track sit close together. St Michael’s church at Cascob is dedicated to the archangel associated with defeating evil; the CPAT survey describes the church as Grade II* listed, with a west tower perhaps of the early fourteenth century, a nave possibly of the thirteenth century, and later medieval fabric.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://heneb.org.uk/archive/cpat/ycom/radnor/cascob.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: heneb.org.uk">[Heneb]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">heneb.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Heneb Microsoft Word</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Heneb Microsoft Word</span></span></span> The mound around the tower has been interpreted in different ways: the survey says it may disguise remnants of an earlier structure, while also noting claims that it was a prehistoric burial mound.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://heneb.org.uk/archive/cpat/ycom/radnor/cascob.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: heneb.org.uk">[Heneb]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">heneb.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Heneb Microsoft Word</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Heneb Microsoft Word</span></span></span>

The charm’s modern public identity is tied to that church interior. Visit Knighton describes Llanfihangel Cascob as medieval in origin and says that inside the church is a framed “abracadabra” spell made for Elizabeth Lloyd, intended to free her from “witchcraft” and “all Evil spirites and all evil men of women or Wizardes”.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://visitknighton.co.uk/visit-knighton-town-dyke/history/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: visitknighton.co.uk">[Visit Knighton]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">visitknighton.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Visit Knighton History</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Visit Knighton History</span></span></span> Radnor Forest heritage retellings likewise call it the Cascob Charm, or ABRACADABRA charm, and describe it as a mixture of Christian prayer and occult formula, used to protect Elizabeth Lloyd and later found in the churchyard before being framed inside the church.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.radnorfforest.co.uk/news-views/legands" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: radnorfforest.co.uk">[Friends of Radnor Forest]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">radnorfforest.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Friends of Radnor Forest Legend and Lore of the Radnor Forest</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Friends of Radnor Forest Legend and Lore of the Radnor Forest</span></span></span>

The exact discovery story varies in retellings. Some accounts say it was discovered during renovations around 1890, rolled up in or near a grave; others speak more generally of its churchyard discovery.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.mysteriesofmercia.com/post/a-church-that-was-sacred-before-it-was-built" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: mysteriesofmercia.com">[Mysteries of Mercia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">mysteriesofmercia.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">a church that was sacred before it was built</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">a church that was sacred before it was built</span></span></span> What remains consistent is the core tradition: a named woman, a written charm, fear of witchcraft or evil influence, and the later preservation of the object within the church.

## What was it meant to protect?

The simplest answer is that the Cascob Charm was meant to protect Elizabeth Lloyd from witchcraft, evil spirits, hostile people and other malign influences. The wording quoted in local accounts names “witchcraft”, “evil spirits”, “evil men or women” and “wizards”, which places it firmly in the protective rather than aggressive side of folk magic.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://visitknighton.co.uk/visit-knighton-town-dyke/history/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: visitknighton.co.uk">[Visit Knighton]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">visitknighton.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Visit Knighton History</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Visit Knighton History</span></span></span> It was not a charm for treasure-hunting, love magic or weather control. Its purpose was defence.

That defence may have been imagined in several overlapping ways:

**Protection of the living.** If Elizabeth Lloyd was alive when the charm was made, it may have been intended to shield her from illness, misfortune, malicious neighbours or a suspected curse. This would fit the wider pattern of Welsh written charms, which were often made when everyday troubles were attributed to witchcraft or ill-wishing. Aberystwyth University’s summary of Judith Tulfer’s research notes that problems such as illness, sick livestock, butter failing to churn, or family members forming unsuitable relationships could be interpreted as witchcraft-related misfortune.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.aber.ac.uk/en/news/archive/2024/08/title-274668-en.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: aber.ac.uk">[Aberystwyth University]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">aber.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Aberystwyth University August: News, Aberystwyth University</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Aberystwyth University August: News, Aberystwyth University</span></span></span>

**Protection around death and burial.** Some local accounts interpret the charm as protecting Elizabeth Lloyd’s corpse against witchcraft and evil.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.mysteriesofmercia.com/post/a-church-that-was-sacred-before-it-was-built" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: mysteriesofmercia.com">[Mysteries of Mercia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">mysteriesofmercia.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">a church that was sacred before it was built</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">a church that was sacred before it was built</span></span></span> That reading may come from the reported churchyard or grave context. If correct, the charm belongs to a particularly anxious borderland of belief: the dead body as something needing spiritual guarding, not merely remembrance.

**Protection through concealment.** Welsh charms of this kind were often folded, rolled, sealed, hidden or carried, rather than displayed. Museum Wales notes that written charms against witchcraft could be rolled up, sealed inside a bottle and placed under a hearth or hidden in a main beam to protect a household from witchcraft and evildoers.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://museum.wales/blog/1203/Witch-bottles-and-healing-charms/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: museum.wales">[Museum Wales]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">museum.wales</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Wales Witch-bottles and healing charms &#124; Museum Wales</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wales Witch-bottles and healing charms &#124; Museum Wales</span></span></span> The Cascob Charm’s later framing makes it visible to visitors, but its original power may have depended on being physically placed in relation to the person, grave or churchyard.

The key point is that protection did not have to mean one dramatic supernatural attack. In rural belief, “witchcraft” could be the explanation for a run of bad luck, a wasting illness, damaged relationships, failed farm work or a sense that someone bore malice. The charm answered those fears with written words that were meant to hold power.

## Written charms against witchcraft

The Cascob Charm belongs to a wider Welsh tradition of written protective charms, especially strong in mid Wales. Aberystwyth University’s account of Judith Tulfer’s research explains that paper charms were used because particular written words were believed to carry power against witches and evil; this practice continued far later than many modern readers expect, with belief in curses and witchcraft still present in some communities into the early twentieth century.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.aber.ac.uk/en/news/archive/2024/08/title-274668-en.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: aber.ac.uk">[Aberystwyth University]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">aber.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Aberystwyth University August: News, Aberystwyth University</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Aberystwyth University August: News, Aberystwyth University</span></span></span>

These charms were often produced by Welsh cunning men: local specialists consulted when ordinary explanations and remedies did not seem enough. Tulfer describes a typical pattern: the client explained the problem, the cunning man consulted books such as almanacks, astrological charts or magical texts, and then provided a paper charm, often folded or sealed with instructions not to read it.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.aber.ac.uk/en/news/archive/2024/08/title-274668-en.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: aber.ac.uk">[Aberystwyth University]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">aber.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Aberystwyth University August: News, Aberystwyth University</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Aberystwyth University August: News, Aberystwyth University</span></span></span> Around 1918, the standard rate for a written charm was said to be five shillings.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.aber.ac.uk/en/news/archive/2024/08/title-274668-en.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: aber.ac.uk">[Aberystwyth University]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">aber.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Aberystwyth University August: News, Aberystwyth University</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Aberystwyth University August: News, Aberystwyth University</span></span></span>

The Cascob case is especially valuable because it appears to name the client. Many surviving charms include the person’s name, sometimes their location and the problem the charm was intended to address. Tulfer’s research notes that more than 30 such charms are known to survive in collections, with likely more in private ownership; the mid-Wales examples from about 1840 to 1939 often combine English and Latin text, Christian invocations, astrological symbols and pleas for relief from witchcraft, evil people and hardness of heart.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.aber.ac.uk/en/news/archive/2024/08/title-274668-en.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: aber.ac.uk">[Aberystwyth University]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">aber.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Aberystwyth University August: News, Aberystwyth University</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Aberystwyth University August: News, Aberystwyth University</span></span></span>

A close comparison lies just over the historic county boundary at Llangurig in Montgomeryshire, another mid-Wales centre associated with cunning men and written charms. Museum Wales records that Llangurig was especially famous in the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries for such figures, whose services ranged from countering witchcraft and healing to astrology, fortune telling and finding lost property.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://museum.wales/blog/1203/Witch-bottles-and-healing-charms/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: museum.wales">[Museum Wales]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">museum.wales</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Wales Witch-bottles and healing charms &#124; Museum Wales</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wales Witch-bottles and healing charms &#124; Museum Wales</span></span></span> People’s Collection Wales also preserves a “Written charm against witchcraft, from Llangurig, Montgomeryshire”, uploaded by Amgueddfa Cymru, showing that the Cascob Charm is not an isolated oddity but part of a documented regional practice.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.peoplescollection.wales/items/13182" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: peoplescollection.wales">[People&#x27;s Collection Wales]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">peoplescollection.wales</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on peoplescollection.wales.</span></span></span>

This comparison helps keep the Radnorshire evidence grounded. The Cascob Charm is special because it survives in a named Radnorshire church, but its method — writing protective formulae on paper — was recognisable across mid Wales.

## Christian prayer and occult formulae together

The most striking thing about the Cascob Charm is not that it is simply “pagan” or simply “Christian”. It is both more ordinary and more complicated than that. Local descriptions emphasise that it combines Christian prayer with occult formula.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.radnorfforest.co.uk/news-views/legands" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: radnorfforest.co.uk">[Friends of Radnor Forest]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">radnorfforest.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Friends of Radnor Forest Legend and Lore of the Radnor Forest</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Friends of Radnor Forest Legend and Lore of the Radnor Forest</span></span></span> The quoted opening invokes the Father, Son, Holy Ghost and Jesus Christ, yet the charm is also known for its “ABRACADABRA” pattern and for occult-style symbols or formulae.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.mysteriesofmercia.com/post/a-church-that-was-sacred-before-it-was-built" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: mysteriesofmercia.com">[Mysteries of Mercia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">mysteriesofmercia.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">a church that was sacred before it was built</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">a church that was sacred before it was built</span></span></span>

For modern readers, that mixture can look contradictory. For the people who used such charms, it may have felt practical. Holy names, archangels, biblical language, Latin, repeated words, written symbols and secrecy were all part of a protection technology. Tulfer’s research on Welsh paper charms notes that these slips often used Christian tradition, astrological symbols and appeals to God, Jesus, the Holy Ghost, archangels and heavenly powers in the same protective object.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.aber.ac.uk/en/news/archive/2024/08/title-274668-en.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: aber.ac.uk">[Aberystwyth University]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">aber.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Aberystwyth University August: News, Aberystwyth University</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Aberystwyth University August: News, Aberystwyth University</span></span></span>

The word “ABRACADABRA” also deserves less mockery than it often gets. Today it sounds like a stage-magician’s flourish, but in charm traditions it could function as a written formula, sometimes arranged in a shrinking pattern so that the thing feared was symbolically diminished. In the Cascob retellings, the visual and verbal force of the word is part of what makes the object memorable: it is a prayer, but it is also a diagram-like written act.

This is why the Cascob Charm is such a useful doorway into practical magic in Radnorshire. It shows a world in which church and counter-witchcraft did not always sit neatly apart. A charm could call on Christ while behaving like an amulet. A churchyard could be a place of burial, worship and magical concealment. A St Michael church could belong both to official Christianity and to local stories about holding back dragons, devils and witchcraft.

## How old and well-attested is the tradition?

The safest answer is that the Cascob Charm is well attested as a local object and tradition, but some details of its date, maker and original use remain uncertain. Several public-facing heritage accounts describe it as displayed in St Michael’s church, connected with Elizabeth Lloyd and designed to protect against witchcraft or evil.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://visitknighton.co.uk/visit-knighton-town-dyke/history/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: visitknighton.co.uk">[Visit Knighton]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">visitknighton.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Visit Knighton History</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Visit Knighton History</span></span></span> That makes the tradition visible and locally anchored.

The date is less settled. Some online retellings place the charm in the late seventeenth or eighteenth century.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.mysteriesofmercia.com/post/a-church-that-was-sacred-before-it-was-built" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: mysteriesofmercia.com">[Mysteries of Mercia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">mysteriesofmercia.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">a church that was sacred before it was built</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">a church that was sacred before it was built</span></span></span> More recent specialist research complicates that picture: a Women’s Archive Wales conference paper summary says Elizabeth Lloyd has not been traced with certainty and that the charm was probably cast by James Morris, a cunning man active between 1860 and 1892.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.womensarchivewales.org/getfile/documents/Pressentations/Conference%202024/judith-tulfer-agency.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: womensarchivewales.org">[Women’s Archive Wales]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">womensarchivewales.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">judith tulfer agency</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">judith tulfer agency</span></span></span> If that attribution is right, the Cascob Charm belongs not to the era of witch trials but to the much later persistence of counter-witchcraft belief in rural Wales.

That later date would not make the charm less folkloric. In some ways, it makes it more revealing. It would show that written magical protection was still meaningful in nineteenth-century Radnorshire, long after educated opinion often claimed such beliefs were vanishing. Aberystwyth University’s account of Tulfer’s wider research makes this point clearly: people could still attribute misfortune to witchcraft into the twentieth century, and paper charms remained one everyday solution.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.aber.ac.uk/en/news/archive/2024/08/title-274668-en.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: aber.ac.uk">[Aberystwyth University]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">aber.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Aberystwyth University August: News, Aberystwyth University</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Aberystwyth University August: News, Aberystwyth University</span></span></span>

The evidence should therefore be read in layers. The object is real enough as a preserved local charm; the Elizabeth Lloyd tradition is strong but not fully biographically pinned down; the older dating in popular accounts should be treated cautiously; and the possible James Morris connection links Cascob to a documented nineteenth-century mid-Wales charm economy.

## Why it matters for Radnorshire folklore

The Cascob Charm matters because it brings Radnorshire folklore down to the scale of a single anxious human need. The county is rich in landscape legend — the [Radnor Forest dragon]({{ 'dragon-ring/' | relative_url }}), St Michael churches, old churchyards, yews, hills and stones — but the charm shows folklore as something people used, bought, hid and hoped would work.

It also fits Radnorshire’s border character. Cascob sits near the English border, with Presteigne to the east and Kington not far beyond. The CPAT survey places it on the eastern edge of Radnor Forest, with its brook running towards the Lugg, a river system leading eastwards.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://heneb.org.uk/archive/cpat/ycom/radnor/cascob.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: heneb.org.uk">[Heneb]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">heneb.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Heneb Microsoft Word</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Heneb Microsoft Word</span></span></span> The charm’s language, as quoted in English, and its Welsh rural setting reflect that border mixture: Welsh magical practice, English words, Christian formulae, local church authority and rural suspicion of witchcraft all occupying the same small object.

The St Michael setting adds another layer. Visit Knighton notes that old Radnorshire contains multiple St Michael churches and links them with the Radnor Forest dragon tradition, in which churches dedicated to the archangel form a protective ring around the sleeping dragon.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://visitknighton.co.uk/visit-knighton-town-dyke/history/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: visitknighton.co.uk">[Visit Knighton]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">visitknighton.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Visit Knighton History</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Visit Knighton History</span></span></span> Cascob’s charm is not the dragon legend itself, but it belongs to the same symbolic geography: St Michael as defender, church as boundary, written holiness as a barrier against evil.

For readers exploring Radnorshire folklore, this makes Cascob a natural companion to pages on the Radnor Forest dragon, St Michael churches, holy wells, churchyard yews and cunning folk. It shows that “protection” in local tradition was not only mythical and monumental. Sometimes it was a piece of paper.

## How the Cascob Charm is retold today

Today the Cascob Charm is usually encountered in three ways: as a church curiosity, as a Radnor Forest folklore detail, and as an example of Welsh practical magic. Visit Knighton includes it among the St Michael and dragon material around the “Radnor Ring”, while Radnor Forest local lore pages present it as one of the area’s strange and memorable traditions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://visitknighton.co.uk/visit-knighton-town-dyke/history/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: visitknighton.co.uk">[Visit Knighton]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">visitknighton.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Visit Knighton History</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Visit Knighton History</span></span></span> Contemporary creative responses have also appeared, including an abridged printed version of the charm by Fallow Pages, which describes it as found in St Michael’s church at Cascob and reshapes the text as an art object.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://fallowpages.art/cacsob-charm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: fallowpages.art">[fallowpages.art]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">fallowpages.art</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on fallowpages.art.</span></span></span>

That modern afterlife is not a corruption of the tradition, but it does change its function. Originally, the charm was probably private, protective and bound to fear. Now it is public, framed, visited, photographed, discussed and sometimes aestheticised. The danger is that it becomes merely “spooky” or decorative. The richer reading is to see it as evidence of how rural people tried to manage vulnerability before modern medicine, policing, insurance and social services could answer every crisis.

The Cascob Charm’s power as folklore lies in that tension. It is strange enough to catch the eye: ABRACADABRA in a Radnorshire church, made for a woman whose identity remains uncertain. But it is also deeply ordinary: a community faced illness, death, malice or misfortune, and someone wrote down words of protection. In that small act, practical magic becomes part of Radnorshire’s local history rather than a fantasy imposed on it.
