## Introduction

For this UK historic-county project, Fermanagh is treated as one of Northern Ireland’s six historic counties. Modern local government is different: since 2015, the whole of County Fermanagh has sat within the wider Fermanagh and Omagh District, alongside part of County Tyrone. That matters for folklore because older tales follow parishes, loughs, townlands, pilgrim routes and estates rather than modern council lines.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://wikishire.co.uk/wiki/Fermanagh" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wikishire.co.uk">[Wikishire]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wikishire.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wikishire.co.uk.</span></span></span>

## Why Fermanagh’s folklore feels different

Fermanagh is often described through its lakelands, and that is more than tourist language. Upper and Lower Lough Erne run through the county, with islands, reedbeds, ferry crossings, monastic ruins and water roads giving the place a natural folklore geography. Stories here often begin with movement over water: a saint founding a monastery on an island, a supernatural woman giving her name to a lough, a hidden people beneath the surface, or a family fleeing a haunted cottage only to find that the story follows them.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://wikishire.co.uk/wiki/Fermanagh" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wikishire.co.uk">[Wikishire]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wikishire.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wikishire.co.uk.</span></span></span>

The county also sits at a cultural edge. Fermanagh borders counties Donegal, Leitrim, Cavan and Monaghan, so its traditions naturally overlap with wider Ulster and north-west Irish story patterns: fairy forts and fairy roads, saintly wells, giant and hero legends, banshee-like death omens, talking animals, ominous lights and tales of treasure. The most useful way to read Fermanagh folklore is therefore not as a sealed local museum, but as a county version of wider Irish and Ulster tradition, pinned to very specific Fermanagh places.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fermanagh_and_Omagh" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Fermanagh and Omagh</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Fermanagh and Omagh</span></span></span>

A good example is Doreen McBride’s *Fermanagh Folk Tales*, published by The History Press. Its publisher’s description captures the range of county storytelling: “Shining Folk” beneath Lough Erne, fairies, the Cooneen poltergeist, the ghost of Belleek Pottery, and tall tales about clever pigs, talking horses and unlikely animals. That mixture is important. Fermanagh folklore is not only solemn mythology; it also includes humour, local exaggeration, fireside performance and stories told because they make a place memorable.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://thehistorypress.co.uk/publication/fermanagh-folk-tales/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: thehistorypress.co.uk">[The History Press]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">thehistorypress.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on thehistorypress.co.uk.</span></span></span>

## Lough Erne, Enniskillen and names that carry myth

The most obvious mythic landscape in Fermanagh is Lough Erne itself. One tradition says the lough takes its name from a supernatural or legendary woman, Erne, sometimes described as one of Queen Maeve’s attendants, who fled north and drowned with her companions. Other traditions explain the lake as the result of an overflowing magical well, or as a body of water that burst from the ground during battle. These are not geological explanations in the modern sense; they are mythic accounts that make the lake a living part of story rather than a passive backdrop.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lough_Erne" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Lough Erne</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Lough Erne</span></span></span>

Enniskillen, the county town, carries an even more visible myth in its name. The town’s Irish name is commonly explained as “Cethlenn’s island”, referring to Cethlenn or Ceithlenn, a figure associated with the Fomorians in Irish myth. Local tradition links her with the island setting of the town, while modern visitor interpretation often retells Enniskillen as a place where myth and history meet.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enniskillen" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wikipedia.org.</span></span></span>

This is a useful case where folklore, place-name history and later retelling need to be kept distinct. The place-name connection is well established in modern accounts of Enniskillen, but the fuller dramatic tale of a wounded mythic woman reaching, or failing to reach, the island is best treated as local legend rather than recoverable history. Its power lies in how neatly it fits the town’s geography: Enniskillen is an island settlement between Upper and Lower Lough Erne, and its name lets residents and visitors imagine the town as part of a much older mythic map.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enniskillen" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wikipedia.org.</span></span></span>

## Boa Island and the county’s most haunting stones

Boa Island, in Lower Lough Erne, gives Fermanagh one of its most striking encounters between archaeology and folklore. At Caldragh graveyard stand two carved stone figures: the larger two-faced Dreenan figure, often called the Boa Island Janus, and the smaller Lustymore figure, moved there from nearby Lustymore Island. Official tourism material notes that the better-known figure has been at Caldragh since at least 1841, while archaeological and heritage summaries describe the figures as enigmatic, weathered and difficult to date with certainty.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://discovernorthernireland.com/listing/janus-figure-boa-island/70078101/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: discovernorthernireland.com">[Discover Northern Ireland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">discovernorthernireland.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on discovernorthernireland.com.</span></span></span>

The temptation is to call the larger figure a pagan god and leave it there. The evidence is more cautious. It is widely treated as a pre-Christian or early medieval survival, and often compared with Iron Age stone figures, but the exact date, purpose and identity of the figures remain uncertain. The name “Janus” is a modern comparison with the two-faced Roman god, not proof that Roman Janus worship was taking place in Fermanagh.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.tuatha.ie/boa-island/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: tuatha.ie">[Tuatha]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">tuatha.ie</span><span class="citation-popover-title">boa island</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">boa island</span></span></span>

That uncertainty is part of the appeal. Boa Island matters to folklore because it shows how a county can inherit a mysterious object before inheriting a settled explanation. The figures have become part of Fermanagh’s imaginative identity precisely because they resist easy interpretation: graveyard guardians, old idols, boundary figures, ancestors, deities or simply extraordinary survivals from a lost sculptural tradition. Their presence also links Fermanagh naturally with wider Ulster and Irish traditions about carved stones, sacred islands and the uneasy meeting of Christian burial places with older ritual landscapes.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.tuatha.ie/boa-island/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: tuatha.ie">[Tuatha]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">tuatha.ie</span><span class="citation-popover-title">boa island</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">boa island</span></span></span>

## Saints, wells and sacred islands

Fermanagh’s Christian folklore is just as important as its older mythic material. Devenish Island, on Lower Lough Erne, is one of the county’s great sacred sites. The monastery is traditionally associated with St Molaise and is described by official heritage sources as a site founded in the sixth century, later raided by Vikings in 837, burned in 1157 and developed in the Middle Ages as a parish church site and Augustinian priory.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.communities-ni.gov.uk/heritage-sites/devenish-ecclesiastical-site" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: communities-ni.gov.uk">[Department for Communities]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">communities-ni.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Department for Communities Devenish Ecclesiastical Site</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Department for Communities Devenish Ecclesiastical Site</span></span></span>

For folklore readers, Devenish is important because it turns the lough into a spiritual route. The island was not merely remote; it was reachable by water, visible from shore, and wrapped in stories of saints, pilgrimage, relics and survival. Modern pilgrimage interpretation still presents Devenish as a centre of the county’s spiritual and cultural life, showing how older religious memory continues to shape Fermanagh’s public storytelling.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://loughernepilgrimway.com/discover/devenish-island/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: loughernepilgrimway.com">[Lough Erne Pilgrim Way]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">loughernepilgrimway.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on loughernepilgrimway.com.</span></span></span>

Holy wells carry this same blend of faith, cure and story at a smaller scale. St Naile’s Well at [Kinawley]({{ 'kinawley/' | relative_url }}) is especially rich. The National Folklore Collection’s Schools’ Collection records that the well was blessed by St Naile, that its water was used as a cure for jaundice, and that two trout once lived in it until a man caught one, after which his wife died, he went insane and the remaining trout disappeared. A separate holy-wells project records related traditions: St Naile and St Ninian competing over where to build, an apple linked to a wart-curing tree, and the saint striking rock to produce a spring.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.duchas.ie/en/cbes/5044804/5040162/5083301" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: duchas.ie">[Dúchas]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">duchas.ie</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on duchas.ie.</span></span></span>

These stories are not just quaint miracle tales. They show how sacred places were protected by narrative rules. The trout story warns against taking what belongs to the well. The cure traditions make the water socially useful. The saintly origin story ties a local place to a named holy figure. Together they show how a rural community remembered ownership, reverence, danger and healing through story.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.duchas.ie/en/cbes/5044804/5040162/5083301" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: duchas.ie">[Dúchas]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">duchas.ie</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on duchas.ie.</span></span></span>

## Fairy roads, forts and the hidden traffic of the county

Fermanagh’s fairy lore is scattered through local accounts rather than concentrated in one famous national tale. The 1930s Schools’ Collection is especially valuable because it preserves material gathered from local informants before many oral traditions weakened. In one account from the Kinawley area, an old funeral road is described as having become a fairy pass, still used by fairy funerals when “bringing any one away”. The story includes lights, procession-like noise and the unsettling sound of women coaxing a crying child.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.duchas.ie/en/cbes/5044803/5040031/5083194" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: duchas.ie">[Dúchas]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">duchas.ie</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on duchas.ie.</span></span></span>

That detail is classic and deeply local at the same time. Across Ireland and Britain, fairy routes are often imagined as invisible roads that humans should not block or disturb. In the Fermanagh example, the route is not an abstract fairy highway; it is an old practical road once used by local people, including funeral traffic. As ordinary use faded, folklore reoccupied it. The tale preserves a memory of movement through the landscape even after the route itself became less central to daily life.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.duchas.ie/en/cbes/5044803/5040031/5083194" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: duchas.ie">[Dúchas]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">duchas.ie</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on duchas.ie.</span></span></span>

Fairy forts also appear in the county’s borderland records. One Schools’ Collection item describes a fort in Ceibne in Fermanagh as a circular stone structure where lights were often seen. Nearby accounts from adjoining districts speak of forts surrounded by whitethorn bushes and inhabited by fairies. These details matter because they show the typical social function of fairy-fort lore: it marks certain archaeological or unusual landscape features as places to treat carefully.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.duchas.ie/en/cbes/5044808/5040554/5084063" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: duchas.ie">[Dúchas]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">duchas.ie</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on duchas.ie.</span></span></span>

## Witches, hounds and giant-country storytelling

Fermanagh also has hero and witch traditions tied to hills. Big Dog Forest, near Derrygonnelly, is linked in Cuilcagh Lakelands Geopark interpretation with the hills called Big Dog and Little Dog. The story says they are named for Bran and Sceolan, the hounds of Fionn MacCool, who chased a witch and were turned into stone.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cuilcaghlakelands.org/about-us/what-is-a-geopark/history-folklore/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cuilcaghlakelands.org">[Cuilcagh Lakelands Geopark]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cuilcaghlakelands.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cuilcaghlakelands.org.</span></span></span>

This is a good example of a localised Fionn tradition. Fionn MacCool stories appear across Ireland and Scotland, often explaining odd stones, causeways, mountains and place-names. In Fermanagh, the heroic cycle becomes intimate and visible: two hills on a skyline are read as enchanted dogs. The witch is not a court-trial witch or a historical accused woman; she belongs to the older storytelling world of pursuit, transformation and landscape explanation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cuilcaghlakelands.org/geosite/big-dog-forest/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cuilcaghlakelands.org">[Cuilcagh Lakelands Geopark]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cuilcaghlakelands.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cuilcaghlakelands.org.</span></span></span>

The same landscape logic appears around Benaughlin, where local tradition recorded in modern summaries says people climbed the hill on the last Sunday of July for Lughnasa and that a great white horse appeared there each year and spoke. The hill’s name has itself been interpreted as connected with a “speaking horse”. Whether read as seasonal custom, place-name legend or heroic folklore, the story shows how Fermanagh’s uplands were imagined as active, speaking places rather than empty scenery.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benaughlin_Mountain" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Benaughlin Mountain</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Benaughlin Mountain</span></span></span>

## Ghosts, poltergeists and modern local memory

Fermanagh’s best-known ghost story is the Cooneen or Coonian Ghost, associated with a farmhouse near Brookeborough. The story is usually set in the early twentieth century and centres on Bridget Murphy and her children, who were said to experience knocks, movements of objects and other disturbances before leaving for America. Local-history accounts say the case was reported in local newspapers and later preserved in Shane Leslie’s *Ghost Book*.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://fermanaghroots.com/wiki/index.php?title=Cooneen_Ghost" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: fermanaghroots.com">[fermanaghroots.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">fermanaghroots.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Cooneen Ghost</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cooneen Ghost</span></span></span>

The Cooneen story is a good reminder that “folklore” is not only medieval or ancient. This is a modern haunting tradition, close enough to census records, newspapers, named people and emigration memory to feel unusually concrete. That does not make the supernatural claims proven. It does make the story socially revealing: a rural household becomes famous, neighbours argue over causes, clergy enter the tale, and emigration turns the haunting into a transatlantic legend.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.belfastentries.com/stories/cooneen-ghost/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: belfastentries.com">[Belfast Entries]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">belfastentries.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Belfast Entries The Cooneen Ghost</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Belfast Entries The Cooneen Ghost</span></span></span>

Modern tourism and heritage writing continue to retell Fermanagh as a haunted county, including Cooneen, castle ghosts and eerie loughside stories. These retellings are valuable, but they need to be read carefully. A Halloween tourism page may preserve genuine local motifs, yet it may also sharpen them for seasonal atmosphere. The older the source trail — oral informants, local newspapers, antiquarian references, Schools’ Collection entries — the easier it is to see what has been inherited and what has been newly packaged.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.fermanaghlakelands.com/blog/read/2024/10/spooky-stories-and-folklore-unearthing-fermanaghs-haunting-legends-b270" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: fermanaghlakelands.com">[Fermanagh Lakelands]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">fermanaghlakelands.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">spooky stories and folklore unearthing fermanaghs haunting legends b270</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">spooky stories and folklore unearthing fermanaghs haunting legends b270</span></span></span>

## Standing stones and the border between archaeology and legend

Not every ancient monument in Fermanagh has a strong surviving legend, but monuments still shape the county’s mythic feel. Drumskinny Stone Circle, Cairn and Alignment is a good example. Official heritage information describes it as an upland bog site drained after excavation in 1962, with a stone circle of 39 stones, a small cairn and an alignment of 24 stones. It is much smaller than many visitors expect, and is compared with the Beaghmore circles and other mid-Ulster monuments.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.communities-ni.gov.uk/heritage-sites/drumskinny-stone-circle-cairn-and-alignment" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: communities-ni.gov.uk">[Department for Communities]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">communities-ni.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Department for Communities Drumskinny Stone Circle, Cairn and Alignment</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Department for Communities Drumskinny Stone Circle, Cairn and Alignment</span></span></span>

The important distinction is that archaeological age is not the same as folklore evidence. A stone circle can be ancient without having a well-attested fairy, giant or witch story attached to it. For a folklore page, Drumskinny matters because it helps explain why Fermanagh is so receptive to legend: the county’s visible prehistoric remains invite questions about ritual, burial, astronomy and old belief, even where the exact stories have not survived.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ireland.com/en-us/magazine/built-heritage/ancient-irelands-standing-stones/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ireland.com">[Ireland.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ireland.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Uncover ancient Ireland&#x27;s standing stones</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Uncover ancient Ireland&#x27;s standing stones</span></span></span>

That makes Fermanagh different from places where a single spectacular monument dominates the imagination. Here, the effect is cumulative: a small stone circle, an island graveyard with carved figures, old wells, monastic ruins, fairy roads and wooded estates. The folklore comes from the way these places sit close together in a wet, islanded, borderland county.

## What is old tradition, and what is modern retelling?

Fermanagh folklore is strongest when its layers are kept visible. Some traditions are preserved in twentieth-century oral collection, especially the Schools’ Collection entries for wells, fairy passes, local heroes, customs and cures. These are not “ancient texts”, but they are valuable records of what local people were saying in the 1930s.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.duchas.ie/en/cbes/5044804/5040162/5083301" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: duchas.ie">[Dúchas]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">duchas.ie</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on duchas.ie.</span></span></span>

Other material belongs to medieval or early Irish myth retold through place-names, such as Lough Erne’s origin stories and Enniskillen’s association with Cethlenn. These traditions may be old in theme, but many modern readers meet them through tourism pages, local-history websites or popular summaries.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lough_Erne" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Lough Erne</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Lough Erne</span></span></span>

A third layer is antiquarian and archaeological interpretation, especially around Boa Island and Drumskinny. Here the objects are real and visitable, but the stories told about them often involve educated guesswork. “Pagan idol”, “Janus figure” and “Celtic deity” are interpretive labels, not eyewitness traditions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.tuatha.ie/boa-island/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: tuatha.ie">[Tuatha]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">tuatha.ie</span><span class="citation-popover-title">boa island</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">boa island</span></span></span>

Finally, there is modern folklore: books, Halloween articles, walking-route interpretation, podcasts, local blogs and visitor storytelling. These do not invalidate the tradition. They show that folklore is still being selected, reshaped and performed. The key is to avoid treating every modern retelling as equally old.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://thehistorypress.co.uk/publication/fermanagh-folk-tales/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: thehistorypress.co.uk">[The History Press]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">thehistorypress.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on thehistorypress.co.uk.</span></span></span>

## Why Fermanagh matters in a UK folklore map

Fermanagh gives the UK folklore map something distinctive: a historic county where water, islands and borders do much of the storytelling. Its traditions connect naturally with neighbouring Ulster counties through fairy forts, Fionn MacCool legends, holy wells and standing stones, but the county’s own identity is unmistakable. Lough Erne is not just scenery; it is origin myth, route, refuge, danger and sacred water. Enniskillen is not just a county town; it is a named mythic island. Boa Island is not just an archaeological stop; it is one of the most memorable stone mysteries in Northern Ireland.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://wikishire.co.uk/wiki/Fermanagh" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wikishire.co.uk">[wikishire.co.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wikishire.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wikishire.co.uk.</span></span></span>

The most rewarding way to explore Fermanagh folklore is therefore place by place. Start with the lough and its islands, then follow the saints to Devenish and Kinawley, the fairies along old roads and forts, the hounds into Big Dog Forest, the ghosts to Cooneen, and the stones to Boa Island and Drumskinny. What emerges is not a fantasy version of the county, but a map of how generations have explained, warned, entertained, remembered and re-enchanted the Fermanagh landscape.
