## Introduction

Fenland witchcraft in [Cambridgeshire]({{ 'cambridgeshire/' | relative_url }}) was not just a matter of frightening stories about strange neighbours. It was a practical household belief system: windows, chimneys, floors, hearths and thresholds were treated as weak points where harm might enter, and where charms could stop it. In the fen villages around Ely, Wisbech, Witchford and the northern half of historic Cambridgeshire, witches were imagined as people who could blight children, animals, milk, crops and luck, often with the help of imps or familiar spirits. The response was equally physical: blue witch balls in windows, bottles buried in fireplaces or walls, shoes, bones, metal objects and other hidden safeguards placed where a home felt most vulnerable.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.museumofcambridge.org.uk/2018/01/safeguards-against-witchcraft/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: museumofcambridge.org.uk">[The Museum of Cambridge]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">museumofcambridge.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Museum of Cambridge Safeguards Against Witchcraft – The Museum of Cambridge</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Museum of Cambridge Safeguards Against Witchcraft – The Museum of Cambridge</span></span></span>



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/Cambridgeshire_f74246_fenland_witchcraft_p_673dd9-overview.webp" | relative_url }}" alt="Overview image for Witches" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

This tradition matters because it makes Cambridgeshire folklore unusually domestic. The uncanny was not only out on the drove road or in the misty fen; it was imagined at the door, the chair, the hearth and the window. [Enid Porter]({{ 'enid-porter/' | relative_url }}), curator of the Cambridge and County Folk Museum from 1947 to 1976, preserved much of this local material through oral history and collected objects, and the Museum of Cambridge still presents these charms as part of the county’s distinctive folklore inheritance.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.museumofcambridge.org.uk/2018/01/safeguards-against-witchcraft/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: museumofcambridge.org.uk">[The Museum of Cambridge]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">museumofcambridge.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Museum of Cambridge Safeguards Against Witchcraft – The Museum of Cambridge</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Museum of Cambridge Safeguards Against Witchcraft – The Museum of Cambridge</span></span></span>

## Why Fenland homes feared the threshold

The Cambridgeshire Fens gave witch belief a particular intensity because they combined isolation, poverty, dampness, close village life and hard-to-explain misfortune. Francis Young, writing on Ely witchcraft, describes the Cambridgeshire Fens as one of the last English areas where traditional witch belief remained widespread, with belief surviving in some communities into the twentieth century. He links this longevity to isolated settlements, environmental illness, close-knit communities and suspicion of outsiders.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://drfrancisyoung.com/wp-content/uploads/2014/01/witches-witchcraft-in-ely.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: drfrancisyoung.com">[Francis Young]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">drfrancisyoung.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Francis Young</span></span></span>

That helps explain why household protection was so important. In a village world where a sick child, a dead cow, sour milk or a run of bad luck could be blamed on malice, the home had to be defended like a small borderland. The vulnerable points were not random. Doors, windows, chimneys, hearths and gaps within walls were all openings between safe inside space and the uncertain world beyond. The Museum of Cambridge’s account of local safeguards says protective objects were often hidden near entry points so that witches could be distracted, trapped or drawn away from the people inside.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.museumofcambridge.org.uk/2018/01/safeguards-against-witchcraft/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: museumofcambridge.org.uk">[The Museum of Cambridge]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">museumofcambridge.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Museum of Cambridge Safeguards Against Witchcraft – The Museum of Cambridge</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Museum of Cambridge Safeguards Against Witchcraft – The Museum of Cambridge</span></span></span>

This is why the threshold is the key mechanism. Fenland household magic was not simply “superstition” in the abstract. It worked by imagining a route of attack and then blocking, confusing or absorbing the attack at that route. A witch might enter by a door, influence a room through a hearth, travel down a chimney, or be caught by an object that stood in for the household. In folklore terms, the home became a guarded enclosure.

## Witches, imps and familiar spirits

Cambridgeshire’s witch stories belong to a wider East Anglian pattern in which witches were often thought to work through animal helpers. The Museum of Cambridge states that people in Cambridgeshire and the Fenlands strongly believed witches possessed familiar spirits, locally known in the Fens as imps. These imps were usually imagined as small animals such as cats, mice or toads, and the Witch-finder General tradition recorded elaborate names including Pyewacket, Grizzel Greedigut and Vinegar Tom.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.museumofcambridge.org.uk/witches-and-their-familiars/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: museumofcambridge.org.uk">[The Museum of Cambridge]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">museumofcambridge.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Museum of Cambridge Witches and their Familiars – The Museum of Cambridge</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Museum of Cambridge Witches and their Familiars – The Museum of Cambridge</span></span></span>

The idea of the imp was more than a decorative detail. It explained how harm could travel. A witch did not need to stand in the dairy, stable or bedroom; an imp could be sent. Young notes that in East Anglia the devil or familiar might appear as dogs, cats, mice, toads, birds and other creatures, and that real animals could be identified as witches’ imps.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://drfrancisyoung.com/wp-content/uploads/2014/01/witches-witchcraft-in-ely.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: drfrancisyoung.com">[Francis Young]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">drfrancisyoung.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Francis Young</span></span></span>

That belief also gave local accusations a frightening everyday plausibility. A mouse near the hearth, a toad at the door or a cat lingering by the yard could be read as more than an animal. In a fen village where people knew one another’s quarrels and dependencies, the familiar made suspicion portable: it could cross boundaries that people could not openly cross.

The Civil War witch-hunt sharpened this language. Ely sat at the heart of the Fenland legal geography, and Young describes it as the place where local witchcraft suspects were brought to trial under the Bishop of Ely’s jurisdiction. The Eastern Association witch-hunts of the 1640s, associated with Matthew Hopkins and John Stearne, reached the Isle of Ely, and surviving trial material from the region repeatedly turns on familiar spirits, contracts with the devil and attacks on livestock.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://drfrancisyoung.com/wp-content/uploads/2014/01/witches-witchcraft-in-ely.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: drfrancisyoung.com">[Francis Young]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">drfrancisyoung.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Francis Young</span></span></span>

One Sutton case from 1647 shows the pattern clearly. Depositions recorded claims that John Bonham confessed to a devil in the form of a mole, while William Watson was said to have encountered the devil in the likeness of a great mouse and to have used imps to destroy animals. These are not “proof” of witchcraft; they are evidence of what accusers, magistrates and sometimes accused people believed could explain damaged cattle, dead pigs, dead geese and local misfortune.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://practitioners.exeter.ac.uk/wp-content/uploads/2014/11/Eastanglianwitchtrialappendix2.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: practitioners.exeter.ac.uk">[practitioners.exeter.ac.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">practitioners.exeter.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on exeter.ac.uk.</span></span></span>

## Witch balls, bottles and hidden charms

The most memorable Fenland household protections were objects placed where harm might enter. Some were visible, like witch balls in windows. Others were hidden inside the building fabric, where later owners might only discover them during repairs.

The Museum of Cambridge has a striking blue witch ball made in 1721 at Gamlingay, south-west of Cambridge, and says it hung in the window of a house. The belief attached to it was that refracted light would dazzle witches and stop them entering; another belief connected with such balls was that bright, reflective surfaces mesmerised evil spirits, causing them to touch the ball and become trapped within it.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.museumofcambridge.org.uk/2024/10/folklore-finds-witch-balls/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: museumofcambridge.org.uk">[The Museum of Cambridge]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">museumofcambridge.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Museum of Cambridge Folklore Finds: Witch Balls – The Museum of Cambridge</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Museum of Cambridge Folklore Finds: Witch Balls – The Museum of Cambridge</span></span></span>

Witch bottles worked differently. Instead of dazzling the witch at the window, they were usually hidden in the body of the house. The Museum of Cambridge describes witch bottles as protective charms against witches and the havoc they could bring, often containing pins, nail clippings, urine and rosemary, and buried in walls, floors and fireplaces. Its collection listing adds that nails, iron bars, animal bones and bottles have been found buried in buildings across Cambridgeshire as safeguards against witches.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.museumofcambridge.org.uk/adopt-an-object/witchs-bottle/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: museumofcambridge.org.uk">[The Museum of Cambridge]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">museumofcambridge.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on museumofcambridge.org.uk.</span></span></span>

The contents mattered because they made the bottle a trap or counter-charm. Hair, nail clippings and urine stood for the threatened person; pins, nails and iron suggested piercing, binding or holding power. In Young’s account of a Cambridgeshire story remembered by W. H. Barrett’s mother, a suspected witch in the Brandon Creek area instructed a blacksmith to make a special metal bottle, filled it with hair and toenail clippings, added blood and internal organs from a slaughtered hen, told the couple to add urine, stopper the bottle with clay and put it on the fire. When the stopper burst, the spell was said to be broken.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://drfrancisyoung.com/wp-content/uploads/2014/01/witches-witchcraft-in-ely.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: drfrancisyoung.com">[Francis Young]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">drfrancisyoung.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Francis Young</span></span></span>

That story is vivid because it shows counter-witchcraft as a household technology. It used bodily traces, animal matter, metalwork, fire and containment. It was not a decorative charm but a ritual machine: gather the threatened person’s essence, bottle the danger, heat it, seal it, and let the breaking of the stopper mark the breaking of the spell.

Other charms were less dramatic but followed the same border logic. The Museum of Cambridge’s safeguards exhibition notes that shoes and bones were frequently concealed, and that objects might be placed near entry points to distract or trap witches. It also records the belief that, if a witch entered the home, placing metal objects such as a key or scissors beneath the witch’s chair would render them powerless, while holly could be used because drawing blood from witches was believed to break their spells.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.museumofcambridge.org.uk/2018/01/safeguards-against-witchcraft/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: museumofcambridge.org.uk">[The Museum of Cambridge]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">museumofcambridge.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Museum of Cambridge Safeguards Against Witchcraft – The Museum of Cambridge</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Museum of Cambridge Safeguards Against Witchcraft – The Museum of Cambridge</span></span></span>

## The house as a map of danger

Fenland protection charms make most sense when read as a map of the house. Each place had a job.

The **window** was a watching point and a weak point. A witch ball hung there was meant to catch the eye, refract light and confuse or absorb hostile attention. The **chimney and hearth** were even more important because they were open to the sky and central to family life. This explains why bottles, shoes and other objects are so often associated with fireplaces, hearths and chimney spaces in British house-protection traditions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.museumofcambridge.org.uk/2024/10/folklore-finds-witch-balls/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: museumofcambridge.org.uk">[The Museum of Cambridge]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">museumofcambridge.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Museum of Cambridge Folklore Finds: Witch Balls – The Museum of Cambridge</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Museum of Cambridge Folklore Finds: Witch Balls – The Museum of Cambridge</span></span></span>

The **floor, wall and foundation** turned the whole building into a charm. Hidden objects were not always meant to be seen. They could be buried within walls or floors, becoming part of the house’s unseen defences. The Museum of Cambridge records local protective material discovered in buildings, including a bone found in 1959 in the foundations of the former White Horse Inn stables, later part of the museum site.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.museumofcambridge.org.uk/2018/01/safeguards-against-witchcraft/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: museumofcambridge.org.uk">[The Museum of Cambridge]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">museumofcambridge.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Museum of Cambridge Safeguards Against Witchcraft – The Museum of Cambridge</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Museum of Cambridge Safeguards Against Witchcraft – The Museum of Cambridge</span></span></span>

The **chair** mattered because it was a social threshold rather than an architectural one. A suspected witch might be allowed indoors, but the household could still act. A key or scissors placed under the chair turned hospitality into containment: the guest was present, but the household secretly controlled the danger.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.museumofcambridge.org.uk/2018/01/safeguards-against-witchcraft/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: museumofcambridge.org.uk">[The Museum of Cambridge]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">museumofcambridge.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Museum of Cambridge Safeguards Against Witchcraft – The Museum of Cambridge</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Museum of Cambridge Safeguards Against Witchcraft – The Museum of Cambridge</span></span></span>

This is the most useful way to understand Fenland household protection. It was not a random jumble of charms. It treated the home as a set of entrances, cavities and contact points. The witch might be outside, inside, disguised, represented by an animal, or acting from a distance, so protection had to be layered.

## Fear, accusation and everyday protection

The darker side of these traditions is that protection and accusation fed one another. If a household believed a neighbour had bewitched its children, livestock or luck, then charms might feel like self-defence. But the same belief could make a vulnerable person dangerous in the eyes of the village.

Young stresses that witches were often imagined as marginal people, especially poor women, widows or unmarried women who might be accused of envy and supernatural malice. In rural communities, illness in children and cattle and sour milk were among the common misfortunes blamed on witches.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://drfrancisyoung.com/wp-content/uploads/2014/01/witches-witchcraft-in-ely.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: drfrancisyoung.com">[Francis Young]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">drfrancisyoung.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Francis Young</span></span></span>

This point is important for modern readers because Fenland witchcraft should not be romanticised as harmless quaintness. A witch ball in a window is beautiful; a bottle hidden in a hearth is fascinating; but the fear behind them could lead to surveillance, humiliation and violence. Young notes that after formal witch trials declined, suspected witches could still face vigilante justice in their communities.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://drfrancisyoung.com/wp-content/uploads/2014/01/witches-witchcraft-in-ely.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: drfrancisyoung.com">[Francis Young]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">drfrancisyoung.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Francis Young</span></span></span>

At the same time, the evidence does not support a simple picture in which all belief was imposed from above by judges or clergy. The material collected by Porter and discussed by the Museum of Cambridge shows householders using counter-rituals themselves. Ordinary people did not merely fear witchcraft; they acted on that fear through domestic objects, remembered instructions and inherited local practice.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.museumofcambridge.org.uk/2018/01/safeguards-against-witchcraft/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: museumofcambridge.org.uk">[The Museum of Cambridge]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">museumofcambridge.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Museum of Cambridge Safeguards Against Witchcraft – The Museum of Cambridge</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Museum of Cambridge Safeguards Against Witchcraft – The Museum of Cambridge</span></span></span>

This is what gives the Cambridgeshire material its force. The boundary between “official” witchcraft history and “folk” household protection is porous. The trial records show accusation in public; the hidden bottles and charms show anxiety in private.

## How old and well-attested is the tradition?

Fenland witch belief has several layers of evidence, and they should not be confused.

The earliest layer is legal and literary. Ely’s witchcraft history includes medieval legend, Reformation-era anxieties and the seventeenth-century witch-hunts associated with Hopkins and Stearne. This material is well-attested as history of belief and accusation, though not as evidence that supernatural events occurred.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://drfrancisyoung.com/wp-content/uploads/2014/01/witches-witchcraft-in-ely.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: drfrancisyoung.com">[Francis Young]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">drfrancisyoung.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Francis Young</span></span></span>

The second layer is oral tradition and local memory. Enid Porter’s work is central here. The Museum of Cambridge credits her with contributing hugely to understanding Cambridgeshire folklore through objects and oral histories, and says her book *Cambridgeshire Customs and Folklore* inspired its “Safeguards Against Witchcraft” exhibition. Young likewise calls Porter’s work the first serious collection of Fenland traditions and describes her 1969 synthesis as a definitive study of Cambridgeshire folklore.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.museumofcambridge.org.uk/2018/01/safeguards-against-witchcraft/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: museumofcambridge.org.uk">[The Museum of Cambridge]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">museumofcambridge.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Museum of Cambridge Safeguards Against Witchcraft – The Museum of Cambridge</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Museum of Cambridge Safeguards Against Witchcraft – The Museum of Cambridge</span></span></span>

The third layer is material culture. Witch balls, witch bottles, concealed shoes, bones and other objects give the tradition a physical presence. They do not always reveal exactly who placed them or what words were spoken over them, but their locations and contents show repeated patterns of protective thinking. The Museum of Cambridge’s collections include a dated Gamlingay witch ball and a witch bottle, while wider research on concealed objects notes that such items were commonly placed in chimneys, floors, walls, roofs, doorways and windows as forms of protection.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.museumofcambridge.org.uk/2024/10/folklore-finds-witch-balls/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: museumofcambridge.org.uk">[The Museum of Cambridge]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">museumofcambridge.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Museum of Cambridge Folklore Finds: Witch Balls – The Museum of Cambridge</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Museum of Cambridge Folklore Finds: Witch Balls – The Museum of Cambridge</span></span></span>

The final layer is modern retelling. Ely and Cambridge museums, local-history groups and folklore writers now present these stories as heritage. That changes their function. A witch bottle no longer protects the hearth; it teaches visitors how earlier households understood danger, blame and safety.

## Why this belongs to Cambridgeshire

Fenland witchcraft is not unique to Cambridgeshire, but Cambridgeshire gives it a distinctive setting. The historic Isle of Ely forms the northern half of Cambridgeshire, including the hundreds of Wisbech, Witchford and Ely, and is described by Wikishire as a flat, wide-horizoned landscape shaped by drained fenland.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://wikishire.co.uk/wiki/Isle_of_Ely" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wikishire.co.uk">[Wikishire]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wikishire.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Isle of Ely</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Isle of Ely</span></span></span>

That landscape matters. The Fens were watery, difficult, scattered and long remembered as separate from the drier south of the county. Modern administrative Cambridgeshire has changed, but the folklore centre of gravity here is the old county and especially the Isle of Ely: Ely as legal and religious hub, Wisbech and Witchford as fenland districts, and the villages where Porter’s informants preserved stories of imps, charms and counter-magic.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://wikishire.co.uk/wiki/Isle_of_Ely" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wikishire.co.uk">[Wikishire]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wikishire.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Isle of Ely</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Isle of Ely</span></span></span>

This also explains why the tradition links naturally to neighbouring East Anglian folklore. Imps, witch bottles, cunning folk and household charms appear beyond Cambridgeshire, especially across East Anglia and the wider Fenland region. But the Cambridgeshire version is anchored by Ely’s witch-trial history, Porter’s collecting, and the Museum of Cambridge’s surviving objects.

## How the tradition is retold today

Today, Fenland witchcraft is usually encountered through museums, local history, Halloween events, folklore books and online heritage pages rather than through living fear of neighbours. The Museum of Cambridge places witch balls and witch bottles within its Fen and Folklore material, and its public interpretation explains them as protective objects once used against witches and evil forces.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.museumofcambridge.org.uk/2024/10/folklore-finds-witch-balls/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: museumofcambridge.org.uk">[The Museum of Cambridge]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">museumofcambridge.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Museum of Cambridge Folklore Finds: Witch Balls – The Museum of Cambridge</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Museum of Cambridge Folklore Finds: Witch Balls – The Museum of Cambridge</span></span></span>

This modern framing is useful because it shifts attention from “were witches real?” to a better question: what did people think danger looked like, and how did they try to protect themselves? The answer is more interesting than a simple ghost story. Fenland households imagined danger as mobile, sneaky and able to cross ordinary boundaries. Their safeguards answered that fear with objects that could catch light, trap spirits, bind harmful magic or guard the hidden cavities of the home.

The result is one of Cambridgeshire’s strongest folklore mechanisms. Fenland witchcraft turned everyday architecture into a supernatural defence system. The window dazzled, the bottle trapped, the hearth contained, the threshold warned, and the hidden charm kept working silently in the wall long after the original fear had been forgotten.
