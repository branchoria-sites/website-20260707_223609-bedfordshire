## Introduction

Northampton’s witch lore is not one simple tale of “old witches” and village fear. It is a sharp local example of how three things overlapped in [Northamptonshire]({{ 'where-northamptonshires-strange-stories/' | relative_url }}): the lethal court culture of early seventeenth-century witchcraft, the printed panic of London pamphlets, and the everyday counter-magic of charms, amulets and cunning folk. The best-known event is the Northamptonshire Witch Trials of 1612, when Agnes Browne, Joan Vaughan or Varnham, Arthur Bill, Helen Jenkenson and Mary Barber were reported as executed at Northampton on 22 July. But the surviving evidence is awkward, partial and sometimes contradictory, so the story matters as much for how it was told as for what happened.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://quod.lib.umich.edu/e/eebo/A17030.0001.001?view=toc" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: quod.lib.umich.edu">[Quod Libet]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">quod.lib.umich.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on umich.edu.</span></span></span>



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/Northamptonshire_ad0dee_northampton_witch_tr_8d0ba5-overview.webp" | relative_url }}" alt="Overview image for Witches" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

For Northamptonshire folklore, the trials sit beside a quieter but more persistent layer of belief: charms against illness, protection for horses and homes, “lucky bones”, holed stones, horseshoes, crossed straws and the local figure of the cunning man or charmer. These traditions show a county where people feared harmful witchcraft, but also believed that illness, cattle trouble and uncanny misfortune could be resisted through practical, household forms of counter-magic.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/stream/dialectfolkloreo00steruoft/dialectfolkloreo00steruoft_djvu.txt" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Internet Archive Full text of &quot;The dialect and folk-lore of Northamptonshire</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Internet Archive Full text of &quot;The dialect and folk-lore of Northamptonshire</span></span></span>

## The 1612 Northampton executions

The core printed source is the 1612 pamphlet *The witches of Northampton-shire*, published in London by Thomas Purfoot for Arthur Johnson. Its title names Agnes Browne, Ioane Vaughan, Arthur Bill, Hellen Ienkenson and Mary Barber as witches “executed at Northampton the 22. of Iuly last. 1612”, and the Early English Books Online record identifies it as a twenty-eight-page early modern work on witchcraft in England.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://quod.lib.umich.edu/e/eebo/A17030.0001.001?view=toc" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: quod.lib.umich.edu">[Quod Libet]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">quod.lib.umich.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on umich.edu.</span></span></span>

The event was rooted in the legal world of Jacobean England. Parliament notes that witchcraft was first made a capital crime in 1542, restored under Elizabeth I in 1562, and further addressed in 1604 under James I, who had a strong personal interest in demonology. The 1562 and 1604 Acts moved witchcraft cases from church jurisdiction into the ordinary courts, which is why Northampton Castle and the assizes matter in this story.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.parliament.uk/about/living-heritage/transformingsociety/private-lives/religion/overview/witchcraft/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: parliament.uk">[Parliament UK News]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">parliament.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on parliament.uk.</span></span></span>

The named places give the trial its Northamptonshire shape. Guilsborough is central because Agnes Browne and her daughter Joan Vaughan or Varnham were associated with the village, while the wider group of accusations reached Raunds, Thrapston and Stanwick. A modern Guilsborough Parish Council summary states that four women and one man were hanged at Abington Gallows, and that the Guilsborough pair were accused of bewitching Elizabeth Belcher and Master Avery, as well as causing the deaths of a child and livestock.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://guilsborough-pc.gov.uk/parish-history-and-folklore" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: guilsborough-pc.gov.uk">[guilsborough-pc.gov.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">guilsborough-pc.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Parish History and Folklore – Guilsborough Parish Council</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Parish History and Folklore – Guilsborough Parish Council</span></span></span>

Yet even this apparently firm story becomes uncertain once the sources are compared. Marion Gibson’s study in *Northamptonshire Past and Present* explains that the case survives in two different forms: the printed London pamphlet and a related manuscript account, British Library Sloane 972, folio 7. Gibson stresses that the pamphlet was written commercially for a London readership and appears to distort important details, while the manuscript seems better informed but also incomplete.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.northamptonshirerecordsociety.org.uk/pdf/npp/number/npp-n51.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: northamptonshirerecordsociety.org.uk">[Northamptonshire Record Society]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">northamptonshirerecordsociety.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">npp n51</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">npp n51</span></span></span>

The longest part of both accounts concerns Elizabeth Belcher and Master William Avery. Gibson identifies Elizabeth as the wife of Dabriscourt Belcher and notes that the pamphlet presents her as a virtuous gentlewoman, even though the same narrative begins the trouble with Elizabeth striking Joan Vaughan. That detail is crucial: the witchcraft accusation did not appear from nowhere, but from a social conflict in which illness, status, anger and fear were interpreted through the language of bewitchment.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.northamptonshirerecordsociety.org.uk/pdf/npp/number/npp-n51.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: northamptonshirerecordsociety.org.uk">[Northamptonshire Record Society]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">northamptonshirerecordsociety.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">npp n51</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">npp n51</span></span></span>

The printed pamphlet also shaped the story visually. Gibson reproduces and discusses a title-page woodcut showing three witches riding on a sow, probably made for the pamphlet. Guilsborough’s modern folklore page still preserves a later version of this motif, telling of Mother Roades of Ravensthorpe and women riding on a sow between Guilsborough and Ravensthorpe, while warning that names change from version to version. This is a good example of how a courtroom panic became a village legend, and how a printed image may have helped fix the strangest part of the story in popular memory.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.northamptonshirerecordsociety.org.uk/pdf/npp/number/npp-n51.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: northamptonshirerecordsociety.org.uk">[Northamptonshire Record Society]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">northamptonshirerecordsociety.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">npp n51</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">npp n51</span></span></span>

Arthur Bill’s case adds another dark feature: the water ordeal, or “swimming” of suspected witches. Wallace Notestein’s history records that in the Northampton cases one accused woman was cast into water with hands and feet bound and “could not sink”, and that a similar experiment was applied to Arthur Bill and his parents after he was accused of bewitching Martha Aspine. Notestein is careful that we do not know how far this influenced the assize court itself, but the case shows how an unofficial or semi-official test could harden suspicion into apparent proof.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.free-ebooks.net/academic-history/A-History-of-Witchcraft-in-England/html/4" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: free-ebooks.net">[Free-eBooks.net]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">free-ebooks.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on free-ebooks.net.</span></span></span>

## Cunning men and counter-charms

The Northampton trials show witchcraft as prosecution; Thomas Sternberg’s *The Dialect and Folk-lore of Northamptonshire* shows witchcraft as everyday belief. Published in 1851, Sternberg’s work aimed to collect local speech, superstitions, fairy lore and rural traditions that he believed still survived among Northamptonshire people. His table of contents places “Witchcraft” and “Charming” side by side, which is exactly how the county’s folk belief seems to have worked: fear of harm was matched by techniques for protection and cure.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/stream/dialectfolkloreo00steruoft/dialectfolkloreo00steruoft_djvu.txt" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Internet Archive Full text of &quot;The dialect and folk-lore of Northamptonshire</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Internet Archive Full text of &quot;The dialect and folk-lore of Northamptonshire</span></span></span>

Sternberg says Northamptonshire tradition distinguished between the feared witch and the local counter-magical specialist. In his account, the “white witches” of older writers had their place in Northamptonshire occupied by the “cunning man”, who was believed to counteract spells. This does not mean such a person was harmless in every modern sense; rather, it shows that villagers could see magic as a contested field, with some people blamed for causing misfortune and others sought out for undoing it.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/stream/dialectfolkloreo00steruoft/dialectfolkloreo00steruoft_djvu.txt" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Internet Archive Full text of &quot;The dialect and folk-lore of Northamptonshire</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Internet Archive Full text of &quot;The dialect and folk-lore of Northamptonshire</span></span></span>

Protection could be physical and domestic. Sternberg records the use of the “lucky bone”, holed stones, horseshoes, crossed straws and knives laid on the cottage floor to ward off witchcraft. He also quotes Morton’s earlier *Natural History of Northamptonshire* on perforated pebbles hung in stables and at bedsides against witchcraft. These are not grand ceremonial rites; they are stable, hearth and threshold magic, belonging to the places where ordinary people feared illness, bad luck, restless animals and spiteful neighbours might enter.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/stream/dialectfolkloreo00steruoft/dialectfolkloreo00steruoft_djvu.txt" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Internet Archive Full text of &quot;The dialect and folk-lore of Northamptonshire</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Internet Archive Full text of &quot;The dialect and folk-lore of Northamptonshire</span></span></span>

Sternberg’s section on charming is especially revealing. He says few villages in Northamptonshire, especially in the southern district, lacked an old woman who claimed to cure diseases by charming. The cure required belief, avoided the ordinary politeness of “please” and “thank you”, and usually involved crossing the afflicted part while whispering secret words. In some cases, blessed cords or leather thongs were worn around the neck; near Brackley, he records a woman known for curing agues by such means.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/stream/dialectfolkloreo00steruoft/dialectfolkloreo00steruoft_djvu.txt" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Internet Archive Full text of &quot;The dialect and folk-lore of Northamptonshire</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Internet Archive Full text of &quot;The dialect and folk-lore of Northamptonshire</span></span></span>

This is where “cunning folk” should be understood carefully. They were not simply stage magicians or later romantic witches. In Northamptonshire evidence, they appear as local healers, charmers and counter-spell workers whose authority depended on secrecy, reputation and inherited formulae. Sternberg says one old woman claimed to have received her charm from her mother, who had received it from hers, and that payment was thought to break the spell. That detail matters because it places charm-working inside kinship, trust and gift culture rather than straightforward trade.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/stream/dialectfolkloreo00steruoft/dialectfolkloreo00steruoft_djvu.txt" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Internet Archive Full text of &quot;The dialect and folk-lore of Northamptonshire</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Internet Archive Full text of &quot;The dialect and folk-lore of Northamptonshire</span></span></span>

The same world also helps explain why accusations could be so dangerous. If people believed a charm could stop bleeding, cure an ague or protect a stable, then a neighbour’s curse or ill-wishing could also feel practical and immediate. Witchcraft belief was not only about theology; it was about whether the horse sickened, the child fitted, the beer soured, the cow failed, or the body suffered pains that ordinary medicine did not explain.

## Why witchcraft evidence must be read carefully

The most important lesson from Northampton’s witch lore is that a source is not the same thing as a neutral window. Gibson’s article is blunt on this point: history here was written by prosecutors, by ill-informed or tendentious narrators, and by writers whose accounts were shaped by the expectations of readers. She argues that the two surviving versions of the Northampton cases both preserve valuable information, but also force us to question what “accuracy” means when all stories are built from subjective perceptions of a fiercely contested reality.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.northamptonshirerecordsociety.org.uk/pdf/npp/number/npp-n51.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: northamptonshirerecordsociety.org.uk">[Northamptonshire Record Society]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">northamptonshirerecordsociety.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">npp n51</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">npp n51</span></span></span>

The pamphlet is especially risky because it is not just reporting; it is persuading. Gibson describes its tone as polemical, angry and gloating, and notes that it presents Agnes Browne and Joan Vaughan through stock accusations of poverty, wickedness and bad character. The early modern “witch” was often stereotyped as poor, spiteful and socially marginal, but Gibson warns that stereotype and reality did not always match.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.northamptonshirerecordsociety.org.uk/pdf/npp/number/npp-n51.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: northamptonshirerecordsociety.org.uk">[Northamptonshire Record Society]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">northamptonshirerecordsociety.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">npp n51</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">npp n51</span></span></span>

The manuscript is cooler, but not perfect. Gibson notes that it has more specific knowledge of dates and events and may draw on legal material from the Northampton Assizes, but it lacks trial outcomes and remains selective. It may reflect someone who saw and heard Elizabeth Belcher give evidence at Northampton Castle, yet even that does not make it complete or impartial.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.northamptonshirerecordsociety.org.uk/pdf/npp/number/npp-n51.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: northamptonshirerecordsociety.org.uk">[Northamptonshire Record Society]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">northamptonshirerecordsociety.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">npp n51</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">npp n51</span></span></span>

This matters for modern retelling. A folklore page can say that five people were reported as executed in 1612, that Abington Gallows and Northampton Castle belong to the story, and that Guilsborough, Raunds, Thrapston and Stanwick are named in the tradition. But it should not repeat every pamphlet claim as fact. Claims about sow-riding, demonic meetings, magical attacks, floating bodies and deathbed prophecies should be treated as accusations, printed motifs or later legend unless there is firmer evidence.

It also matters because Northamptonshire’s witch lore has two different afterlives. One is tragic and legal: people were accused, tried and killed in a climate where misfortune could be turned into capital evidence. The other is folkloric and domestic: later villagers remembered witches, charms, lucky bones, horseshoes and cunning helpers as part of the old texture of rural life. The strongest modern reading keeps both in view without collapsing them together.

## What Northampton’s witch lore adds to the county story

Northamptonshire’s witch traditions are distinctive because they connect a nationally noticed trial with the small-scale magic of village life. The 1612 case belongs beside other Jacobean witch panics, including the better-known Lancashire trials later that year, but it has its own local geography: Northampton Castle, Abington Gallows, Guilsborough, Ravensthorpe, Raunds, Thrapston and Stanwick.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://quod.lib.umich.edu/e/eebo/A17030.0001.001?view=toc" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: quod.lib.umich.edu">[Quod Libet]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">quod.lib.umich.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on umich.edu.</span></span></span>

The county also preserves one of the clearest contrasts between prosecution and protection. In the courtroom, witchcraft meant felony, testimony, printed accusation and execution. In the cottage, it meant whispered charms, hidden knives, holed stones, a horseshoe over the threshold, a charm passed from mother to daughter, or a cunning man consulted to undo harm. Sternberg’s nineteenth-century collecting shows that these beliefs did not vanish when the witchcraft statutes disappeared; they remained in local memory, reshaped as superstition, anecdote and folklore.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.parliament.uk/about/living-heritage/transformingsociety/private-lives/religion/overview/witchcraft/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: parliament.uk">[parliament.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">parliament.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on parliament.uk.</span></span></span>

That is why Northampton’s witch lore is best read with sympathy and caution. Sympathy, because the accused were real people caught in a fearful legal and social world. Caution, because many of the most colourful details come from hostile pamphlets, repeated legends and belief systems that turned illness or conflict into supernatural evidence. The result is one of Northamptonshire’s most powerful folklore case-families: not a celebration of witch-hunting, but a reminder of how stories, fear, law and local memory can become dangerously entangled.
