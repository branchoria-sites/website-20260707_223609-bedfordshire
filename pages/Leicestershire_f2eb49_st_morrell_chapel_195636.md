## Introduction

St Morrell’s Chapel is one of [Leicestershire]({{ 'where-leicestershire-turns-history-into/' | relative_url }})’s clearest examples of folklore being sharpened, not weakened, by archaeology. For centuries [Hallaton]({{ 'hallaton/' | relative_url }}) had its Easter Monday Hare Pie Scramble and Bottle Kicking on Hare Pie Bank, a rough seasonal custom wrapped in local legend. Then excavations by Hallaton Fieldwork Group and University of Leicester Archaeological Services uncovered the lost medieval chapel of St Morrell, its cemetery, coins, building remains and a pilgrim badge naming Morrell on the same hillside. The result is a rare layered landscape: prehistoric and Roman ritual activity nearby, a medieval pilgrimage site, a Reformation-era loss of sacred practice, and a modern walking route that turns the old pilgrimage memory back into something people can experience on foot.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ulasnews.com/2014/09/16/hallatons-lost-chapel/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ulasnews.com">[ulasnews.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ulasnews.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ULAS News Hallaton’s Lost Chapel – ULAS News</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ULAS News Hallaton’s Lost Chapel – ULAS News</span></span></span>

## Who St Morrell Was in Hallaton Tradition

St Morrell is not a familiar national saint in the way that Cuthbert, Alban or Thomas Becket are. In Hallaton’s local telling, he is linked with Angers in north-west France and identified with a bishop who belonged to the early Christian world of the fifth century. Modern accounts connected with the St Morrell’s Round describe him as Bishop of Angers, dying in AD 453, and say that a later Norman family from Angers brought his cult to Hallaton when they established a chapel dedicated to him.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.britishpilgrimage.org/portfolio/st-morrells-round" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: britishpilgrimage.org">[British Pilgrimage Trust]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">britishpilgrimage.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">British Pilgrimage Trust St Morrell&#x27;s Round</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">British Pilgrimage TrustSt Morrell&#x27;s Round - British Pilgrimage Trust...</span></span></span>

That foreign connection matters because it helps explain why a small Leicestershire village might have had a chapel dedicated to such an otherwise obscure figure. The Diocese of Leicester’s account links the chapel’s foundation to Stephen Martival, described as a Norman lord who came from Angers and built the chapel around 1250. It presents Morrell as Martival’s patron saint and says the chapel became a focus for pilgrims for roughly three centuries, until the Reformation ended such forms of pilgrimage.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.leicester.anglican.org/news/launch-of-st-morrells-round-by-the-bishop-of-leicester.php" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: leicester.anglican.org">[Diocese of Leicester]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">leicester.anglican.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Diocese of Leicester Launch of St Morrell’s Round by the Bishop of Leicester</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Diocese of LeicesterLaunch of St Morrell’s Round by the Bishop of Leicester - Diocese of Leicester...</span></span></span>

The most intriguing part of the tradition is not that Hallaton had a chapel, but that Morrell seems so localised in England. The British Pilgrimage Trust notes that Hallaton is treated as the only church in Britain mentioning Morrell, which may have encouraged the belief that Morrell’s exile or saintly story somehow touched Hallaton itself. That is exactly the kind of local devotional memory that can become folklore: a saint from elsewhere is naturalised into one village’s sacred geography, and the village landscape begins to tell the saint’s story as if it had always belonged there.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.britishpilgrimage.org/portfolio/st-morrells-round" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: britishpilgrimage.org">[British Pilgrimage Trust]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">britishpilgrimage.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">British Pilgrimage Trust St Morrell&#x27;s Round</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">British Pilgrimage TrustSt Morrell&#x27;s Round - British Pilgrimage Trust...</span></span></span>

## The Rediscovered Chapel on Hare Pie Bank

Before modern fieldwork, the chapel’s exact location had been lost. According to the University of Leicester Archaeological Services account, local historian John Morison suggested that the site might lie on Hare Pie Bank, the same hillside associated with Hallaton’s Easter Monday custom. Geophysical survey by Hallaton Fieldwork Group then identified a square boundary about 36 metres across, and later excavation revealed the chapel plan, building material and associated cemetery.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ulasnews.com/2014/09/16/hallatons-lost-chapel/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ulasnews.com">[ULAS News]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ulasnews.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ULAS News Hallaton’s Lost Chapel – ULAS News</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ULAS News Hallaton’s Lost Chapel – ULAS News</span></span></span>

The archaeological evidence is unusually satisfying for a folklore landscape because it moves the story from vague “old site” language into visible material detail. The excavators found the chapel walls and tiled floor, as well as fragments of stonework, wall plaster, window lead and other traces of a once-finished medieval building. Silver pennies dating from the twelfth to sixteenth centuries helped indicate the period over which the site was used, while a pilgrim badge inscribed with “Morrell” directly tied the place to devotional travel rather than merely local memory.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ulasnews.com/2014/09/16/hallatons-lost-chapel/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ulasnews.com">[ULAS News]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ulasnews.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ULAS News Hallaton’s Lost Chapel – ULAS News</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ULAS News Hallaton’s Lost Chapel – ULAS News</span></span></span>

The cemetery is just as important. University of Leicester reported that eleven skeletons had been excavated at the time of the 2014 account, all laid east-west in Christian fashion and radiocarbon dated to the fourteenth century. One older male had a severe head injury consistent with a sharp weapon, while the wider cemetery raised a practical question: why were these people buried at a chapel on a hillside when Hallaton had its parish church nearby?<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ulasnews.com/2014/09/16/hallatons-lost-chapel/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ulasnews.com">[ULAS News]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ulasnews.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ULAS News Hallaton’s Lost Chapel – ULAS News</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ULAS News Hallaton’s Lost Chapel – ULAS News</span></span></span>

That question gives the site much of its power. The chapel was not just a decorative outpost. It seems to have been a place where people came, gave, prayed, bought or wore pilgrim souvenirs, and in some cases were buried. In folklore terms, Hare Pie Bank becomes more than the starting ground of a boisterous Easter game. It becomes a remembered sacred hill whose meanings were partly buried, then literally dug back into public awareness.

## Why the Cemetery Changed the Story

The chapel cemetery turned St Morrell from a name in local tradition into a human landscape. The well-publicised discovery of two skeletons apparently holding hands made the site briefly famous, but the deeper importance lies in what the burials say about attachment to place. The Council for British Archaeology summarised the excavations as uncovering both the lost chapel and a medieval graveyard at the spot where the Hallaton bottle kicking traditionally takes place every Easter.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archaeologyuk.org/resource/buildings--burials-and-bones--the-story-of-hallaton-s-lost-pilgrims-chapel.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archaeologyuk.org">[Archaeology UK]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archaeologyuk.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on archaeologyuk.org.</span></span></span>

The holding-hands burial is memorable because it invites a human story, but it should be handled carefully. It is tempting to turn the couple into a ready-made romance, yet the archaeologists’ more cautious question was why they were buried at the chapel at all. A chapel cemetery outside the main parish church suggests that St Morrell’s hillside may have carried special meaning for certain people, whether through pilgrimage, status, devotion, family memory, illness, exclusion, accident, or circumstances now lost to evidence.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.medievalists.net/2014/09/medieval-couple-holding-hands-700-years/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: medievalists.net">[Medievalists.net]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">medievalists.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">medieval couple holding hands 700 years</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">medieval couple holding hands 700 years</span></span></span>

This is where archaeology and folklore meet without needing to pretend that archaeology “proves” a legend. The excavation does not prove every story told about Hallaton’s Easter landscape. It does show that people in medieval Hallaton treated the hillside as a serious religious place. That changes how a reader should understand the later custom: Bottle Kicking is not simply happening in a random field with a picturesque name, but on ground already thick with ritual use, devotion and local memory.

## A Pilgrim Landscape Older Than the Chapel

St Morrell’s Chapel belongs to medieval Christianity, but the hill and its surroundings reach further back. The University of Leicester account says the excavations showed evidence that the hillside had been used since at least the Roman period. Local heritage material goes further in public-facing terms, describing Hare Pie Hill as a site in use for more than 2,000 years, a medieval pilgrimage place and probably the site of a Roman temple.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ulasnews.com/2014/09/16/hallatons-lost-chapel/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ulasnews.com">[ULAS News]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ulasnews.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ULAS News Hallaton’s Lost Chapel – ULAS News</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ULAS News Hallaton’s Lost Chapel – ULAS News</span></span></span>

Nearby Hallaton also has national archaeological significance because of its Iron Age and Roman ritual landscape. Historic England scheduled the Iron Age and Roman ritual site, settlement, enclosures and linear features east of Swallow Hill Farm in 2024, describing it as a Late Iron Age and early Roman open-air ritual site overlooking the Welland Valley. The listing calls it the first and currently only identified large open-air Iron Age shrine in England, with well-documented buried remains and exceptional potential for understanding pre-Roman ritual activity.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://historicengland.org.uk/listing/the-list/list-entry/1483470" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: historicengland.org.uk">[Historic England]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">historicengland.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on historicengland.org.uk.</span></span></span>

This does not mean that St Morrell’s Chapel is simply a Christian “replacement” for an earlier pagan shrine; that would be too neat. What can be said with more confidence is that Hallaton’s wider landscape repeatedly attracted ritual, gathering and memory. The medieval chapel, the saint’s cult, the burials, the Easter custom and the modern pilgrim route all sit within a district where people have long marked certain ridges, fields and routes as meaningful.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archaeologyuk.org/resource/buildings--burials-and-bones--the-story-of-hallaton-s-lost-pilgrims-chapel.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archaeologyuk.org">[Archaeology UK]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archaeologyuk.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on archaeologyuk.org.</span></span></span>

For Leicestershire folklore, that layering is the key point. Some county legends survive mainly as stories attached to a name. Hallaton gives something more tangible: a hillside where prehistoric ritual, medieval devotion and modern custom can be discussed together, while still keeping the evidence for each period distinct.

## Pilgrimage, Easter and the Hare Pie Bank Custom

Hallaton’s famous Easter Monday Hare Pie Scramble and Bottle Kicking is a separate tradition from the medieval pilgrimage, but the two now illuminate each other. Hallaton Museum describes the present custom as a day of processions, hare pie and penny loaves, followed by the movement to Hare Pie Bank where opposing teams battle to carry the “bottles” across their boundary line. The museum also notes that the contest’s origins are disputed and wrapped in old explanations rather than securely documented beginnings.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://hallaton-museum.co.uk/a-tale-of-rituals-relics-and-restoration/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: hallaton-museum.co.uk">[Hallaton Museum]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">hallaton-museum.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Hallaton Museum A Tale of rituals, relics and restoration – Hallaton Museum</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Hallaton Museum A Tale of rituals, relics and restoration – Hallaton Museum</span></span></span>

The important point for this page is not to retell the whole Bottle Kicking tradition, but to notice where it happens. The chapel site overlooks Hallaton and lies where the Easter custom begins. Local planning and heritage documents identify Hare Pie Bank as the site of Morrell’s chapel and state that the bottle kicking starts there, while quoting University of Leicester’s view that Roman archaeology beneath the medieval chapel suggests the hilltop has been a special place for more than 2,000 years.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.harborough.gov.uk/download/downloads/id/6762/hallaton_appendix_10_rev2_22-2-21.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: harborough.gov.uk">[Harborough District Council]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">harborough.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Harborough District Council</span></span></span>

This creates a striking before-and-after effect. Before excavation, the Easter custom could be read mainly as rough sport, village rivalry and seasonal festivity. After excavation, it also sits beside a rediscovered chapel, a medieval graveyard and a pilgrim economy. That does not make the custom “really” a survival of ancient religion, and it does not erase its later social history. It does, however, make Hare Pie Bank one of the county’s richest examples of a folk landscape: a place where play, devotion, violence, food, burial and memory have all gathered.

The “lost chapel” also helps explain why modern Hallaton can feel both ancient and local rather than merely picturesque. The Hallaton Neighbourhood Plan presents the village’s identity as built from deep layers: Iron Age importance, the Hallaton Hoard, the Norman motte and bailey, the excavated chapel, medieval markets and fairs, and the continuing Easter custom. That is community self-description rather than neutral folklore collection, but it shows how strongly St Morrell now belongs to Hallaton’s sense of itself.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.harborough.gov.uk/download/downloads/id/6763/hallaton_neighbourhood_plan_referendum_version_rev_1.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: harborough.gov.uk">[Harborough District Council]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">harborough.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Harborough District Council</span></span></span>

## St Morrell’s Round and the Revival of Walking Pilgrimage

The modern St Morrell’s Round turns the recovered pilgrimage story into a walkable route. The British Pilgrimage Trust describes it as an 18-mile Leicestershire circular pilgrimage, usually walked over two days and rated moderate. It starts and finishes at Hallaton Museum beside St Michael’s Church, then follows a route through High Leicestershire and Rutland, passing churches and places that would have been familiar in the medieval period.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.britishpilgrimage.org/portfolio/st-morrells-round" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: britishpilgrimage.org">[British Pilgrimage Trust]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">britishpilgrimage.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">British Pilgrimage Trust St Morrell&#x27;s Round</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">British Pilgrimage TrustSt Morrell&#x27;s Round - British Pilgrimage Trust...</span></span></span>

Crucially, the route does not claim to be the exact medieval path. The Hallaton Benefice account says there is no record of the precise routes medieval pilgrims took to and from Hallaton. Instead, St Morrell’s Round visits places where modern walkers can see buildings, landscapes and church settings that medieval travellers could plausibly have known, including Norman and pre-Reformation church architecture, fragments of ancient woodland and Launde Abbey.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ourbenefice.webador.co.uk/the-st-morrell-round" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ourbenefice.webador.co.uk">[ourbenefice.webador.co.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ourbenefice.webador.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The St. Morrell Round &#124; Ourbenefice</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The St. Morrell Round &#124; Ourbenefice</span></span></span>

That honesty is part of the route’s value. It avoids pretending that a continuous medieval itinerary has survived unchanged. Instead, it revives the idea of pilgrimage in a historically aware way: not as costume drama, but as a modern practice shaped by old churches, ridges, lanes, settlement memory and the rediscovered chapel. The Diocese of Leicester marked this revival in 2022, when the Bishop of Leicester launched the route and linked it with plans including a St Morrell tapestry and work to restore the crypt at St Michael’s Church for the reinterment of remains found during the chapel excavation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.leicester.anglican.org/news/launch-of-st-morrells-round-by-the-bishop-of-leicester.php" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: leicester.anglican.org">[Diocese of Leicester]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">leicester.anglican.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Diocese of Leicester Launch of St Morrell’s Round by the Bishop of Leicester</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Diocese of LeicesterLaunch of St Morrell’s Round by the Bishop of Leicester - Diocese of Leicester...</span></span></span>

For readers interested in folklore, this is modern tradition-making in plain sight. A lost sacred site is excavated; local historians, archaeologists, church members and villagers interpret it; a route is created; and the landscape begins to acquire new habits of storytelling. St Morrell’s Round is not a medieval survival in the simple sense. It is a twenty-first-century response to medieval evidence.

## Why St Morrell Matters to Leicestershire Folklore

St Morrell’s Chapel matters because it complicates the usual boundary between history and folklore. Many local legends survive because people kept telling them after the physical evidence vanished or became unclear. At Hallaton, the process has partly run in reverse: archaeology has restored a physical setting that makes older tradition more intelligible.

The chapel also gives Leicestershire a kind of saintly landscape that is different from the county’s better-known bogey figures and rough customs. [Black Annis]({{ 'black-annis/' | relative_url }}) belongs to frightening edge-of-town story; Hallaton’s Bottle Kicking belongs to seasonal disorder and village rivalry; St Morrell belongs to devotion, burial, walking, loss and recovery. Together they show how varied Leicestershire folklore is: not one mythic system, but a county of sharply local places where story clings to fields, hills, churches and roads.

Its strongest lesson is one of caution as well as wonder. The rediscovery of the chapel does not license every romantic claim about uninterrupted pagan survival, secret ancient rites or a single unbroken tradition running from the Iron Age to the present. The evidence supports something more interesting: Hallaton’s hillside has been repeatedly meaningful in different ways. A prehistoric ritual landscape, a possible Roman sacred site, a medieval pilgrim chapel, an Easter gathering place and a modern pilgrimage route are not the same thing, but they do rhyme across time.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://historicengland.org.uk/listing/the-list/list-entry/1483470" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: historicengland.org.uk">[historicengland.org.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">historicengland.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on historicengland.org.uk.</span></span></span>

That is why St Morrell’s Chapel is now one of Leicestershire’s most useful folklore sites. It shows how local tradition can be buried, doubted, rediscovered, reinterpreted and walked again. The lost chapel behind Hallaton’s legends is no longer just a name in village memory; it is a mapped, excavated and retold place where Leicestershire’s sacred past and living custom meet on the same hillside.
