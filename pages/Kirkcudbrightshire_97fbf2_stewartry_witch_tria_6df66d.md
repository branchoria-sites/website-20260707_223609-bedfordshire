## Introduction

[Kirkcudbrightshire]({{ 'kirkcudbrightshire/' | relative_url }})’s witch-trial history is not mainly a tale of broomsticks, covens or monsters. It is a record of community fear: neighbours blaming illness, sour milk, dead livestock, bad luck and household quarrels on women who were already vulnerable or unpopular. In the Stewartry of Kirkcudbright, the most telling cases come from kirk-session minutes, burgh records, Privy Council commissions, later antiquarian extracts and modern scholarship on south-west Scottish witch belief.



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/Kirkcudbrightshire_97fbf2_stewartry_witch_tria_6df66d-overview.webp" | relative_url }}" alt="Overview image for Witch Trials" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

The county’s witchcraft memory matters because it shows folklore at its most dangerous. Belief was not just a story told by the fireside; it could move from gossip to church discipline, from local suspicion to imprisonment in Kirkcudbright Tolbooth, and in Elspeth McEwan’s case to execution at Silver Craigs in 1698. A century later, Jean Maxwell’s 1805 case shows a changed legal world: she was punished not for being a witch, but for pretending to possess supernatural powers. That shift did not mean fear had vanished. It meant fear had found a new legal shape.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/files/43966/43966-h/43966-h.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Project Gutenberg</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Witchcraft and Superstitious Record in the South-Western District of Scotland, by J. Maxwell Wood—A Project Gutenberg eBook...</span></span></span>

## Why the Stewartry’s witch stories feel so local

The Stewartry witch records are powerful because they are domestic. Lizanne Henderson’s study of witchcraft and witch belief in south-west Scotland stresses that, in this region, trial evidence shows comparatively little elaborate demonic material. The accusations are usually rooted in “the troubles of everyday life”: disputes between neighbours, agricultural problems, family tensions, livestock misfortune and suspicion of people who did not fit comfortably into the community.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://eprints.gla.ac.uk/7706/1/Lizanne2.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: eprints.gla.ac.uk">[Enlighten Publications]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">eprints.gla.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on gla.ac.uk.</span></span></span>

That makes Kirkcudbrightshire’s witchcraft tradition different from a simple ghost story or a spectacular legend. It belongs to the same broad world as charms, omens, fairy belief and local supernatural explanation, but the documents show how belief could be enforced by institutions. The same anxious culture that could explain a sick cow through witchcraft could also call in the kirk session, send a woman to the Tolbooth, or gather witnesses to turn rumour into evidence.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/files/43966/43966-h/43966-h.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Witchcraft and Superstitious Record in the South-Western District of Scotland, by J. Maxwell Wood—A Project Gutenberg eBook...</span></span></span>

The Stewartry was also a late and revealing setting in the wider Scottish story. Henderson notes that Dumfries and Galloway, including Kirkcudbrightshire and Wigtownshire, was a relative latecomer to witch-hunting compared with some other parts of Scotland, but witch belief there remained resilient after the major Scottish witch-hunting outbreaks had declined elsewhere.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://eprints.gla.ac.uk/7706/1/Lizanne2.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: eprints.gla.ac.uk">[Enlighten Publications]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">eprints.gla.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on gla.ac.uk.</span></span></span>

## Milk, livestock and household misfortune

The most revealing Stewartry accusations are not grand claims about midnight assemblies. They are claims about food, animals and the fragile economy of ordinary homes. In a rural county, a cow that stopped giving usable milk, a horse that died after a quarrel, hens that failed to lay, or a child who wasted away were not minor inconveniences. They threatened livelihood, health and reputation.

Elspeth McEwan of Bogha, near Cubbox in Balmaclellan, was suspected by the countryside of “witching” before the case became a formal legal matter. J. Maxwell Wood’s 1911 antiquarian collection preserves a tradition that she kept a movable wooden pin in the rafters of her cottage. By touching a neighbour’s cow with it, she was said to be able to draw an unnatural supply of milk. The same body of tradition accused her of interfering with hens, making them either stop laying or produce with strange abundance.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/files/43966/43966-h/43966-h.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Witchcraft and Superstitious Record in the South-Western District of Scotland, by J. Maxwell Wood—A Project Gutenberg eBook...</span></span></span>

That detail matters because milk was one of the classic pressure points of witch fear. It was visible, valuable and mysterious enough to invite explanation when it failed. If a cow’s milk turned foul, if butter would not come, or if a household seemed to prosper at another’s expense, the language of witchcraft gave the community a culprit.

Other Kirkcudbrightshire cases show the same pattern. Janet M‘Robert of Milnburn was accused in 1701 after a chain of domestic claims: a woman’s breast swelling, a nursing child wasting away, a cow’s milk losing its normal colour and taste, hens, dogs, borrowed money, strange noises and apparitions all entered the record. The evidence was not treated as one isolated wonder, but as a pattern of neighbourhood misfortune clustering around a suspect woman.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/files/43966/43966-h/43966-h.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Witchcraft and Superstitious Record in the South-Western District of Scotland, by J. Maxwell Wood—A Project Gutenberg eBook...</span></span></span>

The Twynholm case of Jean M‘Murrie in 1703 repeats the logic. Witnesses said she had long carried the reputation of a witch, and the accusations included refused charity, anger after a dispute, sickness, death, a horse sweating until it died, and milk becoming useless with a foul smell. The pattern is painfully human: someone asks, borrows, bargains or quarrels; something bad happens afterwards; the community reads the sequence as supernatural revenge.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/files/43966/43966-h/43966-h.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Witchcraft and Superstitious Record in the South-Western District of Scotland, by J. Maxwell Wood—A Project Gutenberg eBook...</span></span></span>

## Elspeth McEwan and the 1698 execution memory

Elspeth McEwan is the central Kirkcudbrightshire witch-trial memory because her story joins parish suspicion, legal machinery and a named place of execution. Kirkcudbright History Society identifies her as the last woman burned for witchcraft in Dumfries and Galloway, with alleged offences in Balmaclellan parish, an initial appearance before Dalry Kirk Session, imprisonment in Kirkcudbright Tolbooth and execution at Silver Craigs in 1698.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.kirkcudbrighthistorysociety.org.uk/news/elspeth-mcewan-the-last-woman-burned-for-witchcraft/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: kirkcudbrighthistorysociety.org.uk">[kirkcudbrighthistorysociety.org.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">kirkcudbrighthistorysociety.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Elspeth McEwan: the last woman burned for witchcraft.23 Dec 2025 — She was held in the Tolbooth for two years before her execution in 169...</span></span></span>

The route of the case shows how local fear became state violence. According to Wood’s extracts, complaint was made to the Dalry kirk session, and the beadle was sent with the minister’s mare to bring Elspeth in. A local tradition later attached itself to the journey: the mare supposedly sweated drops of blood at a rising hill near the manse, remembered as “Bluidy Brae”. Whether read as literal belief or later legendary embroidery, the detail shows how legal memory and folklore fused around the case.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/files/43966/43966-h/43966-h.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Witchcraft and Superstitious Record in the South-Western District of Scotland, by J. Maxwell Wood—A Project Gutenberg eBook...</span></span></span>

Once sent to Kirkcudbright, Elspeth lay in prison for about two years. A Dalry Kirk Session entry from 15 October 1697 recorded payment for her maintenance while imprisoned on suspicion of witchcraft. Wood’s account says she was treated so harshly in Kirkcudbright prison that she begged for death, a grim reminder that confessions in witch cases cannot be read as calm admissions of guilt.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/files/43966/43966-h/43966-h.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Witchcraft and Superstitious Record in the South-Western District of Scotland, by J. Maxwell Wood—A Project Gutenberg eBook...</span></span></span>

The Privy Council commission of March 1698 is especially important because it survives in formal language. It describes Elspeth McCourn, or McEwan in other spellings, and Mary Millar as prisoners in the Tolbooth of Kirkcudbright, accused within the Stewartry of the “horrid crime” of witchcraft. The council authorised named commissioners to meet at Kirkcudbright, summon jurors and witnesses, try the case locally, and, if guilt was found, sentence the accused to be burned or otherwise executed, while requiring further approval before carrying out the sentence.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://spcr.ac.uk/commission-by-the-council-1-march-1698-edinburgh/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: spcr.ac.uk">[Scottish Privy Council Records]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">spcr.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Scottish Privy Council Records</span></span></span>

Elspeth was ultimately found guilty on confession and witness evidence of compact with the Devil, charms and harmful acts. The Privy Council ordered the death sentence to be carried out under the care of the Steward of Kirkcudbright and his deputies. Wood records that she was executed at what is now Silver Craigs Park on 24 August 1698.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/files/43966/43966-h/43966-h.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Witchcraft and Superstitious Record in the South-Western District of Scotland, by J. Maxwell Wood—A Project Gutenberg eBook...</span></span></span>

The surviving expense accounts make the episode feel chillingly ordinary. They include payments for candles on the night the assize sat, payments connected with William Kirk, food and drink, and expenses around the day of execution. These mundane entries are part of the horror: the death of an accused witch was also an administered event, paid for and recorded by local authorities.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/files/43966/43966-h/43966-h.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Witchcraft and Superstitious Record in the South-Western District of Scotland, by J. ## What fear did to neighbours

The records are not just about accused women; they are about the behaviour of communities under fear. A witch accusation could gather old quarrels, poverty, charity disputes and dislike into a single story. The accused person became the explanation for everything that had gone wrong.

Several repeated features stand out in the Stewartry material:

**Refused help could become dangerous.** In the Jean M‘Murrie material, a request for food or charity followed by misfortune became a suspicious sequence. If a marginal woman asked for something and was refused, any later illness, foul milk or livestock trouble could be read as revenge.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/files/43966/43966-h/43966-h.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Witchcraft and Superstitious Record in the South-Western District of Scotland, by J. Maxwell Wood—A Project Gutenberg eBook...</span></span></span>

**Reputation mattered before evidence.** Witnesses in the Twynholm case stated that Jean M‘Murrie had long been “under the name of a witch” by report of the country. That phrase is revealing: suspicion could precede the incident, shaping how each later misfortune was interpreted.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/files/43966/43966-h/43966-h.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Witchcraft and Superstitious Record in the South-Western District of Scotland, by J. Maxwell Wood—A Project Gutenberg eBook...</span></span></span>

**The kirk session acted as a bridge.** Local church courts did not merely preserve gossip; they could gather declarations, recommend incarceration and move a case towards civil authority. In Janet M‘Robert’s case, the Kirkcudbright session recommended that magistrates apprehend and imprison her until trial could be had.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/files/43966/43966-h/43966-h.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Witchcraft and Superstitious Record in the South-Western District of Scotland, by J. Maxwell Wood—A Project Gutenberg eBook...</span></span></span>

**Not every accusation produced an execution.** Janet M‘Robert’s case also shows limits in the system. When extracts of the accusations were sent to Edinburgh, a commission for prosecution was refused because the material was judged insufficient to found a legal process. Yet even then she agreed to banishment and left for Ireland, showing that acquittal or non-prosecution did not necessarily restore a person’s place in the community.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/files/43966/43966-h/43966-h.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Witchcraft and Superstitious Record in the South-Western District of Scotland, by J. One of the most striking side episodes in Elspeth McEwan’s case concerns Janet Corbie, who reportedly tried to persuade Elspeth not to confess and said that people endangered their souls by calling her a witch. Instead of appearing as a heroic modern sceptic in the record, Corbie herself was treated as scandalous and disruptive, imprisoned briefly, expelled across the ferry, and forbidden from returning to local society. The episode shows how fear policed not only the accused, but also those who challenged the accusation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/files/43966/43966-h/43966-h.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Witchcraft and Superstitious Record in the South-Western District of Scotland, by J. ## Jean Maxwell and the shift to pretended witchcraft

Jean Maxwell’s 1805 trial at Kirkcudbright belongs to a different legal age, but it sits naturally in the Stewartry witch-trial afterlife. By then, the law no longer punished a person for being an actual witch in the old sense. The Witchcraft Act of 1735 had repealed older witchcraft statutes and created an offence around pretending to use witchcraft, sorcery, enchantment, conjuration, fortune-telling or occult knowledge. Wood’s account of the Jean Maxwell trial quotes this legal framework and emphasises the key point: she was arraigned not for being a witch, but for pretending to possess witch power.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/files/43966/43966-h/43966-h.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Witchcraft and Superstitious Record in the South-Western District of Scotland, by J. Maxwell Wood—A Project Gutenberg eBook...</span></span></span>

The trial still shows the persistence of fear. Maxwell was accused of pretending to exercise witchcraft and undertaking to tell fortunes. The court found her guilty and sentenced her to one year in Kirkcudbright Tolbooth without bail, with public punishment once every quarter at the market cross. Wood records that the sentence was rigorously carried out.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/files/43966/43966-h/43966-h.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Witchcraft and Superstitious Record in the South-Western District of Scotland, by J. Maxwell Wood—A Project Gutenberg eBook...</span></span></span>

The printed pamphlet, *Remarkable Trial of Jean Maxwell, the Galloway Sorceress*, was published at Kirkcudbright in 1805, giving the case a public afterlife almost immediately. Bibliographer John Ferguson later highlighted the importance of the word “pretending”, observing that the indictment fell under the post-1735 legal regime rather than the older capital witchcraft law.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/files/43966/43966-h/43966-h.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Witchcraft and Superstitious Record in the South-Western District of Scotland, by J. Maxwell Wood—A Project Gutenberg eBook...</span></span></span>

For folklore readers, Jean Maxwell is important because she marks a change from supernatural crime to fraudulent performance. Yet the public setting of her punishment — the Tolbooth, the market cross, the pillory — means the case still belonged to communal drama. The law may have changed its theory of witchcraft, but reputation, fear, shame and spectacle remained active in Kirkcudbright. Modern tourism and storytelling continue to retell Maxwell’s case as “The Galloway Sorceress”, often stressing that she was tried for claiming such powers rather than for actually practising witchcraft.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://scotlandstartshere.com/event/jean-maxwell-the-galloway-sorceress/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: scotlandstartshere.com">[Scotland Starts Here]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">scotlandstartshere.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Scotland Starts Here Jean Maxwell: The Galloway Sorceress &#124; South of Scotland</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Scotland Starts Here Jean Maxwell: The Galloway Sorceress &#124; South of Scotland</span></span></span>

## What these cases reveal about Kirkcudbrightshire folklore

The Stewartry witch trials reveal a form of folklore that was embedded in everyday explanation. People did not need a spectacular monster when a neighbour’s anger, a failed milking, a sick child or a dead horse could seem terrifying enough. The supernatural entered through the ordinary.

That is why these records belong in Kirkcudbrightshire’s folklore landscape alongside fairy hills, haunted places, charms and local customs. They show belief being used to explain uncertainty and misfortune, but also show the danger of turning belief into accusation. The old stories are not neutral curiosities. They carry memories of imprisonment, banishment, public humiliation and death.

They also show the county’s historic geography at work. The Stewartry of Kirkcudbright was not just a picturesque district within modern Dumfries and Galloway; it was a legal and communal setting where parish, burgh and national authority intersected. Balmaclellan, Dalry, Twynholm, Tongland, Kirkcudbright Tolbooth, the market cross and Silver Craigs all appear not as decorative backdrops, but as working places in the machinery of suspicion.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/files/43966/43966-h/43966-h.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Project Gutenberg</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Witchcraft and Superstitious Record in the South-Western District of Scotland, by J. Maxwell Wood—A Project Gutenberg eBook...</span></span></span>

Modern readers often approach witch stories looking for mystery. The Stewartry material offers something more unsettling: a record of how fear behaves when it is shared, repeated, institutionalised and given a culprit. Elspeth McEwan’s death and Jean Maxwell’s punishment sit more than a century apart, but together they show the long afterlife of magical accusation in Kirkcudbrightshire — from lethal witch prosecution to the public punishment of pretended sorcery, and from court record to local memory.

    if (select) {
      select.addEventListener('change', function () {
        applyMarket(section, select.value, true);
      });
    }
    var trigger = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-trigger]');
    var menu = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-menu]');
    if (trigger && menu) {
      trigger.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
        event.stopPropagation();
        var expanded = trigger.getAttribute('aria-expanded') === 'true';
        menu.hidden = expanded;
        trigger.setAttribute('aria-expanded', expanded ? 'false' : 'true');
        var picker = trigger.closest('.fr-ebay-market-picker');
        if (picker) picker.classList.toggle('fr-ebay-market-picker--open', !expanded);
      });
      Array.prototype.slice.call(menu.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-option]')).forEach(function (button) {
        button.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
          event.stopPropagation();
          applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-option'), true);
        });
      });
      document.addEventListener('click', function () { closeMenu(section); });
    }
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      button.addEventListener('click', function () {
        applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button'), true);
      });
    });
    applyMarket(section, marketId, false);
  });
