## Introduction

Devil’s Bridge, or Pontarfynach, is [Cardiganshire]({{ 'where-cardiganshire-turns-landscape-into/' | relative_url }})’s best-known trickster landscape story: a deep gorge, a terrifying crossing, a bargain with the Devil, and a local woman clever enough to win. The usual tale says that an old woman needed to retrieve her cow from the far side of the Afon Mynach. The Devil offered to build a bridge in return for the first living thing to cross it. When the bridge was ready, she threw bread across the span, her dog ran after it, and the Devil was left cheated of a human soul.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://sites.pitt.edu/~dash/type1191.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sites.pitt.edu">[Pitt Sites]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sites.pitt.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Sites Devil&#x27;s Bridge Legends</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Pitt SitesDevil&#x27;s Bridge Legends...</span></span></span>



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/Cardiganshire_0418d7_devils_bridge_0f8623-overview.webp" | relative_url }}" alt="Overview image for Devil s Bridge" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

The legend matters because it does more than explain a name. It turns one of Ceredigion’s most dramatic landmarks into a story about wit, danger and local independence. The physical place is real: three bridges, one above another, cross the Mynach gorge near Aberystwyth, and the structure is protected as a Grade II* listed building because of its remarkable stacked form and picturesque fame.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://britishlistedbuildings.co.uk/300009870-devils-bridge-pont-ar-fynach-pontarfynach" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: britishlistedbuildings.co.uk">[British Listed Buildings]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">britishlistedbuildings.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on britishlistedbuildings.co.uk.</span></span></span>

## The old woman, the dog and the bargain

The heart of the Devil’s Bridge legend is simple enough to be retold to children, walkers and visitors in a few sentences. An old woman sees her cow stranded across the ravine. The crossing is impossible, or at least frightening enough to feel beyond ordinary human effort. The Devil appears and offers a solution: he will build the bridge, but the first living creature to cross it will belong to him. The woman agrees, then escapes the trap by sending her dog across first with a piece of bread.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://sites.pitt.edu/~dash/type1191.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sites.pitt.edu">[Pitt Sites]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sites.pitt.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Sites Devil&#x27;s Bridge Legends</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Pitt SitesDevil&#x27;s Bridge Legends...</span></span></span>

That little trick is what makes the story memorable. The woman does not defeat the Devil by force, saintly holiness or noble sacrifice. She wins by noticing the wording of the bargain. The Devil asks for the first living thing to cross; he does not specify a human being. In one older printed summary, she throws a crust across the bridge and her little black dog runs after it, leaving her to say, in effect, that the dog is his prize.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://sites.pitt.edu/~dash/type1191.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sites.pitt.edu">[Pitt Sites]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sites.pitt.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Sites Devil&#x27;s Bridge Legends</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Pitt SitesDevil&#x27;s Bridge Legends...</span></span></span>

Nineteenth-century and later versions give the woman more character. Sabine Baring-Gould’s account names her as old Megan of Llandunach and adds that the Devil appears disguised as a monk, with signs of his true nature betrayed by a hoof and a strangely jointed leg. In that version, the story becomes a sharper piece of comic detection: Megan not only exploits the wording of the bargain, she sees through the disguise before the Devil realises he has been read.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://sites.pitt.edu/~dash/type1191.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sites.pitt.edu">[Pitt Sites]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sites.pitt.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Sites Devil&#x27;s Bridge Legends</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Pitt SitesDevil&#x27;s Bridge Legends...</span></span></span>

The cow is important too. It is not a decorative detail. In a rural Cardiganshire setting, a cow could represent livelihood, food, rent-paying capacity and household survival. The bargain is comic, but the problem is practical: how does a poor or isolated person cross a dangerous landscape to recover something essential? That practical pressure makes the Devil’s offer tempting, and it makes the woman’s victory feel more satisfying than a simple joke.

## Three bridges and a layered landmark

Devil’s Bridge is not only a legend site. It is a visible stack of history. The listed-building record describes three bridges, one above another, over the narrow ravine of the Afon Mynach. The lowest bridge is of uncertain date, possibly medieval or possibly sixteenth century, with later repair or rebuilding. A second stone arch was thrown above it in 1753 when the road level was raised. A further bridge was added in 1901, later replaced in the twentieth century while retaining the 1901 railings.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://britishlistedbuildings.co.uk/300009870-devils-bridge-pont-ar-fynach-pontarfynach" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: britishlistedbuildings.co.uk">[British Listed Buildings]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">britishlistedbuildings.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on britishlistedbuildings.co.uk.</span></span></span>

That stacked structure helps explain why the story has lasted. Many legends fade when the landscape no longer looks strange. Devil’s Bridge still looks improbable. Even before a visitor hears about the bargain, the place raises the obvious question: why are there three bridges on top of one another? The legend gives a vivid answer to a real visual puzzle, even though the historical explanation is one of changing road levels, repairs, engineering choices and the need to cross a steep gorge.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://britishlistedbuildings.co.uk/300009870-devils-bridge-pont-ar-fynach-pontarfynach" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: britishlistedbuildings.co.uk">[British Listed Buildings]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">britishlistedbuildings.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on britishlistedbuildings.co.uk.</span></span></span>

The local authority’s visitor account preserves the usual distinction between tradition and likely history. It retells the Devil’s bargain, but also notes that the first bridge was probably built in the twelfth century by monks of Strata Florida on a route towards Ysbyty Cynfyn. That is exactly the kind of double vision folklore often creates: the official explanation points to monks, routeways and medieval travel, while the legend says the bridge was too difficult for mortals and must have needed diabolical help.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.discoverceredigion.wales/areas-of-ceredigion/cambrian-mountains/communities-of-the-cambrian-mountains/devils-bridge/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: discoverceredigion.wales">[discoverceredigion.wales]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">discoverceredigion.wales</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Devil&#x27;s Bridge</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Discover Ceredigion...</span></span></span>

The Welsh name Pontarfynach also matters. It is not a direct translation of “Devil’s Bridge”; it points towards the Mynach, usually glossed here as “the monk’s river” or a name connected with monastic land and routes. The English name foregrounds the supernatural builder, while the Welsh place-name keeps the bridge tied to river, crossing and religious landscape. Together, they show how one place can hold two kinds of memory: practical monastic movement and later legendary drama.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.discoverceredigion.wales/areas-of-ceredigion/cambrian-mountains/communities-of-the-cambrian-mountains/devils-bridge/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: discoverceredigion.wales">[discoverceredigion.wales]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">discoverceredigion.wales</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Devil&#x27;s Bridge</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Discover Ceredigion...</span></span></span>

## Why tricking the Devil suits this place

Devil legends across Britain and Europe often attach themselves to bridges, roads, churches or buildings that seem too hard, too sudden or too daring to have been made by ordinary means. Devil’s Bridge in Cardiganshire fits that pattern perfectly: a narrow ravine, a rushing river, a difficult route and an engineering solution that looks almost theatrical when seen from below. The local story belongs to the wider “Devil’s Bridge” family of folktales, in which the Devil builds a crossing for a price and is cheated by a clever human who sends an animal over first.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Devil%27s_Bridge" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Devil&#x27;s Bridge</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Devil&#x27;s Bridge</span></span></span>

The Cardiganshire version is especially neat because the landscape itself already feels like a test. The Mynach falls drop through a wooded gorge before the river joins the Rheidol, and modern visitor descriptions still emphasise the steep paths, the waterfall circuit and the spectacle of the three bridges above the ravine.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.visitwales.com/attraction/nature-or-wildlife/devils-bridge-falls-537211" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: visitwales.com">[Visit Wales]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">visitwales.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on visitwales.com.</span></span></span> The story turns that physical difficulty into a moral contest: the Devil can master stone and distance, but he cannot master local wit.

The trick also suits a county folklore landscape in which ordinary people often have to negotiate with forces larger than themselves. In other Cardiganshire traditions, fairies, saints, ghosts, relics and uncanny places press into daily life. At Devil’s Bridge, the supernatural challenge is unusually comic. The woman is not swallowed by the landscape or punished for trespass. She reads the contract better than the Devil does.

There is also a sly anti-authoritarian pleasure in the tale. The Devil is the powerful outsider, the grand builder, the one who thinks he controls the terms. The old woman is local, poor in some tellings, and apparently vulnerable. Yet the story belongs to her. The gorge may be frightening and the Devil may be ancient, but Cardiganshire’s memorable human figure is the canny woman with bread in her pocket and a dog at her side.

## How old is the tradition?

The legend is old enough to be firmly embedded in nineteenth-century printed folklore and travel writing, but its exact oral age is difficult to prove. Wirt Sikes included the Cardiganshire Devil’s Bridge story in *British Goblins* in 1880, presenting it as one of the best-known Welsh natural objects linked with the Devil in popular lore. He also stressed that similar bridge-building legends existed in other countries, which suggests that the Cardiganshire story was both local and part of a much wider European tale pattern.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://sites.pitt.edu/~dash/type1191.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sites.pitt.edu">[Pitt Sites]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sites.pitt.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Sites Devil&#x27;s Bridge Legends</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Pitt SitesDevil&#x27;s Bridge Legends...</span></span></span>

There is also an important late eighteenth-century travel witness. Catherine Hutton, visiting in 1787, recorded the story of the woman, the cow, the bargain and the dog. Her account is valuable because it shows that the tale was already attached to the place before Victorian folklore collecting made such legends fashionable in print. She also describes standing on the lower bridge beneath the upper arch, looking down into the chasm, which shows how strongly the tale and the physical experience of the site were already linked for visitors.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://sublimewales.wordpress.com/places/devils-bridge-near-aberystwyth/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sublimewales.wordpress.com">[Early Tourists in Wales]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sublimewales.wordpress.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wordpress.com.</span></span></span>

The tradition is therefore not merely a modern tourist invention, though modern tourism has certainly kept it alive. It appears in early visitor accounts, nineteenth-century folklore collections, local tourism material and modern retellings. What changes is the detail: the woman may be unnamed or called Megan; the dog may be simply a dog or a little black cur; the Devil may appear openly or disguised as a monk. The core mechanism remains stable: impossible crossing, dangerous bargain, animal sent first, Devil defeated.

The age of the physical bridge and the age of the story should not be confused. The lower structure may be medieval or later, and official accounts often connect the earliest bridge with Strata Florida’s monastic route network. The folktale motif itself is older and more widespread than any single surviving bridge. That does not weaken the Cardiganshire legend. It explains how a travelling story found an unusually convincing home in this particular gorge.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://britishlistedbuildings.co.uk/300009870-devils-bridge-pont-ar-fynach-pontarfynach" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: britishlistedbuildings.co.uk">[britishlistedbuildings.co.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">britishlistedbuildings.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on britishlistedbuildings.co.uk.</span></span></span>

## A crossing on routes, estates and tourist maps

Devil’s Bridge sits in a landscape where movement mattered. Ceredigion Historical Society’s historic landscape account places the area within the former county of Cardiganshire and notes its relationship to Strata Florida Abbey’s Cwmystwyth grange in the medieval period. It also identifies the crossing point of the Mynach as part of an important route, with Devil’s Bridge becoming a junction of turnpike roads in 1770: one route from Aberystwyth towards Cwmystwyth and London, and another northwards towards Shrewsbury.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ceredigionhistory.wales/devils-bridge-history/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ceredigionhistory.wales">[CEREDIGION HISTORICAL SOCIETY]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ceredigionhistory.wales</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on ceredigionhistory.wales.</span></span></span>

That route history gives the legend extra depth. A bridge is never just a picturesque object; it controls who can pass, trade, visit, herd animals, reach markets or travel between upland and coast. The Devil’s bargain dramatises the cost of crossing. In folklore terms, the bridge asks: what must be paid to pass through a dangerous place?

The nearby Hafod landscape also shaped the site’s reputation. In the late eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, visitors increasingly sought wild scenery, waterfalls and “picturesque” views. Devil’s Bridge and the Mynach falls became part of that itinerary, helped by roads, inns and the cultivated landscape of the Hafod estate. Ceredigion Historical Society notes that a visit to the waterfalls and bridge became a vital part of a tourist’s route in the late eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, while the number of drawings, paintings and engravings shows how popular the falls became.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ceredigionhistory.wales/devils-bridge-history/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ceredigionhistory.wales">[CEREDIGION HISTORICAL SOCIETY]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ceredigionhistory.wales</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on ceredigionhistory.wales.</span></span></span>

This is where folklore and tourism meet without cancelling each other out. The legend gives the visitor a story to carry away; the gorge gives the story a setting that feels earned. The more people came to see the falls, the more the tale was repeated. The more the tale was repeated, the more the bridge became not only an engineering curiosity but a Cardiganshire folklore landmark.

## The picturesque Devil

By the late eighteenth century, Devil’s Bridge was already being seen through the language of sublime and picturesque travel. Visitors described chasms, torrents, cataracts, rocks and woods. Catherine Hutton’s 1787 account moves smoothly from the comic legend to the physical thrill of standing between the bridges and looking down towards the river.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://sublimewales.wordpress.com/places/devils-bridge-near-aberystwyth/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sublimewales.wordpress.com">[Early Tourists in Wales]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sublimewales.wordpress.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wordpress.com.</span></span></span>

Artists and writers reinforced that reputation. J. M. W. Turner sketched and painted Welsh falls and bridge scenes connected with Devil’s Bridge and the River Mynach, and Tate’s Turner research records a watercolour of the “Second Fall of the River Monach, Devil’s Bridge, Cardiganshire” exhibited at the Royal Academy in 1794.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.tate.org.uk/art/research-publications/jmw-turner/joseph-mallord-william-turner-a-waterfall-r1141130" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: tate.org.uk">[Tate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">tate.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on tate.org.uk.</span></span></span> William Wordsworth later published “To the Torrent at the Devil’s Bridge, North Wales, 1824”, treating the place as a landscape of force, sound and moral grandeur rather than as a comic folktale setting.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.simple-poetry.com/poems/to-the-torrent-at-the-devil-s-bridge-north-wales-1824-97603794059" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: simple-poetry.com">[Simple Poetry]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">simple-poetry.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">to the torrent at the devil s bridge north wales 1824 97603794059</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">to the torrent at the devil s bridge north wales 1824 97603794059</span></span></span>

That contrast is revealing. The folklore makes the Devil ridiculous; the picturesque tradition makes the landscape overwhelming. Together they create the site’s distinctive mood. The same gorge can be a place where a woman tricks Satan, a place where tourists admire engineered danger, and a place where poets and artists respond to water, height and shadow.

For Cardiganshire identity, this layering is part of the appeal. Devil’s Bridge is not a remote tale pinned to a vague “Welsh” landscape. It is a named place near Aberystwyth, attached to the old county, visible in visitor routes, recorded in travel writing and still marketed through its bridges, falls and legend. It shows how local folklore can survive because the landscape continues to perform the story every time someone looks down into the gorge.

## What the legend is not

It is tempting to treat Devil’s Bridge as proof that people once literally believed the Devil built the span. The evidence is more interesting than that. Older sources present the story as popular lore, travel anecdote and comparative legend, not as engineering history. The listed-building record explains the bridge sequence through repair, road raising and later construction, while local visitor material openly balances the legend with the likely role of Strata Florida monks in the earliest crossing.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://britishlistedbuildings.co.uk/300009870-devils-bridge-pont-ar-fynach-pontarfynach" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: britishlistedbuildings.co.uk">[British Listed Buildings]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">britishlistedbuildings.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on britishlistedbuildings.co.uk.</span></span></span>

It is also not unique in the sense of having no parallels. The Cardiganshire story belongs to a wider family of Devil’s Bridge legends found in many European settings. In some versions the Devil is cheated with a dog, in others with a rooster, goat, pig or other animal. What makes the Cardiganshire version distinctive is not the bare plot but its perfect fit with a real triple bridge, a Welsh monastic route-name, a dramatic wooded gorge and a long history of visitors recording the place.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://sites.pitt.edu/~dash/type1191.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sites.pitt.edu">[Pitt Sites]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sites.pitt.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Sites Devil&#x27;s Bridge Legends</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Pitt SitesDevil&#x27;s Bridge Legends...</span></span></span>

Nor should the story be flattened into a moral fable about cruelty to the dog. Traditional tales often use animals as legal loopholes, symbolic substitutes or comic reversals. In this case the dog’s role is to expose the Devil’s mistake. The woman does not overpower evil; she makes evil obey its own wording.

The legend’s strongest value lies in that combination of humour and place. It says that dangerous landscapes invite stories, that strange engineering invites supernatural explanation, and that a small local act of cleverness can be more memorable than grand demonic power.

## How it is retold today

Today, Devil’s Bridge remains one of Ceredigion’s most visitor-friendly legends. Discover Ceredigion presents it as “a place built on a legend” and retells the bargain with the old woman, bread and dog alongside the history of the three bridges. Devil’s Bridge Falls also retells the story for visitors, keeping the old comic structure alive as part of the modern attraction.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.discoverceredigion.wales/areas-of-ceredigion/cambrian-mountains/communities-of-the-cambrian-mountains/devils-bridge/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: discoverceredigion.wales">[discoverceredigion.wales]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">discoverceredigion.wales</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Devil&#x27;s Bridge</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Discover Ceredigion...</span></span></span>

The site’s modern appeal is not only folkloric. Visitors come for the waterfalls, the steep wooded walks, the view of the stacked bridges and the Vale of Rheidol Railway from Aberystwyth. Visit Wales describes Devil’s Bridge Falls as a major Mid Wales nature attraction, with the Mynach waterfalls dropping through a deep wooded gorge and the three bridges forming part of the experience.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.visitwales.com/attraction/nature-or-wildlife/devils-bridge-falls-537211" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: visitwales.com">[Visit Wales]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">visitwales.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on visitwales.com.</span></span></span>

Modern media has added another layer. The area and hotel became familiar to many viewers through the Welsh crime drama *Y Gwyll*, known in English as *Hinterland*, which used Devil’s Bridge’s atmosphere of isolation, weather and brooding landscape. That is not the same as the old Devil legend, but it shows how the place still works imaginatively: remote, dramatic, visually memorable and slightly unsettling.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.visitmidwales.co.uk/showmewales/Pontarfynach-%7C-Devil%27s-Bridge-p1725411" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: visitmidwales.co.uk">[Visit Mid Wales]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">visitmidwales.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on visitmidwales.co.uk.</span></span></span>

For a folklore map of Cardiganshire, Devil’s Bridge is a key stop because it gathers so many county themes into one place: a historic route, a monastic hint, a dangerous river crossing, a named local trickster, a tourist landmark and a story that has travelled far beyond Wales while remaining unmistakably attached to this gorge. The Devil builds the bridge, but the county keeps the punchline.

    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-selected-market-flag], [data-ebay-trigger-market-flag]')).forEach(function (flag) {
      flag.className = flagClass(marketId);
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-trigger-market-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = selectedText;
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-domain-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = domainLabel(marketId);
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-option]')).forEach(function (button) {
      button.setAttribute('aria-selected', button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-option') === marketId ? 'true' : 'false');
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      var active = button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button') === marketId;
      button.setAttribute('aria-pressed', active ? 'true' : 'false');
    });
    section.setAttribute('data-ebay-selected-market', marketId);
    if (persist) {
      try { if (window.localStorage) localStorage.setItem('phoenix-ebay-market', marketId); } catch (err) {}
    }
    closeMenu(section);
  }
  sections.forEach(function (section) {
    if (section.getAttribute('data-ebay-localized-init') === '1') return;
    section.setAttribute('data-ebay-localized-init', '1');
    var available = availableMarkets(section);
    var marketId = defaultMarket;
    if (window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation && window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation.inferMarket) {
      marketId = window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation.inferMarket(available, {
        storageKey: 'phoenix-ebay-market',
        defaultMarket: defaultMarket
      });
    } else if (available.indexOf(defaultMarket)< 0) {
      marketId = available[0] || defaultMarket;
    }
    var select = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-select]');
    if (select) {
      select.addEventListener('change', function () {
        applyMarket(section, select.value, true);
      });
    }
    var trigger = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-trigger]');
    var menu = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-menu]');
    if (trigger && menu) {
      trigger.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
        event.stopPropagation();
        var expanded = trigger.getAttribute('aria-expanded') === 'true';
        menu.hidden = expanded;
        trigger.setAttribute('aria-expanded', expanded ? 'false' : 'true');
        var picker = trigger.closest('.fr-ebay-market-picker');
        if (picker) picker.classList.toggle('fr-ebay-market-picker--open', !expanded);
      });
      Array.prototype.slice.call(menu.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-option]')).forEach(function (button) {
        button.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
          event.stopPropagation();
          applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-option'), true);
        });
      });
      document.addEventListener('click', function () { closeMenu(section); });
    }
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      button.addEventListener('click', function () {
        applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button'), true);
      });
    });
    applyMarket(section, marketId, false);
  });
