## Introduction

Seventeenth-century [Flintshire]({{ 'where-flintshires-strange-stories-still/' | relative_url }})’s witch records show a county where fear rarely looked like a staged witch-hunt. It looked more like a sick child, a lame animal, a quarrel between neighbours, a woman asked to bless someone she was also feared to harm, and a desperate journey to a holy well when ordinary cures had failed. The strongest surviving evidence comes from the Court of Great Sessions, especially cases from Llanasa, Penley and Dyserth, preserved and discussed by the National Library of Wales from J. Gwynn Wiliams’s Flintshire Historical Society studies. Those records matter because they catch belief at street level: not polished legend, but sworn testimony, petitions, rumours, grudges and attempts to make sense of illness.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.library.wales/discover-learn/digital-exhibitions/archives/witchcraft-court-of-great-sessions-rec/witchcraft-in-17th-century-flintshire" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: library.wales">[National Library of Wales]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">library.wales</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Library of Wales Witchcraft in 17th-century Flintshire</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Library of WalesWitchcraft in 17th-century Flintshire - National Library of Wales...</span></span></span>



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/Flintshire_823310_flintshire_witch_rec_51a1fc-overview.webp" | relative_url }}" alt="Overview image for Witch Records" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

The picture is distinctive because Flintshire had both witchcraft accusation and powerful holy-well culture. St Winefride’s Well at Holywell was not a decorative backdrop; it was a living healing shrine, still drawing pilgrims in the Stuart period, even while Protestant authorities worried about superstition, Catholic devotion and unofficial cures. In this county, fear of witchcraft and hope in sacred water belonged to the same anxious world.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cadw.gov.wales/visit/places-to-visit/st-winefrides-chapel-and-well" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cadw.gov.wales">[Cadw]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cadw.gov.wales</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Cadw St Winefride&#x27;s Chapel and Well &#124; Cadw</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cadw St Winefride&#x27;s Chapel and Well &#124; Cadw</span></span></span>

## Why Flintshire’s witch records are unusually revealing

The key source is not a fireside tale collected centuries later, but legal paperwork. Wales had its own Court of Great Sessions, and Flintshire belonged to the north-east circuit with Denbighshire and Montgomeryshire. Wiliams notes that the north-east circuit’s records are the most satisfactory of the Welsh circuits for this subject, and that 186 out of a possible 200 seventeenth-century Flintshire rolls survived and were examined. That is why Flintshire can be read in unusually close detail compared with many counties where accusations have disappeared into rumour.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.library.wales/discover-learn/digital-exhibitions/archives/witchcraft-court-of-great-sessions-rec/witchcraft-in-17th-century-flintshire" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: library.wales">[National Library of Wales]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">library.wales</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Library of Wales Witchcraft in 17th-century Flintshire</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Library of WalesWitchcraft in 17th-century Flintshire - National Library of Wales...</span></span></span>

Even so, the most important finding is restraint. Wiliams found only two fully documented Flintshire witchcraft cases in the seventeenth-century gaol files, plus one case and one petition in which witchcraft was mentioned. His conclusion is blunt: despite gaps, there was “nothing approaching a witch-hunt” in seventeenth-century Flintshire, and prosecutions rarely reached the Court of Great Sessions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.library.wales/discover-learn/digital-exhibitions/archives/witchcraft-court-of-great-sessions-rec/witchcraft-in-17th-century-flintshire" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: library.wales">[National Library of Wales]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">library.wales</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Library of Wales Witchcraft in 17th-century Flintshire</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Library of WalesWitchcraft in 17th-century Flintshire - National Library of Wales...</span></span></span>

That does not mean people were relaxed about magic. It means most fear did not become a successful prosecution. The National Library of Wales’s overview of the Flintshire material distinguishes “white” magic, used for cures and blessings, from harmful magic such as cursing a neighbour or harming cattle; it also explains that later medieval and early modern demonology blurred these boundaries by treating magical power as potentially linked with the Devil. Flintshire’s cases sit exactly in that dangerous overlap, where a person known for healing could also be suspected of hurting.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.library.wales/discover-learn/digital-exhibitions/archives/witchcraft-court-of-great-sessions-rec" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: library.wales">[National Library of Wales]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">library.wales</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Library of Wales Witchcraft (Court of Great Sessions Rec</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Library of WalesWitchcraft (Court of Great Sessions Rec) - National Library of Wales...</span></span></span>

For folklore readers, this is the point. The records do not simply tell us whether anyone was legally guilty. They show how a community explained misfortune. A child worsened after a quarrel. Cattle failed after a tenancy dispute. A mariner saw frightening lights near the coast. A cure worked, then fear returned. In each case, accusation grew from ordinary relationships under pressure.

## Court of Great Sessions cases

### Dorothy Griffith and the frightening lights at Llanasa

The case of Dorothy Griffith of Llanasa is one of Flintshire’s most memorable witchcraft accusations because it has the shape of a local legend but survives through legal testimony. In 1656, William Griffith, a mariner from Picton, accused Dorothy Griffith of witchcraft. The National Library of Wales summarises the case as including testimony from William, his brother Edward, the alehouse-keeper Thomas Rodgers and Rodgers’s wife Margaret Bellis, along with a petition supporting Dorothy signed by thirty-one neighbours, including prominent local people.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.library.wales/discover-learn/digital-exhibitions/archives/witchcraft-court-of-great-sessions-rec" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: library.wales">[National Library of Wales]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">library.wales</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Library of Wales Witchcraft (Court of Great Sessions Rec</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Library of WalesWitchcraft (Court of Great Sessions Rec) - National Library of Wales...</span></span></span>

Wiliams’s fuller discussion places the case in a tight parish world. Llanasa was a self-contained coastal parish, with 231 households and 1,004 people recorded in a 1681 return; marriage records suggest that most people married within the parish, though the presence of seafarers meant the outside world was not absent. Against that background, an accusation was not an abstract claim. It landed inside a small network where families remembered quarrels.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.library.wales/discover-learn/digital-exhibitions/archives/witchcraft-court-of-great-sessions-rec/witchcraft-in-17th-century-flintshire" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: library.wales">[National Library of Wales]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">library.wales</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Library of Wales Witchcraft in 17th-century Flintshire</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Library of WalesWitchcraft in 17th-century Flintshire - National Library of Wales...</span></span></span>

The surviving account suggests that Dorothy’s prosecution grew from a history of ill-feeling between families, sharpened by the events of 11 February 1656. Wiliams’s judgement is careful: the precise reason for the accusation cannot now be recovered, but the evidence points to a serious personal breach, and perhaps to William interpreting alarming experiences through fear of Dorothy’s supposed supernatural power.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.library.wales/discover-learn/digital-exhibitions/archives/witchcraft-court-of-great-sessions-rec/witchcraft-in-17th-century-flintshire" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: library.wales">[National Library of Wales]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">library.wales</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Library of Wales Witchcraft in 17th-century Flintshire</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Library of WalesWitchcraft in 17th-century Flintshire - National Library of Wales...</span></span></span>

What makes the case so useful is the petition. Thirty-one neighbours signed in Dorothy’s favour. That does not prove that nobody believed in witchcraft; it proves that local reputation mattered. A person could be accused, but she could also be defended by a counter-story: she was known, she belonged, and respectable neighbours would vouch for her. In a county with few prosecutions, this social defence may have been as important as legal scepticism.

### Charles Hughes and the cattle dispute

The 1690 case of Charles Hughes, also connected with Llanasa, shows how easily witchcraft suspicion could attach itself to property conflict. Charles was an undertenant of John Evans, and the quarrel centred on tenancy obligations and buildings. According to Hugh ap Edward, Charles’s father, John Evans had failed to complete the promised building work, after which Charles took the tenement directly from Sir Edward Mostyn’s steward at the same yearly rent. Evans was displeased, and the later accusation concerned the maiming of cattle.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.library.wales/discover-learn/digital-exhibitions/archives/witchcraft-court-of-great-sessions-rec/witchcraft-in-17th-century-flintshire-part-2" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: library.wales">[National Library of Wales]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">library.wales</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Library of Wales Witchcraft in 17th-century Flintshire: Part 2</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Library of Wales Witchcraft in 17th-century Flintshire: Part 2</span></span></span>

The most folkloric detail is the use of a fortune teller. Gwen, wife of John Evans, said that her husband had gone to a fortune teller who identified the culprit by a clue about a neighbour’s house: the “first tree” of the house lying upon the chimney. Gwen then connected that sign with Charles Hughes’s house and suspected him of maiming the cattle.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.library.wales/discover-learn/digital-exhibitions/archives/witchcraft-court-of-great-sessions-rec/witchcraft-in-17th-century-flintshire-part-2" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: library.wales">[National Library of Wales]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">library.wales</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Library of Wales Witchcraft in 17th-century Flintshire: Part 2</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Library of Wales Witchcraft in 17th-century Flintshire: Part 2</span></span></span>

This is not a grand tale of sabbaths and demons. It is a farm dispute, a building dispute, a household clue and a fortune teller’s diagnostic riddle. It shows how local magic could function as a form of investigation when people felt wronged but lacked proof.

Again, the accusation did not succeed. Sixteen members of the local gentry signed in Charles Hughes’s favour, certifying that he was a hard-working man of honest behaviour and good reputation, and no further action appears to have been taken. Wiliams stresses the religious complexity too: this was not simply Protestant suspicion of Catholics, because the quarrel itself involved Catholics, while Protestant gentry defended Charles.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.library.wales/discover-learn/digital-exhibitions/archives/witchcraft-court-of-great-sessions-rec/witchcraft-in-17th-century-flintshire-part-2" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: library.wales">[National Library of Wales]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">library.wales</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Library of Wales Witchcraft in 17th-century Flintshire: Part 2</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Library of Wales Witchcraft in 17th-century Flintshire: Part 2</span></span></span>

### Henry John James of Dyserth and the bewitched oxen

The Dyserth material brings the accusation closer to the border between witch, conjurer and local specialist. In 1638, witnesses in the Court of Arches said that Henry John James had acted as a sorcerer and conjurer and had engaged in witchcraft. The central charge was that he had bewitched two oxen belonging to John Winn Edwards during Easter 1637.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.library.wales/discover-learn/digital-exhibitions/archives/witchcraft-court-of-great-sessions-rec/witchcraft-in-17th-century-flintshire-part-2" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: library.wales">[National Library of Wales]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">library.wales</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Library of Wales Witchcraft in 17th-century Flintshire: Part 2</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Library of Wales Witchcraft in 17th-century Flintshire: Part 2</span></span></span>

The details matter because livestock illness was one of the most common settings for witchcraft suspicion. A cow, pig, horse or ox represented food, work, rent and survival. When an animal sickened unexpectedly, the question “who caused this?” could become more urgent than “what disease is this?” Flintshire’s records show that animals were not secondary to witch belief; they were often at the centre of it.

Dyserth also mattered as a landscape of water, parish authority and local status. The dispute appears in a world where church seating, burial rights and gentry influence were contested, not in a timeless fairy-tale village. That gives the case its bite: witchcraft language could enter local conflicts where honour, authority and property already mattered.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.library.wales/discover-learn/digital-exhibitions/archives/witchcraft-court-of-great-sessions-rec/witchcraft-in-17th-century-flintshire-part-2" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: library.wales">[National Library of Wales]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">library.wales</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Library of Wales Witchcraft in 17th-century Flintshire: Part 2</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Library of Wales Witchcraft in 17th-century Flintshire: Part 2</span></span></span>

### Anne Ellis of Penley and the danger of being useful

Anne Ellis of Penley is the clearest Flintshire example of the frightening ambiguity around healing. In 1657, she was accused of acts of magic, both good and bad, involving livestock and children. The National Library of Wales lists depositions from six neighbours, her own testimony and that of a constable; she was later discharged.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.library.wales/discover-learn/digital-exhibitions/archives/witchcraft-court-of-great-sessions-rec" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: library.wales">[National Library of Wales]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">library.wales</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Library of Wales Witchcraft (Court of Great Sessions Rec</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Library of WalesWitchcraft (Court of Great Sessions Rec) - National Library of Wales...</span></span></span>

Wiliams’s account makes her socially vulnerable. Anne was a beggar on the margins of Penley society, and suspicion had accumulated around her. One witness said she had “a bad report to be a witch of all her neighbours”. Another said people gave more to Anne at their doors than to any other beggar because they feared she might injure them or their cattle.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.library.wales/discover-learn/digital-exhibitions/archives/witchcraft-court-of-great-sessions-rec/witchcraft-in-17th-century-flintshire-part-2" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: library.wales">[National Library of Wales]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">library.wales</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Library of Wales Witchcraft in 17th-century Flintshire: Part 2</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Library of Wales Witchcraft in 17th-century Flintshire: Part 2</span></span></span>

The disturbing part is that Anne was not simply feared as harmful. She was also approached for help. She was asked to bless sick people; after one blessing, a child recovered. Anne herself said she hoped she had done good, because God’s blessing must do good through those with power and grace to speak it. Yet the same perceived gift made her dangerous in neighbours’ eyes. If she could cure, perhaps she could also cause.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.library.wales/discover-learn/digital-exhibitions/archives/witchcraft-court-of-great-sessions-rec/witchcraft-in-17th-century-flintshire-part-2" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: library.wales">[National Library of Wales]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">library.wales</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Library of Wales Witchcraft in 17th-century Flintshire: Part 2</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Library of Wales Witchcraft in 17th-century Flintshire: Part 2</span></span></span>

Her case reveals one of the central risks of seventeenth-century popular belief. A poor woman who was thought powerless in ordinary social terms could be imagined as powerful in supernatural terms. That imagined power might bring food, requests and wary respect, but it could also bring prosecution.

## Healing wells and uneasy cures

Flintshire’s witch records cannot be separated from holy-well culture. Wiliams’s study notes fifty-one identified wells in Flintshire, twenty-seven bearing saints’ names. Two wells called Ffynnon Fihangel, in Bodfari and Caerwys parishes, were said to cure sore eyes and warts; Ffynnon Asa in Cwm was visited for rheumatism and nervous disorders; and Ffynnon Ddier in Bodfari preserved a striking mixture of Christian and older ritual practice.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.library.wales/discover-learn/digital-exhibitions/archives/witchcraft-court-of-great-sessions-rec/witchcraft-in-17th-century-flintshire" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: library.wales">[National Library of Wales]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">library.wales</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Library of Wales Witchcraft in 17th-century Flintshire</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Library of WalesWitchcraft in 17th-century Flintshire - National Library of Wales...</span></span></span>

Ffynnon Ddier is especially revealing. The poorest people in the parish were said to offer chickens, a cockerel for a boy and a pullet for a girl, after going round the well nine times. Children were dipped in three corners of the well to stop them crying at night. One source also described an Ascension Day procession to the well, where parts of the liturgy were read.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.library.wales/discover-learn/digital-exhibitions/archives/witchcraft-court-of-great-sessions-rec/witchcraft-in-17th-century-flintshire" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: library.wales">[National Library of Wales]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">library.wales</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Library of Wales Witchcraft in 17th-century Flintshire</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Library of WalesWitchcraft in 17th-century Flintshire - National Library of Wales...</span></span></span>

To modern readers, this may sound like a charming survival. To a seventeenth-century church authority, it could look more troubling: a saint’s well, ritual circling, animal offering, child healing and parish procession all bound together. The anxiety was not simply that people believed too much. It was that official religion, household medicine, older custom and magical thinking were hard to separate.

St Winefride’s Well at Holywell stood above all these local wells. Cadw describes it as a place of pilgrimage since at least 1115, with a late fifteenth-century chapel built into the hillside and spring water rising into a star-shaped basin before flowing to an outdoor pool where pilgrims still bathe in waters associated with healing claims.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cadw.gov.wales/visit/places-to-visit/st-winefrides-chapel-and-well" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cadw.gov.wales">[Cadw]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cadw.gov.wales</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Cadw St Winefride&#x27;s Chapel and Well &#124; Cadw</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cadw St Winefride&#x27;s Chapel and Well &#124; Cadw</span></span></span>

In the seventeenth century, that living pilgrimage culture created both hope and official unease. Wiliams notes that many people, Protestant as well as Catholic, still remembered St Winefride’s blessings and travelled to Holywell for healing. He also records government concern, including a 1637 order to remove the iron bars pilgrims clung to while descending into the cold water. Yet the well continued to flourish, and people sought help there when other remedies had failed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.library.wales/discover-learn/digital-exhibitions/archives/witchcraft-court-of-great-sessions-rec/witchcraft-in-17th-century-flintshire-part-2" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: library.wales">[National Library of Wales]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">library.wales</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Library of Wales Witchcraft in 17th-century Flintshire: Part 2</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Library of Wales Witchcraft in 17th-century Flintshire: Part 2</span></span></span>

This gives the Flintshire material its distinctive atmosphere. A wise woman might be feared as a witch, but the saint’s well could be treated as a higher, safer power. Wiliams makes exactly that comparison through cases of sick people who first consulted suspect healers and then turned to Holywell. In this belief-world, St Winefride had not been replaced by the countryside wise woman; for many, she outranked her.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.library.wales/discover-learn/digital-exhibitions/archives/witchcraft-court-of-great-sessions-rec/witchcraft-in-17th-century-flintshire-part-2" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: library.wales">[National Library of Wales]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">library.wales</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Library of Wales Witchcraft in 17th-century Flintshire: Part 2</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Library of Wales Witchcraft in 17th-century Flintshire: Part 2</span></span></span>

## Fear, accusation and local belief

The Flintshire cases show accusation as a process, not a sudden explosion. It usually began with misfortune: sickness, lameness, dead livestock, damaged cattle, frightening visions or a failed cure. It then moved through memory: a quarrel, a refusal, a curse, a muttered phrase, an old grudge, a person already “reported” to be dangerous. Only after that did it reach authority, and often it stopped before punishment.

This is why the county’s records are so valuable for folklore. They preserve the mechanics of fear. In Anne Ellis’s case, people both sought her blessing and feared her displeasure. In Charles Hughes’s case, a fortune teller’s clue turned a tenancy dispute into a supernatural suspicion. In Dorothy Griffith’s case, a frightening coastal experience became linked to a woman with whom there had already been ill-feeling.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.library.wales/discover-learn/digital-exhibitions/archives/witchcraft-court-of-great-sessions-rec/witchcraft-in-17th-century-flintshire-part-2" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: library.wales">[National Library of Wales]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">library.wales</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Library of Wales Witchcraft in 17th-century Flintshire: Part 2</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Library of Wales Witchcraft in 17th-century Flintshire: Part 2</span></span></span>

They also show the limits of fear. Flintshire did not become a place of mass prosecution. Petitions and gentry support mattered. The Court of Great Sessions records show cases being discharged or apparently abandoned. Anne Ellis was discharged after the grand jury rejected two charges and a petty jury acquitted her of others. Charles Hughes had influential defenders and no further action appears to have followed. Dorothy Griffith’s case likewise seems not to have produced a conviction.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.library.wales/discover-learn/digital-exhibitions/archives/witchcraft-court-of-great-sessions-rec/witchcraft-in-17th-century-flintshire-part-2" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: library.wales">[National Library of Wales]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">library.wales</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Library of Wales Witchcraft in 17th-century Flintshire: Part 2</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Library of Wales Witchcraft in 17th-century Flintshire: Part 2</span></span></span>

One explanation is that local elites became less willing to push witchcraft accusations through the courts. Wiliams argues that the views of gentry and higher clergy affected prosecution levels, and suggests that sceptical Justices of the Peace in Flintshire may have discouraged accusations before they ever reached the Court of Great Sessions. He also notes a shift in episcopal concern: Bishop George Griffith still asked in 1662 about people curing men or cattle by charms, spells, witchcraft or other unlawful means, but later bishops became more preoccupied with Roman Catholic influence, especially the cult of St Winefride.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.library.wales/discover-learn/digital-exhibitions/archives/witchcraft-court-of-great-sessions-rec/witchcraft-in-17th-century-flintshire-part-2" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: library.wales">[National Library of Wales]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">library.wales</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Library of Wales Witchcraft in 17th-century Flintshire: Part 2</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Library of Wales Witchcraft in 17th-century Flintshire: Part 2</span></span></span>

That last point is crucial. Flintshire’s official anxiety did not simply fade; it changed target. Witchcraft remained alive in popular imagination, but by the later seventeenth century, Holywell’s Catholic associations could look like a bigger religious problem to Protestant authorities than village-level bewitchment.

## Why this matters in Flintshire folklore

Flintshire’s seventeenth-century witch records are not just legal curiosities. They are among the county’s best windows into lived supernatural belief. Unlike a polished legend of a saint, fairy or ghost, these documents catch people under pressure: afraid for children, cattle, health, rent, reputation and salvation.

They also complicate the simple idea of “witch folklore”. In Flintshire, the witch was not always a stranger, a monster or a theatrical villain. She might be a marginal beggar whose blessing was requested. He might be a neighbour accused after a property dispute. The suspected power might be a curse, a cure, a fortune teller’s clue, or a reputation that had grown over years. The frightening question was not only “is this person evil?” but “what kind of power do they have, and who can control it?”

Holy wells add a second layer. Flintshire was a county where water could be holy, medical, suspicious, communal and contested all at once. St Winefride’s Well offered an authorised sacred alternative to the feared healer, yet it also alarmed authorities who saw pilgrimage and saintly intercession as dangerous survivals. Smaller wells, with their cures for eyes, warts, rheumatism, nervous disorders and crying children, show that this was not confined to Holywell alone.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cadw.gov.wales/visit/places-to-visit/st-winefrides-chapel-and-well" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cadw.gov.wales">[Cadw]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cadw.gov.wales</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Cadw St Winefride&#x27;s Chapel and Well &#124; Cadw</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cadw St Winefride&#x27;s Chapel and Well &#124; Cadw</span></span></span>

For today’s reader, the most honest retelling is not that seventeenth-century Flintshire was swept by witch panic. The better story is subtler and more human. In a landscape of saints’ wells, coastal lights, parish quarrels and fragile livelihoods, people used the language of witchcraft to explain harm and the language of holy water to seek relief. The court records matter because they reveal both impulses at once: fear of hidden malice, and hope that a stronger blessing might undo it.

    if (select) {
      return Array.prototype.slice.call(select.options)
        .map(function (option) { return option.value; })
        .filter(function (marketId) { return !!markets[marketId]; });
    }
    return Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]'))
      .map(function (button) { return button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button'); })
      .filter(function (marketId) { return !!markets[marketId]; });
  }
  function applyMarket(section, marketId, persist) {
    var available = availableMarkets(section);
    if (available.indexOf(marketId)< 0) marketId = available[0] || defaultMarket;
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-localized-link]')).forEach(function (link) {
      var query = link.getAttribute('data-ebay-query') || '';
      var reference = link.getAttribute('data-ebay-reference') || '';
      link.setAttribute('href', buildUrl(query, reference, marketId));
    });
    var select = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-select]');
    if (select && available.indexOf(marketId) >= 0) select.value = marketId;
    var selectedText = marketId;
    if (select && select.selectedIndex >= 0) selectedText = select.options[select.selectedIndex].text;
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-selected-market-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = selectedText;
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-selected-market-flag], [data-ebay-trigger-market-flag]')).forEach(function (flag) {
      flag.className = flagClass(marketId);
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-trigger-market-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = selectedText;
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-domain-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = domainLabel(marketId);
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-option]')).forEach(function (button) {
      button.setAttribute('aria-selected', button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-option') === marketId ? 'true' : 'false');
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      var active = button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button') === marketId;
      button.setAttribute('aria-pressed', active ? 'true' : 'false');
    });
    section.setAttribute('data-ebay-selected-market', marketId);
    if (persist) {
      try { if (window.localStorage) localStorage.setItem('phoenix-ebay-market', marketId); } catch (err) {}
    }
    closeMenu(section);
  }
  sections.forEach(function (section) {
    if (section.getAttribute('data-ebay-localized-init') === '1') return;
    section.setAttribute('data-ebay-localized-init', '1');
    var available = availableMarkets(section);
    var marketId = defaultMarket;
    if (window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation && window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation.inferMarket) {
      marketId = window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation.inferMarket(available, {
        storageKey: 'phoenix-ebay-market',
        defaultMarket: defaultMarket
      });
    } else if (available.indexOf(defaultMarket)< 0) {
      marketId = available[0] || defaultMarket;
    }
    var select = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-select]');
    if (select) {
      select.addEventListener('change', function () {
        applyMarket(section, select.value, true);
      });
    }
    var trigger = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-trigger]');
    var menu = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-menu]');
    if (trigger && menu) {
      trigger.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
        event.stopPropagation();
        var expanded = trigger.getAttribute('aria-expanded') === 'true';
        menu.hidden = expanded;
        trigger.setAttribute('aria-expanded', expanded ? 'false' : 'true');
        var picker = trigger.closest('.fr-ebay-market-picker');
        if (picker) picker.classList.toggle('fr-ebay-market-picker--open', !expanded);
      });
      Array.prototype.slice.call(menu.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-option]')).forEach(function (button) {
        button.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
          event.stopPropagation();
          applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-option'), true);
        });
      });
      document.addEventListener('click', function () { closeMenu(section); });
    }
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      button.addEventListener('click', function () {
        applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button'), true);
      });
    });
    applyMarket(section, marketId, false);
  });
})();
