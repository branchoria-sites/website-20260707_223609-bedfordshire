## Introduction

Cardigan Bay’s drowned kingdom legend tells of a rich lowland realm beneath the sea off [Cardiganshire]({{ 'where-cardiganshire-turns-landscape-into/' | relative_url }}), lost when water gates were left open and the tide poured in. The kingdom is usually called Cantre’r Gwaelod, or the Lowland Hundred, and its memory gathers around places such as Borth, Ynyslas, Aberystwyth and Aberdyfi. It matters because it is not just a fantasy of a “Welsh Atlantis”: it is a local way of explaining a coastline where old tree stumps, peat beds, storms, bells and shifting sands make the past feel only half-submerged. The evidence does not prove a drowned medieval city under Cardigan Bay, but it does show something more interesting: a powerful legend attached to real drowned landscapes, early Welsh poetry, coastal change and long community memory.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://rcahmw.gov.uk/submerged-forests-shipwrecks-and-sarns-exploring-the-mythical-kingdom-of-cantrer-gwaelod/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: rcahmw.gov.uk">[RCAHMW]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">rcahmw.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Submerged Forests, Shipwrecks and Sarns: Exploring the Mythical Kingdom of Cantre’r Gwaelod...</span></span></span>

## What the drowned kingdom story says happened

In the best-known version, Cantre’r Gwaelod was a fertile, low-lying country protected from Cardigan Bay by sea walls, dykes and sluice gates. The gates had to be opened at low tide to let water drain out, then closed again before the sea returned. One stormy night, the appointed watcher failed in his duty. The gates remained open, the tide broke through, and the land was drowned.

The ruler is commonly named as Gwyddno Garanhir, and the negligent figure as Seithennin or Seithenyn. Later retellings often make Seithennin a drunkard at a feast, too distracted or too intoxicated to close the gates. In these versions, the disaster becomes a moral story about responsibility, pride and the danger of ignoring the sea. The Royal Commission on the Ancient and Historical Monuments of Wales summarises the familiar form as a sixth-century kingdom of Gwyddno Garanhir drowned by a spring tide when sluice gates in a protective sea wall were left open.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://rcahmw.gov.uk/submerged-forests-shipwrecks-and-sarns-exploring-the-mythical-kingdom-of-cantrer-gwaelod/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: rcahmw.gov.uk">[RCAHMW]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">rcahmw.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Submerged Forests, Shipwrecks and Sarns: Exploring the Mythical Kingdom of Cantre’r Gwaelod...</span></span></span>

For Cardiganshire, the legend is especially vivid because the bay is not an abstract setting. The story is usually imagined somewhere off the Ceredigion coast, with Borth and Ynyslas providing the most memorable visible “evidence” in the form of a submerged forest. Local accounts also place the lost land across a much wider sweep of Cardigan Bay, sometimes reaching towards Bardsey Island in the north and Ramsey Island in the south, which shows how the tale belongs to the whole bay while still having a strong Cardiganshire centre of gravity.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.borthcommunity.info/index.php/legends" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: borthcommunity.info">[Borth Community]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">borthcommunity.info</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Borth Community Legends</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Borth Community Legends</span></span></span>

## Gwyddno Garanhir and the opened sea gates

The legend’s cast has changed over time. Today’s popular form tends to centre on Gwyddno Garanhir, his lowland kingdom, and the failed gatekeeper Seithennin. That is the version most readers meet in tourism writing, local storytelling and modern folklore summaries. It has a clear plot: a prosperous land is made safe by engineering, a trusted person neglects the system, and the sea takes everything.

The older literary evidence is not quite the same. A BBC archive page on the legend notes that the story is first thought to be mentioned in the Black Book of Carmarthen, a manuscript written around 1250, in a poem called “Boddi Maes Gwyddno”, or “The Drowning of the Land of Gwyddno”. In that early version, responsibility falls not on the familiar drunken watchman but on a maiden named Mererid, who allows the destructive water to be released.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://downloads.bbc.co.uk/wales/archive/bbc-mid-wales-weird-cantref-gwaelod-the-lost-lands.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: downloads.bbc.co.uk">[BBC Downloads]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">downloads.bbc.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">BBC Downloads</span></span></span>

This difference matters. It shows that Cantre’r Gwaelod is not a single fixed tale handed down unchanged. It is a story-family. Its core elements remain recognisable — Gwyddno’s land, flooding, blame, loss — but the details shift according to storyteller, period and purpose. The medieval poem is spare and dramatic; later prose versions are more like a cautionary local legend; modern retellings often emphasise climate, erosion or the uncanny bells beneath the sea.

The sea-gate version is also unusually practical for a myth. It imagines a lowland defended by human works, not simply punished by divine force or swallowed by magic. That makes it feel close to real coastal life. Anyone living by a storm-prone shore can understand the fear behind it: a gate, wall, embankment or watch left unattended can decide whether land remains land.

## Borth forest and responsible evidence

The most persuasive thing about the drowned kingdom legend is not proof of towers, streets or palaces under Cardigan Bay. It is the way real prehistoric remains make the coastline look like a lost country. At Borth and Ynyslas, storms and low tides can expose peat beds and tree stumps from an ancient forest preserved beneath sand and seawater. The Royal Commission records pine, oak, elder and birch at Borth, with the trees stopping growth between about 4,500 and 6,000 years ago as the landscape was inundated.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://rcahmw.gov.uk/wonders-of-borth-submerged-forest-explained-by-dr-martin-bates-of-university-of-wales-trinity-saint-david-2/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: rcahmw.gov.uk">[RCAHMW]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">rcahmw.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wonders of Borth Submerged Forest Explained by Dr Martin Bates of University of Wales Trinity Saint David...</span></span></span>

Coflein, the Royal Commission’s online record for Welsh archaeological sites, gives a fuller archaeological picture. It records that the Borth submerged forest flourished between about 5,000 and 4,500 years before present, with later waterlogging and peat growth; it also notes finds including a Mesolithic antler tool, flints, part of an auroch skeleton, human and animal footprints, post holes, burnt stone and a wattle walkway exposed after storms in January 2014.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://coflein.gov.uk/en/sites/506500?filter%5B0%5D%5Blevel%5D=Batch&amp;filter%5B1%5D%5Barcnum%5D=6202314&amp;type=archive" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: coflein.gov.uk">[Coflein]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">coflein.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Coflein English – Coflein</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Coflein English – Coflein</span></span></span>

That is remarkable evidence for a changing prehistoric landscape, but it is not evidence for the literal medieval kingdom of later legend. The useful distinction is simple:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **What is well supported:** ancient woodland, peat, footprints, tools, animal remains and trackway-like structures existed in what is now a coastal and intertidal landscape.
* **What is plausible:** visible drowned trees and coastal erosion helped people imagine, preserve or localise stories of lost land.
* **What is not proved:** a walled kingdom with cities, palaces and church bells lies intact beneath Cardigan Bay.

</div>

Dyfed Archaeological Trust’s education material makes the same careful connection: submerged forests around the Welsh coast are tangible evidence of former woods swamped by encroaching tides, and before their true nature was understood they helped inspire stories of lands swept away by the sea. It also notes that these sites do not all belong to one flooding event; dates from Ynyslas suggest trees died around 5,500 years ago, while Borth trees died about 2,000 years later.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://heneb.org.uk/archive/dyfed/lostlandscapes/submergedforests.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: heneb.org.uk">[Heneb]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">heneb.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Heneb Exploring the submerged landscapes of Prehistoric Wales</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Heneb Exploring the submerged landscapes of Prehistoric Wales</span></span></span>

This is why the Borth forest is so important for a folklore reader. It does not “solve” Cantre’r Gwaelod by proving the story literally true. Instead, it shows how a landscape can keep giving storytellers something to work with. After storms, the bay reveals rooted stumps, black peat and traces of human and animal movement. The old tale then supplies meaning: this was once land; something was lost here; the sea has a memory.

## Why the coastline makes the legend believable

Cardigan Bay is a natural home for drowned-land stories because its shore visibly changes. Sand is stripped away, peat reappears, old timber is exposed, and then the sea covers everything again. A visitor who sees the Borth forest at low tide is not looking at an ordinary beach feature. They are seeing evidence that the line between land and sea has moved.

Researchers Erin Kavanagh and Martin Bates, writing in *Internet Archaeology*, are cautious about reconstructing the pre-inundation landscape of Cardigan Bay. They stress that the bay’s earlier shape and flooding history are complex, and that current evidence is not enough to rebuild the whole lost landscape in detail. Even seabed bathymetry, although useful, cannot simply be read as a direct map of the land before inundation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://intarch.ac.uk/journal/issue53/8/4.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: intarch.ac.uk">[Internet Archaeology]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">intarch.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on intarch.ac.uk.</span></span></span>

That caution improves the legend rather than weakening it. The real story is not “archaeology proves Atlantis in Wales”. The better reading is that folklore and science are looking at the same uneasy boundary from different directions. Folklore remembers disaster through blame, bells and named characters. Archaeology records peat, roots, tools, footprints, coastal erosion and sea-level change. Both recognise that Cardigan Bay was not always exactly as it appears now.

The legend also belongs to a wider Welsh pattern of stories attached to drowned places, dangerous waters and old sounds under the surface. But Cantre’r Gwaelod has its own Cardiganshire force because the evidence is so physically accessible. At Borth, the “lost land” is not just far offshore; it can appear underfoot, dark and ribbed with ancient roots.

## Bells beneath the water and coastal memory

The bells are one of the most haunting parts of the tradition. According to the Royal Commission, the church bells of Cantre’r Gwaelod are said to be heard at Aberdyfi, ringing from beneath the sea and foretelling danger.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://rcahmw.gov.uk/submerged-forests-shipwrecks-and-sarns-exploring-the-mythical-kingdom-of-cantrer-gwaelod/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: rcahmw.gov.uk">[RCAHMW]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">rcahmw.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Submerged Forests, Shipwrecks and Sarns: Exploring the Mythical Kingdom of Cantre’r Gwaelod...</span></span></span>

This detail turns the legend from a one-time catastrophe into an ongoing relationship with the coast. The drowned kingdom is not simply gone. It is imagined as still present below the water, capable of sending warnings. Bells are especially powerful in local folklore because they belong to community life: churches, timekeeping, worship, alarm, mourning and celebration. A bell under the sea suggests a whole society continuing as an echo.

The bell motif also helps explain why Cantre’r Gwaelod has survived so well in song and retelling. Dyfed Archaeological Trust quotes lines from “Clychau Cantre’r Gwaelod”, a song by J. J. Williams, in which the bells lie silent beneath the waves.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://heneb.org.uk/archive/dyfed/lostlandscapes/submergedforests.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: heneb.org.uk">[Heneb]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">heneb.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Heneb Exploring the submerged landscapes of Prehistoric Wales</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Heneb Exploring the submerged landscapes of Prehistoric Wales</span></span></span> This is folklore doing what it often does best: compressing a landscape, a warning and a feeling of loss into one memorable image.

For Cardiganshire identity, the bells make the bay feel inhabited by memory. They give the coast a voice. Storms are not just weather; they are reminders. Low tides are not just tide tables; they are openings into older ground. The sound beneath the water is a poetic way of saying that the county’s coastline carries more history than can be seen on a modern map.

## Old tale, literary shaping, tourist retelling

Cantre’r Gwaelod has passed through several forms, and separating them helps avoid two common mistakes: treating every modern detail as ancient, or dismissing the whole tradition because it has changed.

The medieval layer is represented by “Boddi Maes Gwyddno” in the Black Book of Carmarthen, where the land of Gwyddno is drowned and Mererid is blamed. The popular gatekeeper version, with Seithennin failing to close the sea defences, is the version most familiar today. Antiquarian and topographical writers then added another layer by reporting supposed remains, stones or signs of habitation off the coast; the Royal Commission notes references ranging from the medieval poem to William Owen Pughe in 1770 and Samuel Lewis in 1846.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://rcahmw.gov.uk/submerged-forests-shipwrecks-and-sarns-exploring-the-mythical-kingdom-of-cantrer-gwaelod/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: rcahmw.gov.uk">[RCAHMW]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">rcahmw.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Submerged Forests, Shipwrecks and Sarns: Exploring the Mythical Kingdom of Cantre’r Gwaelod...</span></span></span>

Modern tourism and heritage writing add a further layer. Borth community material, for example, openly associates the submerged forest with the legend while also stating that there is no reliable physical evidence for the substantial community promised by the story.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.borthcommunity.info/index.php/legends" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: borthcommunity.info">[Borth Community]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">borthcommunity.info</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Borth Community Legends</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Borth Community Legends</span></span></span> That balance is exactly what a good modern retelling needs: it preserves the wonder without pretending the archaeology says more than it does.

The result is a living legend rather than a museum specimen. A medieval poem, a moral folktale, a coastal warning, an archaeological beach walk and a tourist mystery can all be part of Cantre’r Gwaelod’s afterlife. The important thing is to keep their kinds of truth distinct.

## Why it matters in Cardiganshire folklore

Cardiganshire folklore often ties the strange to a precise feature of the landscape: a bridge, a hill, a lake, a well, a wooded hollow, a beach. Cantre’r Gwaelod is the county’s great sea-facing example. It makes Cardigan Bay feel like a mythic landscape without floating free from evidence. The legend belongs to named shores, real storms, visible peat and a coastline where erosion and exposure are part of local experience.

It also gives the county a story that links everyday coastal risk with deep time. The tale says that negligence drowned a kingdom. The archaeology says that forests, animals and people once occupied environments now altered by sea and sand. Modern readers do not have to choose between “just a myth” and “literal lost city”. The richer interpretation is that Cantre’r Gwaelod is a folklore vessel for real anxieties: sea-level change, storm damage, fragile defences, vanished land and the fear that prosperity can disappear quickly.

That is why the drowned kingdom remains one of the most memorable legends attached to Cardiganshire. It is beautiful, mournful and usefully unsettling. Stand on Borth beach when the old stumps show through, and the story’s endurance becomes easy to understand: the bay does not need to hide a complete kingdom to feel like a place where land, memory and legend have gone under together.
