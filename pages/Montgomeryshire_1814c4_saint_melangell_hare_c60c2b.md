## Introduction

Saint Melangell’s hare sanctuary at Pennant Melangell is one of [Montgomeryshire]({{ 'where-montgomeryshires-old-stories-still/' | relative_url }})’s most memorable saintly traditions: a hunted hare runs beneath the cloak of a holy woman, the prince’s hounds refuse to touch it, and a remote valley becomes a place of refuge. The story belongs to Pennant Melangell at the head of the Tanat Valley, near Llangynog, in the Berwyn mountains of historic Montgomeryshire, now within Powys. It matters because it joins three things rarely held together so vividly in one local legend: a female saint, an animal protected from the hunt, and a landscape remembered as sanctuary. The Shrine Church of Saint Melangell still presents the valley as a place of pilgrimage and care, while heritage sources record the site’s Grade I church, medieval shrine, circular churchyard, ancient yews and unusually strong local association with hares.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://stmelangell.org/saint-melangell/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: stmelangell.org">[stmelangell.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">stmelangell.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Prince&#x27;s hounds fled, and he was moved by</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Saint Melangell Shrine Church &amp; CentreSaint Melangell - patron saint of haresOne day Brochwel, Prince of Powys, was hunting and pursued a...</span></span></span>

The tale should be read as tradition rather than simple biography. The best-known narrative comes through medieval and later hagiography, local devotion, antiquarian notice and modern pilgrimage, not from a contemporary seventh-century record. Yet the place itself is not a vague story-site. Pennant Melangell has material depth: a twelfth-century church, a Romanesque shrine dated architecturally to about 1160–70, medieval fittings, archaeological discoveries and a continuing public life as a shrine church and retreat centre.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cadwpublic-api.azurewebsites.net/reports/listedbuilding/FullReport?id=7634" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cadwpublic-api.azurewebsites.net">[azurewebsites.net]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cadwpublic-api.azurewebsites.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Cadw Public APIListed Buildings</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cadw Public APIListed Buildings</span></span></span>

## Melangell, Brochwel and the hunted hare

The core legend is simple enough to remember after one hearing. Melangell, often described in tradition as an Irish princess who had fled an arranged marriage, lived as a hermit in the Pennant valley. One day Brochwel, Prince of Powys, was hunting. His hounds chased a hare into the thicket where Melangell was praying. The hare hid beneath her cloak or dress, and the dogs either retreated or would not attack. Moved by her courage and holiness, Brochwel granted her the valley as a place of sanctuary, where she founded a religious community.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://stmelangell.org/saint-melangell/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: stmelangell.org">[Saint Melangell Shrine Church &amp; Centre]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">stmelangell.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Prince&#x27;s hounds fled, and he was moved by</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Saint Melangell Shrine Church &amp; CentreSaint Melangell - patron saint of haresOne day Brochwel, Prince of Powys, was hunting and pursued a...</span></span></span>

That narrative works on several levels at once. On the surface it is an animal-rescue story. Beneath that, it is a saint’s legend about power being halted by sanctity: the prince, the hunt and the hounds give way to a woman alone in prayer. For a Montgomeryshire folklore page, the most important mechanism is the reversal of the chase. The hare is not saved by speed, cunning or luck, but by crossing into sacred protection. The valley becomes memorable because the story turns it into a boundary-place: outside it, the ordinary rules of hunting apply; inside it, the vulnerable may claim refuge.

The prince in the story is usually named as Brochwel Ysgithrog, a ruler associated with Powys. The traditional date is often given as 604, but this should be handled carefully. Modern summaries and church accounts preserve the seventh-century framing, while scholarly discussions of the saint’s life note that the surviving hagiographical evidence is much later and that the exact chronology is uncertain. The result is not that the story is worthless, but that it belongs to the world of saintly memory rather than court record.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Melangell" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wikipedia.org.</span></span></span>

The tale’s emotional force comes from its restraint. There is no monster, no spectacular curse, and no elaborate miracle beyond the hounds’ failure to strike. The miracle is a refusal: the animals of the hunt stop behaving like instruments of pursuit. In folklore terms, this is why the hare became so strong an emblem. It is small, frightened and ordinary, but once it reaches Melangell it becomes the sign by which the whole valley is understood.

## Pennant Melangell as sanctuary and pilgrimage place

Pennant Melangell sits at the head of the Tanat Valley, reached from Llangynog by a narrow road. Modern visitor information still emphasises that the approach is part of the experience: the site lies deep in the Berwyn mountains, with access along a small valley road rather than through a large town or obvious tourist centre.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.visitmidwales.co.uk/things-to-do/the-shrine-church-of-saint-melangell-p1725381" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: visitmidwales.co.uk">[Visit Mid Wales]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">visitmidwales.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Visit Mid Wales The Shrine Church of Saint Melangell</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Visit Mid Wales The Shrine Church of Saint Melangell</span></span></span>

That remoteness matters to the legend. The story needs a valley that feels set apart. “Sanctuary” here is not only a legal or ecclesiastical idea; it is also a landscape feeling. The church stands in a round churchyard, and the official shrine church account describes it as ringed by ancient yews and set in a deep bowl of the valley near the stream head. The National Churches Trust similarly describes Pennant Melangell as a simple Norman, twelfth-century church on a Bronze Age site, with a circular churchyard and old yews.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://stmelangell.org/the-shrine-church-of-saint-melangell/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: stmelangell.org">[Saint Melangell Shrine Church &amp; Centre]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">stmelangell.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on stmelangell.org.</span></span></span>

The present church is not merely a scenic setting for a tale. Cadw’s listed-building report records pilgrimage to the shrine as well established by the fifteenth century and identifies the shrine of St Melangell as a fine Norman monument dated architecturally to 1160–70. The same report notes that the shrine was probably broken up in the Reformation, a pattern familiar from many medieval shrines, but especially poignant here because Melangell’s cult is so bound to protection and refuge.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cadwpublic-api.azurewebsites.net/reports/listedbuilding/FullReport?id=7634" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cadwpublic-api.azurewebsites.net">[Cadw Public API]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cadwpublic-api.azurewebsites.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Cadw Public APIListed Buildings</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cadw Public APIListed Buildings</span></span></span>

The shrine’s survival story has become part of the modern meaning of the place. A Living Stones heritage guide describes the shrine as internationally significant, constructed around 1160 to hold Melangell’s relics, and says that after orders against relics in the Reformation period its stones were built into walls and the lychgate before being recognised in 1894 and reconstructed in the twentieth century.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.living-stones.info/downloads/guides/5_St_Melangells_Card_web.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: living-stones.info">[Living Stones]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">living-stones.info</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on living-stones.info.</span></span></span>

For readers interested in folklore, this is where story and object meet. The hare legend explains why the place was loved; the shrine shows that love becoming architecture. Even after disruption, the carved fragments remained physically embedded in the church’s fabric. The sanctuary was, in a literal sense, broken up and built back into the place.

## Why the hare became a sacred emblem

The hare at Pennant Melangell is not just a decorative animal attached to a saint after the fact. It is the hinge of the tradition. Without the hare, there is no stopped hunt, no public revelation of Melangell’s holiness, and no reason for Brochwel to grant the valley as sanctuary. In the legend, the hare is both an animal and a messenger: it brings the prince into contact with the hidden saint.

This is why later tradition remembered hares in the parish with unusual tenderness. Thomas Pennant, the eighteenth-century Welsh antiquarian and travel writer, is often cited for recording that hares in the district were known as St Monacella’s lambs, Monacella being a Latinised form of Melangell’s name. Later summaries of his account also preserve the belief that people would not kill hares in the parish and that a pursued hare could be protected by invoking God and St Monacella.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Melangell" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wikipedia.org.</span></span></span>

That tradition makes Pennant Melangell stand out within Montgomeryshire folklore. Many counties have saintly wells, healing places or churchyard legends, but here the sacred sign is a wild animal normally associated with coursing and the open uplands. The hare’s holiness is not abstract. It changes behaviour: do not kill it; call protection over it; remember that the saint’s first miracle was mercy towards a hunted creature.

Modern retellings often extend Melangell’s patronage to rabbits, small animals and the natural environment. That is a contemporary broadening of the older hare-centred cult, but it is not a random invention. It grows naturally from the legend’s central image: a frightened animal crossing into sacred refuge. The current shrine church presents Melangell as patron saint of hares and tells the Brochwel story as the defining episode of her life.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://stmelangell.org/saint-melangell/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: stmelangell.org">[Saint Melangell Shrine Church &amp; Centre]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">stmelangell.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Prince&#x27;s hounds fled, and he was moved by</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Saint Melangell Shrine Church &amp; CentreSaint Melangell - patron saint of haresOne day Brochwel, Prince of Powys, was hunting and pursued a...</span></span></span>

It is worth resisting two opposite mistakes. One is to flatten the hare into a modern animal-rights mascot and forget the medieval religious frame. The other is to treat the animal element as merely quaint. The strength of the tradition lies in the combination: the hare is protected because Melangell is holy, and Melangell is remembered locally because the hare made her holiness visible.

## What survives in stone, wood and churchyard

Pennant Melangell’s power as a folklore site comes partly from how much a visitor can still see. The church is a Grade I listed building, with parts dating from the twelfth century and later restoration, including the rebuilding of the apse on its original foundations in 1990. The shrine church’s own account says a Christian church has existed there for more than 1,200 years, while Cadw’s more cautious architectural record highlights the medieval fabric, the Norman font, the shrine, and evidence revealed by excavation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://stmelangell.org/the-shrine-church-of-saint-melangell/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: stmelangell.org">[Saint Melangell Shrine Church &amp; Centre]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">stmelangell.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on stmelangell.org.</span></span></span>

Clwyd-Powys Archaeological Trust material describes Pennant Melangell as the best-studied church in Montgomeryshire because of excavations and recording in 1989, later fully published in *Montgomeryshire Collections* in 1994. It also notes the church’s notable contents: the rood screen, two fourteenth-century effigies, the shrine, fragmentary medieval wall paintings, a seventh-century chest, Hanoverian royal arms and an eighteenth-century candelabrum.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://heneb.org.uk/archive/cpat/ycom/mont/pennantmelangell.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: heneb.org.uk">[Heneb]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">heneb.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on heneb.org.uk.</span></span></span>

The site’s depth goes further back than the medieval church. The regional Historic Environment Record records prehistoric pits at the west end of the church and beneath the tower, including charred plant remains and cremated human bone, with radiocarbon evidence indicating a Middle Bronze Age date. That does not prove a straight line from Bronze Age rite to Melangell’s cult, and it should not be turned into a neat “pagan origins” claim. What it does show is that this valley floor held ritual or burial significance long before the surviving medieval church.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archwilio.org.uk/her/chi3/report/page.php?watprn=CPAT50677" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archwilio.org.uk">[Archwilio]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archwilio.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on archwilio.org.uk.</span></span></span>

The churchyard adds another layer. The National Churches Trust describes the round churchyard, the two gates, and ancient yews, some presented in local and church sources as around two thousand years old. Such claims about tree age can be difficult to verify exactly, but the visual and symbolic effect is clear: the church stands inside a living enclosure that feels older than the building itself.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nationalchurchestrust.org/church/st-melangell-pennant-melangell" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nationalchurchestrust.org">[National Churches Trust]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nationalchurchestrust.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nationalchurchestrust.org.</span></span></span>

For folklore readers, this material evidence matters because it keeps the legend grounded. Pennant Melangell is not simply “a story said to have happened somewhere in Wales”. It is a named Montgomeryshire valley with an unusual church, a reconstructed medieval shrine, a long memory of pilgrimage, and archaeological signs that people treated the place as special across very different periods.

## How old and well-attested is the tradition?

The honest answer is layered. The legend is old in tradition, medieval in its surviving religious culture, but not securely documented from Melangell’s supposed lifetime. The saint is commonly placed in the seventh or eighth century, yet the most detailed written life is much later. A modern pilgrimage summary states plainly that the events are placed in the seventh or eighth century but recorded in a fifteenth-century manuscript, while scholarly summaries identify the *Historia Divae Monacellae* as the key hagiographical source and treat its chronology with caution.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.britishpilgrimage.org/places/st-melangells-church-pennant-melangell" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: britishpilgrimage.org">[British Pilgrimage Trust]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">britishpilgrimage.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on britishpilgrimage.org.</span></span></span>

What strengthens the tradition is the medieval cult at the place itself. Cadw records the shrine as a Norman monument of about 1160–70 and says pilgrimage was well established by the fifteenth century. CPAT material points to extensive excavation and recording, while the Living Stones guide links the shrine’s construction to the holding of Melangell’s relics. Taken together, these sources show that devotion to Melangell was not a recent romantic invention.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cadwpublic-api.azurewebsites.net/reports/listedbuilding/FullReport?id=7634" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cadwpublic-api.azurewebsites.net">[azurewebsites.net]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cadwpublic-api.azurewebsites.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Cadw Public APIListed Buildings</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cadw Public APIListed Buildings</span></span></span>

What remains uncertain is the exact historical Melangell. Was there a female religious founder at Pennant whose memory later gathered the hare story? Did the sanctuary tradition preserve a genuine early medieval practice? How much of the Brochwel episode is hagiographical drama? These questions cannot be answered with the certainty of a dated charter or eyewitness chronicle. They are exactly the sort of uncertainty that local saintly folklore often carries.

The distinction is important because it makes the story more interesting, not less. Pennant Melangell’s tradition has survived through different forms of authority: saint’s life, shrine, parish memory, antiquarian writing, archaeology, church restoration and modern pilgrimage. Each layer tells us something slightly different. The written legend tells us what kind of holiness people imagined; the shrine tells us that devotion took institutional and artistic form; the hare taboo tells us how belief entered everyday behaviour.

## The sacred valley in Montgomeryshire identity

Within Montgomeryshire folklore, Pennant Melangell is unusually gentle but not weak. The county has traditions of fairy encounters, holy wells, charms, borderland danger and upland mystery, but Melangell’s valley offers a different emotional centre: protection rather than threat. It is a story about stopping harm, not explaining it.

That makes it especially suited to its landscape. Historic Montgomeryshire is full of valleys and routes where local memory clings to parish, farm, stream and hill rather than to large urban monuments. Pennant Melangell takes that pattern to an extreme. The visitor does not arrive at a grand cathedral city; they travel up a narrow valley to a small church near the head of the stream. The place-name itself is commonly explained by the shrine church as meaning the head of the stream, fitting the legend’s sense of an enclosed source-place.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://stmelangell.org/the-shrine-church-of-saint-melangell/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: stmelangell.org">[Saint Melangell Shrine Church &amp; Centre]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">stmelangell.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on stmelangell.org.</span></span></span>

The legend also preserves an older Powys identity within Montgomeryshire. Brochwel is not a modern county figure but a prince of Powys, while Pennant Melangell now lies in Powys administratively and in historic Montgomeryshire for county-folklore purposes. That overlap is useful rather than confusing. It shows how Welsh folklore often belongs to several maps at once: the medieval kingdom, the historic county, the parish, the modern county, and the tourist route.

The local story also connects naturally with other Welsh traditions of sanctuary and healing. The shrine was a pilgrimage site for people seeking help, and the modern Saint Melangell Centre continues the sanctuary theme in pastoral form, offering space for reflection, listening and care in a Christian context.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://stmelangell.org/saint-melangell-centre/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: stmelangell.org">[Saint Melangell Shrine Church &amp; Centre]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">stmelangell.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on stmelangell.org.</span></span></span>

This continuity is one reason the place still resonates. The medieval story says the hunted can find refuge. The modern centre does not reproduce medieval sanctuary law, but it keeps the valley’s meaning recognisable: people come apart from ordinary pressure, seeking stillness, prayer, healing or simply a place where distress can be held.

## How the story is retold today

Today, Melangell is retold through church interpretation, pilgrimage writing, local tourism, heritage trails, animal symbolism and online folklore. The strongest modern accounts keep the setting and the hare at the centre: a remote valley, a woman at prayer, a hunted animal, hounds that fail, and a prince who grants sanctuary. The Shrine Church of Saint Melangell gives the tradition in devotional form, while visitor and heritage sources explain the church, access, shrine and landscape for a wider public.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://stmelangell.org/saint-melangell/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: stmelangell.org">[stmelangell.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">stmelangell.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Prince&#x27;s hounds fled, and he was moved by</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Saint Melangell Shrine Church &amp; CentreSaint Melangell - patron saint of haresOne day Brochwel, Prince of Powys, was hunting and pursued a...</span></span></span>

Modern retellings also tend to emphasise Melangell as a saint for ecological concern. Nation.Cymru, marking her feast day in 2026, described her as a female saint with a message for the ages and foregrounded her association with hares, small animals and the natural environment. That emphasis speaks strongly to present-day readers, though it should be understood as a modern resonance of an older devotional tradition rather than proof that the original legend was “about ecology” in today’s terms.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://nation.cymru/feature/melangell-the-female-saint-with-a-message-for-the-ages/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nation.cymru">[Nation.Cymru]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nation.cymru</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Melangell: The female saint with a message for the ages</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Melangell: The female saint with a message for the ages</span></span></span>

The site’s restoration history has also shaped how people tell the story. Because the shrine was dismantled, recognised in fragments, reconstructed and re-sited, modern visitors encounter a place that is both ancient and consciously restored. British Pilgrimage Trust notes that the shrine incorporates original fragments and replacement material, allowing visitors to see the difference between survival and reconstruction. That transparency matters: the power of the shrine does not depend on pretending nothing ever changed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.britishpilgrimage.org/places/st-melangells-church-pennant-melangell" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: britishpilgrimage.org">[British Pilgrimage Trust]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">britishpilgrimage.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on britishpilgrimage.org.</span></span></span>

The hare remains the most portable part of the tradition. It appears in art, church imagery, devotional writing and visitor memory because it condenses the whole legend into one figure. A hare under a cloak says: the hunted have reached sanctuary. That is why Melangell’s story travels beyond Pennant Melangell, even as its force still depends on the valley.

## Why this legend lasts

Saint Melangell’s legend lasts because it gives Montgomeryshire a sacred landscape with a clear moral image. A prince can command hounds, but he cannot command holiness. A hunted hare can be seconds from death, yet become untouchable by reaching sanctuary. A remote valley can become famous not through battle or wealth, but through mercy.

The tradition is not best approached as a puzzle to be either believed literally or dismissed. Its value lies in how it binds story, place and practice. The story explains the hare’s sanctity; the place preserves the saint’s memory; the shrine and churchyard give that memory material form; the continuing pilgrimage keeps the valley active rather than merely antiquarian.

For Montgomeryshire folklore, Pennant Melangell is a pillar site because it shows how local legend can make a landscape morally legible. The valley is not just beautiful or old. It is remembered as a refuge. That is the mechanism at the heart of the tale: the sacred interrupts the hunt, and the place where it happens becomes a sanctuary in the county’s imagination.

    closeMenu(section);
  }
  sections.forEach(function (section) {
    if (section.getAttribute('data-ebay-localized-init') === '1') return;
    section.setAttribute('data-ebay-localized-init', '1');
    var available = availableMarkets(section);
    var marketId = defaultMarket;
    if (window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation && window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation.inferMarket) {
      marketId = window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation.inferMarket(available, {
        storageKey: 'phoenix-ebay-market',
        defaultMarket: defaultMarket
      });
    } else if (available.indexOf(defaultMarket)< 0) {
      marketId = available[0] || defaultMarket;
    }
    var select = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-select]');
    if (select) {
      select.addEventListener('change', function () {
        applyMarket(section, select.value, true);
      });
    }
    var trigger = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-trigger]');
    var menu = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-menu]');
    if (trigger && menu) {
      trigger.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
        event.stopPropagation();
        var expanded = trigger.getAttribute('aria-expanded') === 'true';
        menu.hidden = expanded;
        trigger.setAttribute('aria-expanded', expanded ? 'false' : 'true');
        var picker = trigger.closest('.fr-ebay-market-picker');
        if (picker) picker.classList.toggle('fr-ebay-market-picker--open', !expanded);
      });
      Array.prototype.slice.call(menu.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-option]')).forEach(function (button) {
        button.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
          event.stopPropagation();
          applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-option'), true);
        });
      });
      document.addEventListener('click', function () { closeMenu(section); });
    }
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      button.addEventListener('click', function () {
        applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button'), true);
      });
    });
    applyMarket(section, marketId, false);
  });
})();
