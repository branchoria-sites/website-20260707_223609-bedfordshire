## Introduction

[Oxfordshire]({{ 'where-oxfordshires-old-stories-still/' | relative_url }})’s village customs are not only found in old stories about stones, saints or haunted ruins. They are also kept alive by people turning out, year after year, to dance, process, play music, feast and take over familiar streets for a day. Bampton’s morris dancing and Kirtlington’s Lamb Ale are two of the county’s clearest examples: both are public, place-rooted customs in which folklore is performed rather than merely told.



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/Oxfordshire_491609_village_customs_morr_9fef09-overview.webp" | relative_url }}" alt="Overview image for Village Customs" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

Bampton is best known for its Whitsun morris tradition, now centred on the Spring Bank Holiday. Kirtlington’s Lamb Ale is a revived village festival with documented links to Whitsun ales, morris dancing, a “Lady of the Lamb”, processions and feasting from at least the late seventeenth century. Together they show how Oxfordshire custom survives through repetition, adaptation and local pride: not as a frozen museum piece, but as a living village performance shaped by dancers, musicians, pubs, greens, archives and audiences.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://bamptonmorris.co.uk/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: bamptonmorris.co.uk">[bamptonmorris.co.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">bamptonmorris.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on bamptonmorris.co.uk.</span></span></span>

## Why village custom matters in Oxfordshire folklore

Oxfordshire folklore is often imagined through ancient landmarks: the Uffington [White Horse]({{ 'white-horse-383a9c/' | relative_url }}), Dragon Hill, Wayland’s Smithy and the Rollright Stones. Bampton and Kirtlington add a different kind of evidence. Their traditions are not mainly about explaining a mysterious monument. They are about making a village visible to itself.

That matters because seasonal custom works through use. A dance route, a pub stop, a feast day or a village green becomes memorable because people gather there at the same point in the year. The custom is partly the dance or ritual itself, but it is also the local knowledge around it: where to stand, which side appears first, which pub is connected with which team, who remembers an older dancer, who has a photograph, and which details have changed.

Morris dancing is a form of English performance folk dance built around patterned steps, figures, music, costume and often bells, handkerchiefs or sticks. In Oxfordshire and neighbouring Cotswold country, it has become especially associated with village-named dance traditions, including Bampton, Headington Quarry, Adderbury and Kirtlington. The important point for readers is that “morris” is not just a generic national symbol. In these villages it is local: the same broad dance family, but with styles, steps, tunes, stories and claims of continuity attached to particular places.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.efdss.org/images/past/VWMLASSETS/PDFs/morris-bibliography.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: efdss.org">[English Folk Dance and Song Society]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">efdss.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on efdss.org.</span></span></span>

## Bampton morris dancing: a village turned into a stage

Bampton, in West Oxfordshire, is one of the most famous morris villages in England. Its annual day of dancing was historically linked with Whitsuntide and is now performed on the Spring Bank Holiday. The public pattern is distinctive: Bampton’s sides dance around the village through the day, stopping at pubs, streets, gardens and central spaces rather than confining the custom to a single stage. Current visitor information describes the three Bampton sides starting early and moving separately around the town, while a recent Whitsun programme notes dancing at places such as the Talbot Hotel, the Morris Clown, the Market Square, church gates and other village locations.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://bamptonmorris.co.uk/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: bamptonmorris.co.uk">[bamptonmorris.co.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">bamptonmorris.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on bamptonmorris.co.uk.</span></span></span>

This is why Bampton feels different from a festival booking. The village itself is the setting. The performance is not detached from everyday geography; it occupies it. Streets, gardens and pubs become part of the custom’s memory, and the audience follows the dance through the place rather than simply watching it from a seated distance.

Bampton’s claim to antiquity is strong in local tradition, but it needs careful wording. Local and morris sources often describe the custom as centuries old, with some claims reaching back several hundred years. The Bampton Archive gives the more cautious documentary position: morris dancing is first recorded in England in 1448, while the first Bampton recording dates to the eighteenth century, and there have been morris teams in Bampton since that time. That distinction is important. The tradition may well preserve older local practice, but the firm written evidence for Bampton begins later than the most expansive claims suggest.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.bamptonarchive.org/book-list/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: bamptonarchive.org">[The Bampton Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">bamptonarchive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Bampton Archive Read Our Books – The Bampton Archive</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Bampton Archive Read Our Books – The Bampton Archive</span></span></span>

Bampton also matters because it became a source village for the wider morris revival. William Nathan “Jingy” Wells, a Bampton dancer, fiddler and fool, is remembered for helping keep the Bampton morris going and for his strong rhythmic playing. Cecil Sharp and other collectors drew on village performers like Wells when writing down tunes and dances that later circulated far beyond Oxfordshire.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cecilsharpspeople.org.uk/wells-william-nathan.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cecilsharpspeople.org.uk">[cecilsharpspeople.org.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cecilsharpspeople.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Wells, William Nathan</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wells, William Nathan</span></span></span>

The result is a useful paradox. Bampton is intensely local, but its dances have travelled widely. Many people encounter “Bampton” as a dance tradition taught by a revival side somewhere else. Yet the village performance on Bank Holiday Monday still matters because it anchors those exported dances back to a place, a calendar and a social setting.

## Kirtlington Lamb Ale: feast, dance and revival

Kirtlington’s Lamb Ale is one of Oxfordshire’s most vivid seasonal customs because it combines feast, procession, morris dancing and a named ceremonial figure, the Lady of the Lamb. The modern festival is presented by Kirtlington Morris as one of the ancient Whitsun ales, traceable to the early 1600s, and held on the weekend of Trinity Sunday, 56 days after Easter.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://kirtlington-morris.org.uk/kirtlington-lamb-ale-2/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: kirtlington-morris.org.uk">[Kirtlington Morris]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">kirtlington-morris.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Kirtlington Morris Kirtlington Lamb Ale</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Kirtlington Morris Kirtlington Lamb Ale</span></span></span>

The best-known early description comes from Thomas Blount in 1679. It describes a live lamb, village maids with their thumbs tied behind them, the winner becoming “Lady of the Lamb”, music, and a morris dance of men and another of women. The place-name in the old account is often given as “Kidlington”, but specialist discussion treats this as probably an error for Kirtlington. Robert Dunlop’s detailed study for *The Histories of Morris in Britain* describes morris dancing at Kirtlington Lamb Ale as already established in the late seventeenth century, with the Blount reference as the earliest written notice.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://media.efdss.org/docs/HOM/HOM%20Dunlop%20Morris%20Dancing%20at%20Kirtlington.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: media.efdss.org">[EFDSS Media]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">media.efdss.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Media Histories of Morris</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Media Histories of Morris</span></span></span>

The older Lamb Ale was not a quiet heritage day. Dunlop’s account describes an eighteenth- and early nineteenth-century festival with Lord and Lady figures, processions, dancing, feasting, a bower on or near the village green, drinking, popular sports and a degree of social disorder. Morris dancers led processions and stopped to perform, while visiting sides from surrounding villages could be part of the occasion. The custom belonged to a world in which Whitsun ales and parish feasts mixed fundraising, festivity, status, rivalry and release from ordinary discipline.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://media.efdss.org/docs/HOM/HOM%20Dunlop%20Morris%20Dancing%20at%20Kirtlington.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: media.efdss.org">[EFDSS Media]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">media.efdss.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Media Histories of Morris</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Media Histories of Morris</span></span></span>

That same unruliness helps explain its decline. Dunlop notes that the Lamb Ale depended on gentry support, and that changing tastes among “respectable” classes made its boisterous character less acceptable. In the mid-nineteenth century the old Lamb Ale was increasingly displaced by a more orderly club feast connected with friendly-society culture. By 1877, George James Dew saw a morris dance at Kirtlington and described morris as “now almost extinct in these parts”, with dancers lacking the older full costume and insignia.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://media.efdss.org/docs/HOM/HOM%20Dunlop%20Morris%20Dancing%20at%20Kirtlington.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: media.efdss.org">[EFDSS Media]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">media.efdss.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Media Histories of Morris</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Media Histories of Morris</span></span></span>

The modern Lamb Ale is therefore a revival, not an unbroken survival in the same form. Kirtlington Morris states that the present Cotswold morris tradition was reconstructed and handed over to the village in 1979, with dances based on information gathered by Cecil Sharp and George Butterworth in the early twentieth century, and with new material added where needed. The Pitt Rivers Museum’s Englishness project similarly describes the Kirtlington version as reconstructed from partial information in Sharp’s manuscripts and notes the Lamb Ale’s revival around 1979 as a morris dancing festival.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://kirtlington-morris.org.uk/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: kirtlington-morris.org.uk">[Kirtlington Morris]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">kirtlington-morris.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on kirtlington-morris.org.uk.</span></span></span>

That does not make it “fake”. It makes it a good example of how custom is repaired. A broken or faded tradition can be rebuilt from notes, memory, archives, neighbouring practice and village will. Kirtlington’s modern Lamb Ale is not identical to the seventeenth-century Lamb Ale; it is a historically informed village celebration that openly carries older names, dates, themes and performance habits into a form modern people can take part in.

## What Bampton and Kirtlington show about living folklore

These two customs are often grouped under “morris dancing”, but they preserve different kinds of village memory.

Bampton’s strength is continuity of place and annual performance. Its most powerful image is the village day itself: dancers moving through Bampton from morning onwards, with local sides, visiting sides, pubs, gardens, music and spectators all contributing to the atmosphere. The dance is the centre, but the custom is bigger than the choreography.

Kirtlington’s strength is the dramatic history of a feast: the Lady of the Lamb, Whitsun ale, processions, old social licence, decline, and revival. It carries a clearer before-and-after story. The modern event knows it is reviving a custom whose older form partly disappeared, and that honesty makes the tradition more interesting rather than less.

Both also show that folklore is not always supernatural. Oxfordshire’s dragons, witches, haunted ruins and fairy-like landscapes matter, but so do customs that bind a community through public performance. A morris side can be as much a bearer of local memory as a ghost story. A feast day can preserve old ideas about status, season, courtship, village rivalry and communal belonging even when the details have been softened for modern audiences.

There is also a useful lesson about change. Bampton shifted from Whit Monday to the Spring Bank Holiday as the national holiday calendar changed. Kirtlington revived a festival after decline, using archive fragments and living dance culture to rebuild what could be rebuilt. These are not signs that custom has failed. They are the ordinary mechanics of survival. A tradition that cannot adapt may become only a record; a tradition that adapts carefully can remain recognisable while still belonging to the present.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://england.prm.ox.ac.uk/englishness-morris-dancers-costume.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: england.prm.ox.ac.uk">[ox.ac.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">england.prm.ox.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Morris dancer&#x27;s costume</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Morris dancer&#x27;s costume</span></span></span>

## How to read these customs today

For a modern visitor, the most rewarding way to understand Bampton or Kirtlington is not to ask whether every detail is “ancient”. A better question is: what has the village chosen to keep doing, and why?

At Bampton, watch how the custom uses space. The dance moves between pubs, streets, gardens and market places, so the village becomes a route of memory. Notice the repeated figures, the music, the bells, the relationship between dancers and watchers, and the way a public holiday makes room for a local identity that is older than most people present.

At Kirtlington, watch how revival and history sit side by side. The modern Lamb Ale no longer needs to recreate every rough edge of the older feast. Its value lies in the continuity of theme: Trinity-tide timing, morris sides, procession, village gathering, food, drink, music and the memory of the Lamb Ale as a special communal occasion. Current Kirtlington material still frames the festival around Lamb Ale history while also announcing practical modern programmes, visiting sides and local arrangements.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://kirtlington-morris.org.uk/kirtlington-lamb-ale-2/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: kirtlington-morris.org.uk">[Kirtlington Morris]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">kirtlington-morris.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Kirtlington Morris Kirtlington Lamb Ale</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Kirtlington Morris Kirtlington Lamb Ale</span></span></span>

For Oxfordshire folklore as a whole, these customs broaden the map. They show that the county’s traditional culture is not only hidden in prehistoric stones, chalk figures or ghostly ruins. It is also audible in fiddle tunes, visible in white kit and ribbons, and carried through the lanes by people who have learned when to turn up and what to do when the music starts.

## Why these customs still carry local pride

Bampton and Kirtlington endure because they give villages a form of public self-portrait. Outsiders may come for the spectacle, but the customs are not only for visitors. They organise local memory: families remember who danced, who played, who revived the side, who kept photographs, who hosted a stop, who argued about dates, and who made sure the event happened again.

That local pride is not sentimental decoration. It is part of the custom’s structure. A village tradition needs people willing to rehearse, steward, host, feed, photograph, archive, teach and explain. Bampton’s reputation depends on the repeated discipline of annual dancing. Kirtlington’s Lamb Ale depends on a community accepting that revival is worth the effort. In both cases, folklore survives because people implement it.

The strongest takeaway is simple: Oxfordshire’s village customs are not relics lying behind glass. They are annual acts of making-place. Bampton keeps morris dancing alive by sending it through the village. Kirtlington keeps the Lamb Ale alive by turning an old feast into a modern gathering. Together they show a county tradition that is musical, social, public and proudly local.
