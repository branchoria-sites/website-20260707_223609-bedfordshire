## Introduction

The strongest Cumberland material is not one neat mythology but a layered local culture: prehistoric monuments explained as witches or impossible stones, fairy places and boggles, Christian saint legends, haunted houses, black-dog and vampire-like tales, and living customs such as Workington’s Uppies and Downies and Egremont Crab Fair. Some stories are old antiquarian survivals; some are medieval religious legend; some are Victorian Gothic retellings; some are modern tourist folklore. The interest lies in telling those layers apart without draining the stories of their local power.

## Where Cumberland folklore lives on the map

Historic Cumberland is larger and older than today’s administrative name suggests. In geographic terms it includes Carlisle and the Solway Plain, the Cumberland coast, much of the western and northern Lake District, the Eden Valley around Penrith, and upland routes towards the Pennines. The Gazetteer of British Place Names describes the coastal plain with Whitehaven, Workington, Maryport and Silloth; the Eden Valley crossing the east of the county; Carlisle in the north; and Hadrian’s Wall running from Bowness-on-Solway across northern Cumberland.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://gazetteer.org.uk/place/Cumberland" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gazetteer.org.uk">[Gazetteer]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gazetteer.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Gazetteer Cumberland, historiccounty &#124; Gazetteer of British Place Names</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Gazetteer Cumberland, historiccounty &#124; Gazetteer of British Place Names</span></span></span>

That matters because folklore rarely obeys neat modern boundaries. The Local Government Act 1972 created Cumbria County Council from 1 April 1974, replacing older administrative arrangements; the old county identities of Cumberland, Westmorland and the Lancashire and Yorkshire fringe did not vanish from story, dialect or custom.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cumbriaarchives.org.uk/catalogues-and-guides/DC" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cumbriaarchives.org.uk">[cumbriaarchives.org.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cumbriaarchives.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">District Councils &#124; Cumbria Archives</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">District Councils &#124; Cumbria Archives</span></span></span> The 2023 Cumberland Council name revives part of the old identity in local government, but it does not make the historic and modern footprints identical. For a folklore page, “Cumberland” should therefore mean the historic county as a story-landscape, while noting that today’s tourism language often says “Cumbria” or “the Lake District”.

This creates one immediate source of confusion. A legend may be promoted as “Cumbrian” because that is the modern visitor brand, yet its older collecting history may place it in Cumberland, Westmorland, or Lancashire North of the Sands. [Long Meg]({{ 'long-meg/' | relative_url }}), Dunmail Raise, Muncaster, St Bees, Carlisle, Workington and Egremont all make good Cumberland anchors; Windermere lake-monster stories, by contrast, sit more naturally in the wider Lake District and Westmorland orbit, even when modern “Cumbria folklore” round-ups pull them into the same basket.

## Stone circles, witches and the problem of counting

Cumberland’s prehistoric monuments gave later people ready-made theatres for supernatural explanation. Long Meg and Her Daughters, near Little Salkeld in the Eden Valley, is the county’s great example. Archaeologically, Historic England describes it as the largest irregular stone circle in Cumbria, with an associated cursus and prehistoric enclosure, probably used from the Late Neolithic into the Early Bronze Age. Long Meg herself is a red sandstone outlier, decorated with prehistoric rock art including cup-and-ring marks, spirals and other motifs.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://historicengland.org.uk/listing/the-list/list-entry/1007866" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: historicengland.org.uk">[Historic England]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">historicengland.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on historicengland.org.uk.</span></span></span>

Folklore turns that archaeological grandeur into a moral and magical story. The Cumbria County History Trust records a tradition that the stones are a coven of witches turned to stone by the magician Michael Scott, and that anyone who counts the stones exactly may undo the spell or suffer harm. It also notes that the “uncountable stones” motif produced wildly varying counts in the past, though a modern count can be made without catastrophe.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cumbriacountyhistory.org.uk/long-meg-and-her-daughters-background-little-salkeld" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cumbriacountyhistory.org.uk">[Cumbria County History Trust]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cumbriacountyhistory.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cumbriacountyhistory.org.uk.</span></span></span>

The point is not that the legend explains the monument’s real origin. It does something different: it makes an ancient, difficult site legible to local memory. The circle becomes a warning against Sabbath-breaking, a puzzle that resists ordinary measurement, and a place where deep time is translated into a story about women, magic and punishment. Similar “you cannot count the stones twice” traditions recur at other British stone circles, so Long Meg is both distinctively Cumberland and part of a wider prehistoric-monument folklore pattern.

Castlerigg, east of Keswick, works in a related way. The Gazetteer describes it as one of Britain’s largest and most visually impressive stone circles, standing in a natural amphitheatre of fells.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://gazetteer.org.uk/place/Cumberland" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gazetteer.org.uk">[Gazetteer]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gazetteer.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Gazetteer Cumberland, historiccounty &#124; Gazetteer of British Place Names</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Gazetteer Cumberland, historiccounty &#124; Gazetteer of British Place Names</span></span></span> Its folklore is less famous than Long Meg’s, but its setting explains why Cumberland’s ancient stones feel mythic even before any story is added: the monument is not isolated from landscape, but framed by it.

## King Dunmail and the legend of a lost northern kingdom

Dunmail Raise is one of Cumberland’s clearest examples of history becoming legend. The cairn lies in the pass between Grasmere and Thirlmere, close to the old Cumberland-Westmorland boundary. The Cumbria County History Trust calls it the supposed burial place of Dyfnwal ab Owain, a 10th-century king of Strathclyde, and suggests that the pass and cairn may have marked the southern boundary of that kingdom.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cumbriacountyhistory.org.uk/dunmail-raise-backgound-general" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cumbriacountyhistory.org.uk">[Cumbria County History Trust]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cumbriacountyhistory.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Cumbria County History Trust Dunmail Raise</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cumbria County History Trust Dunmail Raise</span></span></span>

The popular legend is more dramatic. Dunmail becomes the last king of Cumbria, slain after battle against English and Scottish forces; his body is covered by stones, while his crown is hidden in the mountains until the day he may rise again. This is not straightforward documentary history. It is a borderland memory of lost independence, compressed into a cairn beside a major route.

Its power comes from location. Dunmail Raise is not a romantic ruin safely tucked away from modern life: the story attaches to a pass, a road and a boundary. Travellers see a heap of stones and are invited to imagine an older political world beneath it. That is a recurring Cumberland pattern: passes, walls, cairns and roads become places where ordinary movement crosses older thresholds.

## Fairies, boggles and the supernatural small places

Cumberland fairy tradition is harder to summarise than the great stone-circle legends because it survives in fragments: place-names, local anecdotes, antiquarian notes and short collected tales. That is itself important. [Fairies]({{ 'fairies/' | relative_url }}) in Cumberland are less a single “fairy kingdom” than a way of marking certain hills, wells, mounds, roads and old sites as uncanny.

The antiquarian basis is real. The 1901 volume of the *Transactions of the Cumberland and Westmorland Antiquarian and Archaeological Society* includes “Surviving Fairies”, and the same volume was edited by W. G. Collingwood, a major figure in local antiquarian and archaeological culture.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/stream/transactionsofcu01cumb/transactionsofcu01cumb_djvu.txt" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on archive.org.</span></span></span> A later scholarly handlist of Cumbrian fairy place-names also cites Mrs Hodgson’s “On Some Surviving Fairies” in the Society’s 1901 transactions, showing how these small traditions became part of the region’s record.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://centre-for-english-traditional-heritage.org/TraditionToday8/5_Young_Fairy_Placenames.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: centre-for-english-traditional-heritage.org">[centre-for-english-traditional-heritage.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">centre-for-english-traditional-heritage.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on centre-for-english-traditional-heritage.org.</span></span></span>

Cumberland’s fairy material often overlaps with boggles, household spirits and black-dog lore. In northern English usage, names such as boggle, barguest and gytrash can blur: sometimes the being is a dog, sometimes a ghost, sometimes a noisy or shapeshifting thing attached to a lane, tomb or watercourse. This makes Cumberland different from the more courtly fairyland of literary romance. The county’s supernatural beings are often local, abrupt and practical: they frighten travellers, explain noises, guard liminal places, or warn people away from danger.

This is also where modern readers need caution. Many online retellings smooth Cumberland fairies into generic “Celtic” fantasy. The older evidence is usually more local and less tidy. The best way to read it is as a patchwork of beliefs and tale-motifs preserved by antiquarians, not as one unified ancient religion.

## Saints, wells and Christian legend

Cumberland’s sacred folklore is strongly tied to saints, relics and wells. St Bees is the clearest example. The medieval cult of St Bega centres on the west Cumberland coast, and British History Online’s account of St Bees Priory records that the chief relic venerated by the monks was a bracelet associated with St Bega, left behind in Cumberland in the saint’s legend.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.british-history.ac.uk/vch/cumb/vol2/pp178-183" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: british-history.ac.uk">[British History Online]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">british-history.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on british-history.ac.uk.</span></span></span>

Modern local scholarship is careful about the difference between saint, cult and story. The St Bees village account frames Bega as a medieval cult figure associated with an Irish virgin said to have lived in the time of St Hilda and to have spent part of her life at St Bees.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://stbees.org.uk/home/village/published-booklets/st-bega-fact-cult-or-legend/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: stbees.org.uk">[stbees.org.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">stbees.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on stbees.org.uk.</span></span></span> The legend is memorable because it blends coastal arrival, female holiness, flight, relic, landholding and miracle. In some retellings, Bega gains land through a midsummer snow miracle; in others, the focus falls on her bracelet as a sacred object.

Holy wells extend that sacred geography. St Bega’s Well at St Bees is recorded as a holy well, though its medieval history is described as uncertain and fragmentary.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://britishholywells.co.uk/england/cumberland/begaswellstbees/begaswellstbees.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: britishholywells.co.uk">[britishholywells.co.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">britishholywells.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on britishholywells.co.uk.</span></span></span> St Ninian’s Well near Briscoe is recorded in Historic England’s research records as a holy well, protected by a 19th-century arched structure.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.heritagegateway.org.uk/Gateway/Results_Single.aspx?resourceID=19191&amp;uid=11545" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: heritagegateway.org.uk">[Heritage Gateway]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">heritagegateway.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on heritagegateway.org.uk.</span></span></span> Brampton Old Church also preserves a local tradition that St Ninian built a church and holy well within the earthworks of the old Roman fort, dedicating them to St Martin of Tours.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nationalchurchestrust.org/church/brampton-old-church-brampton" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nationalchurchestrust.org">[National Churches Trust]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nationalchurchestrust.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nationalchurchestrust.org.</span></span></span>

These stories show how Christian folklore works in Cumberland. It does not simply replace older uncanny landscapes; it often settles into the same kind of threshold places: springs, ruins, chapels, coastal headlands, Roman remains and old routes. The holy well becomes a point where water, healing, memory and place all meet.

## Haunted Cumberland: Muncaster, Croglin and the Victorian taste for terror

Cumberland has some of the strongest haunted-house material in northern England, but it needs sorting by source and tone. Muncaster Castle, near Ravenglass, is the most famous living example. The castle’s own history page says the Pennington family have recorded evidence of Muncaster as their home since 1208, and it preserves the “Luck of Muncaster” story: Henry VI, sheltered there after the Battle of Hexham in 1464, is said to have left a Venetian glass bowl with a blessing that the family would thrive while it remained unbroken. The bowl is still known as the Luck of Muncaster.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.muncaster.co.uk/castle" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: muncaster.co.uk">[Muncaster Castle]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">muncaster.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Muncaster Castle The Castle &#124; Muncaster Castle &#124; Lake District Family Attraction</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Muncaster Castle The Castle &#124; Muncaster Castle &#124; Lake District Family Attraction</span></span></span>

Muncaster’s folklore also centres on Thomas Skelton, or Tom Fool. The castle says its archives record the purchase of his jester’s motley, and that his portrait hangs there; it also retells the darker tradition that Tom gave dangerous directions across the estuaries to people he disliked.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.muncaster.co.uk/castle" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: muncaster.co.uk">[Muncaster Castle]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">muncaster.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Muncaster Castle The Castle &#124; Muncaster Castle &#124; Lake District Family Attraction</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Muncaster Castle The Castle &#124; Muncaster Castle &#124; Lake District Family Attraction</span></span></span> In modern visitor culture, Tom is tied to ghost stories, tricks and the haunted Tapestry Room. The castle’s own account presents Muncaster as one of Britain’s most haunted castles, with stories of footsteps, a crying child, a singing woman and the Muncaster Boggle or White Lady.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.muncaster.co.uk/castle" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: muncaster.co.uk">[Muncaster Castle]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">muncaster.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Muncaster Castle The Castle &#124; Muncaster Castle &#124; Lake District Family Attraction</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Muncaster Castle The Castle &#124; Muncaster Castle &#124; Lake District Family Attraction</span></span></span>

Croglin is a different case. The “Vampire of Croglin Grange” is famous, but its evidential footing is much shakier. The tale is usually traced to Augustus Hare’s late Victorian autobiographical writing, where he said the story came from Captain Fisher. Later writers have repeatedly noted problems: uncertainty over the exact house, the fit between the described geography and the real Croglin area, and the possibility that the story is Victorian Gothic anecdote rather than old oral tradition.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Croglin_Grange" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Croglin Grange</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Croglin Grange</span></span></span>

That does not make Croglin worthless as folklore. It makes it a good example of how Cumberland entered wider supernatural literature. The story’s afterlife matters: a local name became attached to one of England’s best-known vampire tales, even as historians and folklorists have reasons to treat the account cautiously.

## Black dogs, boggles and warning creatures

Cumberland’s black-dog traditions belong to a wider northern and British pattern rather than to one single named beast. Across England, the black dog is usually a spectral or demonic hound, often large, dark, associated with roads, water, graveyards, storms or death omens. The broader motif includes famous regional names such as Black Shuck in East Anglia and the Barghest in northern England.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_dog_%28folklore%29" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Black dog (folklore</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Black dog (folklore</span></span></span>

For Cumberland and neighbouring northern counties, the key point is not to import Black Shuck wholesale. The local vocabulary is more likely to include barguest, boggle or gytrash-type beings, and the stories often attach to dangerous places: lanes, bridges, barrows, moor edges and watersides. A scholarly article on black-dog folklore notes Cumberland and Westmorland among the regions where these ominous black-dog traditions occur.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.jstor.org/stable/1258857" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: jstor.org">[JSTOR]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">jstor.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Black Dog</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Black Dog</span></span></span>

This kind of folklore often has a practical emotional function. A black dog at a bridge or lonely road turns everyday risk into a memorable warning. It may be a death omen, a ghost, a fairy hound, or a local explanation for fear after dark. Cumberland’s landscape gives such stories plenty of natural stages: fell roads, river crossings, coastal weather, old burial places and border routes.

## Living customs: rough games, fairs and comic faces

Not all Cumberland folklore is supernatural. Some of the county’s strongest traditions are customs still performed in public. Workington’s Uppies and Downies is a mass traditional football game played at Easter. ITV describes the contest as a centuries-old game with no formal rules, in which the Uppies from the top of the town and the Downies from the bottom aim to “hail the ball” at their goal: the Downies at the Prince of Wales dock capstan and the Uppies at the gates of Workington Hall in Curwen Park.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.itv.com/news/border/2024-03-29/the-game-with-no-rules-what-is-uppies-and-downies" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: itv.com">[ITVX]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">itv.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">XThe game with no rules: What is Uppies and Downies?</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">XThe game with no rules: What is Uppies and Downies?</span></span></span>

The game matters because it turns town geography into ritual. “Top” and “bottom” are not abstract team names; they encode social and spatial identity. The ball, the route, the scrum and the goals make Workington itself the playing field. It is folklore as action rather than story.

Egremont Crab Fair is another major Cumberland custom. Its official history highlights Cumberland wrestling, hunting songs, pipe smoking and the World Gurning Championship, in which contestants pull faces through a horse collar or “braffin”.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.egremontcrabfair.com/history.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: egremontcrabfair.com">[egremontcrabfair.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">egremontcrabfair.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Egremont Crab Fair</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Egremont Crab Fair</span></span></span> Guinness World Records notes that the fair has been held annually, apart from interruptions such as wars and COVID-19, since the medieval period, and that gurning has been a major part of it since at least 1852.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://guinnessworldrecords.de/world-records/758640-longest-running-gurning-world-championships" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: guinnessworldrecords.de">[Guinness World Records]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">guinnessworldrecords.de</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Guinness World Records Longest-running gurning world championships</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Guinness World Records Longest-running gurning world championships</span></span></span>

## Old collection, modern retelling and what to trust

Cumberland folklore reaches readers through several different channels, and they should not be treated as equal evidence.

The oldest layer includes medieval religious material, antiquarian county histories, early place-name records, parish tradition and objects such as wells, relics and monuments. These sources can still be biased or legendary, but they show that a belief or story had a place in local memory. The Cumberland and Westmorland Antiquarian and Archaeological Society, whose 1901 transactions included fairy material and were edited by W. G. Collingwood, is part of this collecting world.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/stream/transactionsofcu01cumb/transactionsofcu01cumb_djvu.txt" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on archive.org.</span></span></span>

A second layer is Victorian and Edwardian literary retelling. This is where stories such as Croglin Grange become famous, but also where Gothic taste, tourism and anecdote may reshape local material. A tale may be “from Cumberland” without being a reliable record of long oral tradition.

A third layer is modern heritage and tourism. Muncaster’s ghost sits, Long Meg visitor guides, St Bega walking routes, Lake District monster stories and local-history blogs all keep folklore alive. Some modern retellings are careful; others merge Cumberland, Cumbria, the Lake District and generic British myth into one attractive but blurry package.

The most useful approach is not to strip the magic away, but to ask three questions: where exactly is the story located, how early is it attested, and who is doing the telling? Long Meg’s witch legend, St Bega’s cult, Muncaster’s house traditions and Uppies and Downies all answer those questions differently.

## Why Cumberland’s folklore has such a strong identity

Cumberland’s folklore feels distinctive because it sits at several borders at once. It is English, but deeply northern and shaped by Scottish proximity. It is Lake District, but not only picturesque: it includes industrial ports, market towns, Roman walls, saints’ wells and rough working-town customs. It is ancient in its monuments, medieval in its saints and border memories, Victorian in some of its ghostly fame, and modern in its tourism and public performance.

The county’s best-known traditions all attach story to place in a particularly direct way. Long Meg gives the Eden Valley a witch-circle that can never quite be counted. Dunmail Raise turns a road pass into the grave of a lost king. St Bees makes a coastal priory and well part of a saint’s sacred geography. Muncaster turns family history into luck, haunting and jester legend. Workington and Egremont keep custom alive through bodies in the street: scrambling for a ball, wrestling, singing, gurning and gathering.

That is the heart of Cumberland folklore. It is not a museum shelf of quaint tales. It is a map of how people have made sense of difficult landscapes, old stones, dangerous crossings, sacred springs, powerful families, border memories and communal identity — and how those meanings are still being retold today.
