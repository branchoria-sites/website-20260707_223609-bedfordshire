## Introduction

[Hertfordshire]({{ 'where-hertfordshires-old-stories-still/' | relative_url }})’s witchcraft history is unusually stark because it shows belief changing shape across a century and a half. At Royston in 1606, witchcraft appears through a printed Jacobean accusation story: bones, charms, confession and execution. At Walkern in 1712, Jane Wenham was condemned at Hertford Assizes but saved by legal scepticism and royal pardon. At Tring in 1751, after witchcraft had ceased to be a capital offence, Ruth Osborne was killed by a crowd convinced that ducking could expose guilt. Together, these cases show not a timeless “witch legend”, but a dangerous movement from printed fear, to courtroom doubt, to mob violence when law no longer served popular belief.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.hertsmemories.org.uk/content/herts-history/topics/witchcraft/johane-and-anne-harrison-witches-of-royston" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: hertsmemories.org.uk">[hertsmemories.org.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">hertsmemories.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">johane and anne harrison witches of royston</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Herts MemoriesJohane and Anne Harrison — Witches of Royston6 Feb 2019 — Alternatively, Johane and Anne Harrison may have come entirely fr...</span></span></span>

The Hertfordshire pattern matters for local folklore because these stories are attached to specific county places: Royston, Walkern, Hertford and Tring. They are not just tales of strange powers. They are memories of how neighbours, clergy, magistrates, pamphlet-writers, juries and crowds decided who was frightening, who was vulnerable, and what counted as proof. The most disturbing lesson is that disbelief at the top of the legal system did not immediately remove belief in the parish, the alehouse or the street.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.parliament.uk/about/living-heritage/transformingsociety/private-lives/religion/overview/witchcraft/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: parliament.uk">[parliament.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">parliament.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">UK News Witchcraft</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UK News Witchcraft</span></span></span>

## Why Hertfordshire’s witch cases stand out

Many English counties have witchcraft traditions, but Hertfordshire is distinctive because three of its best-known cases sit at turning points in the history of belief. The Royston case belongs to the early 1600s, when witchcraft was prosecuted in ordinary courts and printed pamphlets helped turn local accusations into public moral drama. The Jane Wenham case came near the end of English witch trials, when judges and pamphleteers openly argued over whether witchcraft accusations were credible. Ruth Osborne’s death at Tring came after Parliament had repealed the old witchcraft laws, making the case less a prosecution than a lynching carried out under the language of old belief.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.parliament.uk/about/living-heritage/transformingsociety/private-lives/religion/overview/witchcraft/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: parliament.uk">[parliament.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">parliament.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">UK News Witchcraft</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UK News Witchcraft</span></span></span>

This is why the topic belongs in Hertfordshire folklore as much as in criminal history. Folklore is not only fairy hills, ghosts and black dogs; it is also the shared belief-world that made certain explanations feel obvious. In these cases, illness, bad luck, quarrels, poverty and reputation were interpreted through witchcraft. The records and pamphlets preserve what people feared, but they also reveal how fragile that fear became when pushed into print, cross-examination or official judgement.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/31511/pg31511-images.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Project Gutenberg eBook of A History of Witchcraft in England from 1558 to 1718, by Wallace Notestein...</span></span></span>

## Royston and early pamphlet witchcraft fear

The Royston case is usually associated with Johane, or Joan, Harrison and her daughter Anne Harrison, two women said to have been convicted and executed in 1606 for murder by witchcraft. The account survives through a London pamphlet culture that thrived on sensational crime and providential warning. Hertfordshire Memories, drawing attention to the local tradition, notes that a 1606 pamphlet told of the conviction and execution of the two Royston women; the same account also cautions that the Harrisons may be hard to separate from the pamphlet-writer’s shaping of the story.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.hertsmemories.org.uk/content/herts-history/topics/witchcraft/johane-and-anne-harrison-witches-of-royston" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: hertsmemories.org.uk">[Herts Memories]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">hertsmemories.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">johane and anne harrison witches of royston</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Herts MemoriesJohane and Anne Harrison — Witches of Royston6 Feb 2019 — Alternatively, Johane and Anne Harrison may have come entirely fr...</span></span></span>

The striking detail attached to Johane Harrison is the alleged discovery of human bones. Wallace Notestein’s classic history of English witchcraft notes that one principal charge against Johanna Harrison of Royston was that officers found in her possession “all the bones due to the Anatomy of man and woman”, after which further charges followed and she was hanged. Notestein places this detail in the context of the Jacobean statute and demonological fears about using body parts in witchcraft.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.globalgreyebooks.com/online-ebooks/wallace-notestein_history-of-witchcraft-in-england_complete-text.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: globalgreyebooks.com">[Global Grey]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">globalgreyebooks.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">wallace notestein history of witchcraft in england complete text</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">wallace notestein history of witchcraft in england complete text</span></span></span>

For a modern reader, the Royston story needs careful handling. It is important because it shows what early seventeenth-century witchcraft accusation looked like: material objects, bodily remains, sudden illness, sinister reputation and confession-like narrative all gathered into a story of hidden malice. But it is also a reminder that printed witch pamphlets were not neutral court transcripts. Notestein describes the witch chapbook as a partisan and sensational form, the early modern equivalent of a quick crime “extra” printed while public appetite was hot.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.globalgreyebooks.com/online-ebooks/wallace-notestein_history-of-witchcraft-in-england_complete-text.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: globalgreyebooks.com">[Global Grey]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">globalgreyebooks.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">wallace notestein history of witchcraft in england complete text</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">wallace notestein history of witchcraft in england complete text</span></span></span>

That does not make the Royston tradition worthless. It makes it revealing. Whether every detail can be verified or not, the case shows what Hertfordshire readers and London buyers were invited to believe in 1606: that a dangerous woman might hide charms or bones in a house, that ordinary sickness might have a malicious human cause, and that execution could restore moral order. In folklore terms, Royston preserves the printed face of fear: local accusation converted into a warning tale.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.hertsmemories.org.uk/content/herts-history/topics/witchcraft/johane-and-anne-harrison-witches-of-royston" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: hertsmemories.org.uk">[Herts Memories]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">hertsmemories.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">johane and anne harrison witches of royston</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Herts MemoriesJohane and Anne Harrison — Witches of Royston6 Feb 2019 — Alternatively, Johane and Anne Harrison may have come entirely fr...</span></span></span>

## Jane Wenham and the collapse of prosecutions

Jane Wenham of Walkern is the central Hertfordshire witchcraft case because it sits on the edge between prosecution and disbelief. The surviving printed record is unusually rich. Francis Bragge’s *A Full and Impartial Account*, published in 1712 and preserved by the Wellcome Collection, presents the proceedings against Wenham, her trial at Hertford before Justice Powell, and the sentence of death passed on 4 March 1711–12. The Wellcome catalogue identifies Bragge as the attributed author and records that its copy was bound with four other pamphlets relating to the same case, showing how quickly the trial became a printed controversy.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://wellcomecollection.org/works/apcd3jek" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wellcomecollection.org">[Wellcome Collection]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wellcomecollection.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wellcomecollection.org.</span></span></span>

The story began in recognisably local terms. Wenham was a poor widow from Walkern who had long carried suspicion. Notestein’s account says she had been suspected by a neighbouring farmer, and that when a servant began behaving strangely he blamed Wenham. Wenham herself first appealed to Sir Henry Chauncy, a justice of the peace, after being accused; the accuser was fined only a shilling, and she was urged by the clergyman to live more peaceably. This was the sort of village quarrel that could have ended as slander, but instead widened into a witchcraft case.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/31511/pg31511-images.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Project Gutenberg eBook of A History of Witchcraft in England from 1558 to 1718, by Wallace Notestein...</span></span></span>

The accusations against Wenham included Anne Thorne, a servant in the rector’s household, whose strange behaviour became part of the evidence. Notestein summarises the story of Thorne being found partly undressed, carrying sticks and speaking of an encounter with a muffled old woman on Hackney Lane. The detail is vivid, but it also shows how ordinary distress, illness or performance could be read through the expected script of bewitchment: compulsion, wandering, mysterious objects and the sudden appearance of the suspected woman.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/31511/pg31511-images.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Project Gutenberg eBook of A History of Witchcraft in England from 1558 to 1718, by Wallace Notestein...</span></span></span>

What makes the Wenham case famous is not only the accusation, but the legal reaction. She was found guilty by the jury, yet Justice Powell was sceptical. Later summaries of the case often remember his dry response to the claim that she could fly: flying itself was not a crime. The conviction was not carried out; Queen Anne granted a pardon. Parliament’s own historical account notes that the final English witch trials were clustered in the early eighteenth century and that in 1736 Parliament repealed the laws against witchcraft, replacing prosecution of witches with penalties for people who claimed magical powers.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jane_Wenham_%28alleged_witch%29" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Jane Wenham (alleged witch</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Jane Wenham (alleged witch</span></span></span>

The pamphlet war around Wenham is essential to understanding Hertfordshire’s place in witchcraft history. Bragge’s pamphlet defended the seriousness of the accusation, while other writers attacked the case as credulous. Cornell’s Digital Witchcraft Collection preserves *The Case of the Hertfordshire Witchcraft Consider’d*, a 1712 reply examining Bragge’s *Full and Impartial Account*. The very title shows that by 1712 the argument was no longer just “is this woman a witch?” but “what kind of evidence should a reasonable society accept?”<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://digital.library.cornell.edu/catalog/witchcraft032" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: digital.library.cornell.edu">[Cornell Digital Library]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">digital.library.cornell.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cornell.edu.</span></span></span>

For folklore readers, Jane Wenham matters because she stands at the point where elite law and local belief visibly part company. Walkern villagers could still imagine witchcraft as a real explanation for harm. A jury could still convict. But a judge, pamphlet critics and finally the Crown could halt the machinery of death. The old belief had not vanished, but it had become contestable in public.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.globalgreyebooks.com/online-ebooks/wallace-notestein_history-of-witchcraft-in-england_complete-text.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: globalgreyebooks.com">[Global Grey]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">globalgreyebooks.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">wallace notestein history of witchcraft in england complete text</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">wallace notestein history of witchcraft in england complete text</span></span></span>

## Ruth Osborne, mob violence and official warning

Ruth Osborne’s death at Tring in 1751 is the bleakest Hertfordshire case because it happened after the legal world had moved on. The Witchcraft Act of 1735, passed in 1736, repealed the old witchcraft laws and treated claims to magical power as fraud or pretence rather than proof of a capital supernatural crime. Parliament’s historical summary describes the change plainly: the old laws were repealed, while fines or imprisonment applied to people claiming magical powers.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.parliament.uk/about/living-heritage/transformingsociety/private-lives/religion/overview/witchcraft/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: parliament.uk">[Parliament UK News]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">parliament.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">UK News Witchcraft</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UK News Witchcraft</span></span></span>

That legal shift did not protect Ruth Osborne. The Library of Congress legal history blog uses her death as a late and brutal example of “swimming” or ducking an accused witch. It notes that by the end of the seventeenth century courts increasingly treated witch-swimming as assault, and that if the victim drowned it was murder. Osborne died in 1751, sixteen years after the 1735 Act, when Thomas Colley violently drowned her while carrying out the test before a crowd said to number around 5,000.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://blogs.loc.gov/law/2022/02/swimming-a-witch-evidence-in-17th-century-english-witchcraft-trials/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: blogs.loc.gov">[The Library of Congress]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">blogs.loc.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on loc.gov.</span></span></span>

The local setting was Tring and nearby Long Marston, with John and Ruth Osborne accused after misfortune and illness were read as witchcraft. A contemporary trial publication, catalogued by Open Library, gives the legal frame: *The tryal of Thomas Colley* at Hertford Assizes on 30 July 1751 for the murder of Ruth Osborne, wife of John Osborne of Tring, by ducking her in Marlston-Mere under the supposition that she was a witch, until she was suffocated with mud and water.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://openlibrary.org/books/OL59830695M/The_tryal_of_Thomas_Colley" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: openlibrary.org">[Open Library]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">openlibrary.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on openlibrary.org.</span></span></span>

The horror of the case lies in the way crowd belief replaced court procedure. The ducking was not a quaint survival of rustic custom. It was public violence dressed as proof. The supposed test depended on the old belief that water would reject a witch, but in practice it gave a crowd permission to strip, bind, humiliate and endanger an elderly woman. The Library of Congress account is blunt that witch-swimming was often a thinly concealed act of mob justice, and Osborne’s death shows exactly why authorities had come to reject it.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://blogs.loc.gov/law/2022/02/swimming-a-witch-evidence-in-17th-century-english-witchcraft-trials/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: blogs.loc.gov">[The Library of Congress]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">blogs.loc.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on loc.gov.</span></span></span>

Colley was tried, convicted and hanged. The official response was meant to warn the county that belief was no defence to murder. Open Library’s catalogue record identifies the trial as taking place at Hertford Assizes, while the Library of Congress summary records that Colley was hanged for Osborne’s murder after the assize proceedings. Hertfordshire Memories also preserves local material relating to Colley’s confession, showing how the event entered county memory as both a crime record and a witchcraft story.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://openlibrary.org/books/OL59830695M/The_tryal_of_Thomas_Colley" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: openlibrary.org">[openlibrary.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">openlibrary.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on openlibrary.org.</span></span></span>

## What these cases reveal about mob belief

The three Hertfordshire cases are often linked because they are “witch cases”, but their deeper connection is the changing location of authority. In Royston, the pamphlet presents accusation and execution as a grim moral lesson. In Walkern and Hertford, the court still produces a guilty verdict but the judge and Crown interrupt the punishment. In Tring, the crowd acts as if it can supply its own trial, its own test and its own sentence.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.globalgreyebooks.com/online-ebooks/wallace-notestein_history-of-witchcraft-in-england_complete-text.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: globalgreyebooks.com">[globalgreyebooks.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">globalgreyebooks.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">wallace notestein history of witchcraft in england complete text</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">wallace notestein history of witchcraft in england complete text</span></span></span>

This progression helps explain why mob belief is so dangerous. A belief does not need official approval to harm someone. After 1736, English law no longer treated witchcraft as an executable crime, but people could still believe that a neighbour’s glance, mutter, curse or reputation had caused sickness, cattle disease or bad luck. When formal law refused to validate that fear, some believers sought older communal “tests” instead. Ruth Osborne’s death is therefore not a survival of medieval justice; it is a collision between modernising law and local certainty.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.parliament.uk/about/living-heritage/transformingsociety/private-lives/religion/overview/witchcraft/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: parliament.uk">[Parliament UK News]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">parliament.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">UK News Witchcraft</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UK News Witchcraft</span></span></span>

The cases also show why accused witches were so often socially vulnerable. Parliament’s historical account notes that formal accusations usually fell on poor, elderly women, especially in the peak period of English witch trials. Wenham and Osborne fit that broader pattern: women whose age, poverty, dependence or difficult reputation made them easy to imagine as dangerous and hard for them to defend themselves.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.parliament.uk/about/living-heritage/transformingsociety/private-lives/religion/overview/witchcraft/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: parliament.uk">[Parliament UK News]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">parliament.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">UK News Witchcraft</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UK News Witchcraft</span></span></span>

For Hertfordshire identity, this is uncomfortable but important heritage. The county’s witchcraft stories should not be retold simply as spooky local colour. Royston, Walkern and Tring preserve evidence of how fear works in communities: it gathers around misfortune, names a culprit, finds “proof” after suspicion has already formed, and can survive even when the law withdraws support. That is why these stories still matter on a folklore map of Hertfordshire. They are legends with documents attached, and documents with human suffering inside them.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.hertsmemories.org.uk/content/herts-history/topics/witchcraft/johane-and-anne-harrison-witches-of-royston" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: hertsmemories.org.uk">[hertsmemories.org.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">hertsmemories.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">johane and anne harrison witches of royston</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Herts MemoriesJohane and Anne Harrison — Witches of Royston6 Feb 2019 — Alternatively, Johane and Anne Harrison may have come entirely fr...</span></span></span>

## How the stories are retold today

Modern retellings usually frame these cases less as proof of witchcraft and more as warnings about accusation. Hertfordshire Memories groups Johane and Anne Harrison, Jane Wenham and Ruth Osborne within local witchcraft history, helping place the cases in county memory rather than leaving them as scattered legal curiosities. That local heritage framing is useful because it keeps the place names visible: Royston, Walkern, Hertford and Tring are part of the story’s meaning.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.hertsmemories.org.uk/content/category/herts-history/topics/witchcraft" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: hertsmemories.org.uk">[Herts Memories]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">hertsmemories.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on hertsmemories.org.uk.</span></span></span>

Jane Wenham has also moved into theatre and cultural reinterpretation. Modern stage treatments, including Rebecca Lenkiewicz’s *Jane Wenham: The Witch of Walkern*, have treated the case as a story about gender, poverty, belief and power rather than as a simple supernatural tale. Reviews and theatre materials describe Wenham as an impoverished older widow and healer figure caught in a late witchcraft panic, which reflects the modern tendency to recover the accused person’s humanity rather than repeat the accusers’ certainty.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.bsecs.org.uk/criticks-reviews/jane-wenham-the-witch-of-walkern/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: bsecs.org.uk">[BSECS]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">bsecs.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">jane wenham the witch of walkern</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">jane wenham the witch of walkern</span></span></span>

Ruth Osborne is now often described as the “last witch” in England, but that phrase needs care. She was not legally executed as a witch; she was murdered by a mob because people believed she was one. That distinction is the whole point. Calling her the “last witch” may keep the story memorable, but the more accurate memory is sharper: in Hertfordshire, long after official witch prosecution had collapsed, popular belief still proved deadly.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.parliament.uk/about/living-heritage/transformingsociety/private-lives/religion/overview/witchcraft/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: parliament.uk">[parliament.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">parliament.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">UK News Witchcraft</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UK News Witchcraft</span></span></span>

      var active = button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button') === marketId;
      button.setAttribute('aria-pressed', active ? 'true' : 'false');
    });
    section.setAttribute('data-ebay-selected-market', marketId);
    if (persist) {
      try { if (window.localStorage) localStorage.setItem('phoenix-ebay-market', marketId); } catch (err) {}
    }
    closeMenu(section);
  }
  sections.forEach(function (section) {
    if (section.getAttribute('data-ebay-localized-init') === '1') return;
    section.setAttribute('data-ebay-localized-init', '1');
    var available = availableMarkets(section);
    var marketId = defaultMarket;
    if (window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation && window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation.inferMarket) {
      marketId = window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation.inferMarket(available, {
        storageKey: 'phoenix-ebay-market',
        defaultMarket: defaultMarket
      });
    } else if (available.indexOf(defaultMarket)< 0) {
      marketId = available[0] || defaultMarket;
    }
    var select = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-select]');
    if (select) {
      select.addEventListener('change', function () {
        applyMarket(section, select.value, true);
      });
    }
    var trigger = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-trigger]');
    var menu = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-menu]');
    if (trigger && menu) {
      trigger.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
        event.stopPropagation();
        var expanded = trigger.getAttribute('aria-expanded') === 'true';
        menu.hidden = expanded;
        trigger.setAttribute('aria-expanded', expanded ? 'false' : 'true');
        var picker = trigger.closest('.fr-ebay-market-picker');
        if (picker) picker.classList.toggle('fr-ebay-market-picker--open', !expanded);
      });
      Array.prototype.slice.call(menu.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-option]')).forEach(function (button) {
        button.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
          event.stopPropagation();
          applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-option'), true);
        });
      });
      document.addEventListener('click', function () { closeMenu(section); });
    }
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      button.addEventListener('click', function () {
        applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button'), true);
      });
    });
    applyMarket(section, marketId, false);
  });
