## Introduction

Derry’s holy wells matter because they make St Columba’s memory physical. In this tradition, the city is not only a walled settlement on the Foyle, but a sacred landscape of oak, stone, water and pilgrimage. St Columb’s Well, near the Long Tower area below the city walls, is linked to a baptism miracle in which the saint makes water spring from stone when no water can be found. On 9 June, St Columba’s feast day, the well is blessed, oak leaves are worn or used in decoration, and some people take water home for devotion or healing tradition.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.colmcille.org/en/map-marker/4-5-st-columbs-well/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: colmcille.org">[colmcille.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">colmcille.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">According to Manus O&#x27;Donnell&#x27;s Life, a child was brought to Colmcille to be baptised but there was...</span></span></span>

The tradition is old in shape, but layered in evidence. It reaches back through medieval hagiography, early Christian place-memory, seventeenth-century maps, nineteenth-century urban change and present-day local devotion. Its importance for [County Londonderry]({{ 'county-londonderry/' | relative_url }}) lies in that layering: Derry’s Columban story is not locked inside a church history book, but marked into steps, streets, wells, stones, feast-day customs and the city’s oak-grove name.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://thederrywalls.com/tag/columcille/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: thederrywalls.com">[thederrywalls.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">thederrywalls.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Derry Wallscolumcille – The Derry Walls10 Jun 2017 — Here are some photographs which illustrate this. ## The baptism miracle and the spring from stone

The central legend of St Columb’s Well is simple and memorable. According to the tradition preserved on Slí Cholmcille, drawing on Manus O’Donnell’s sixteenth-century Life of Colum Cille, a child was brought to Columba for baptism while he was in Derry. There was no water nearby, so the saint made the sign of the cross over a stone and water came out of it.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.colmcille.org/en/map-marker/4-5-st-columbs-well/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: colmcille.org">[colmcille.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">colmcille.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">According to Manus O&#x27;Donnell&#x27;s Life, a child was brought to Colmcille to be baptised but there was...</span></span></span>

That story does several things at once. It explains why this particular water source is sacred; it connects the well to baptism, not merely to general healing; and it gives the landscape the character of a witness. The stone is not just a prop in the story. It is the point where saintly action, Christian ritual and local topography meet.

For folklore readers, the well is especially interesting because it belongs to a common pattern in saintly tradition: a holy person confronts a practical need, performs a sign, and leaves behind a place that can be visited. The story does not ask the reader simply to admire Columba from a distance. It says that the saint’s presence changed Derry’s ground. That is why the well can carry meaning even for people who approach it as heritage rather than as devotional practice.

The source trail also matters. Manus O’Donnell was not a modern tourist writer repeating a loose anecdote; he was a major Gaelic lord of the north-west who completed a Life of Colum Cille in 1532. Modern summaries of the text describe it as a window into the beliefs and cultural interests of Gaelic Ireland before the Reformation and Tudor conquest.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://triasthaumaturga.blogspot.com/2012/06/manus-odonnells-life-of-colum-cille.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: triasthaumaturga.blogspot.com">[triasthaumaturga.blogspot.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">triasthaumaturga.blogspot.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">manus odonnells life of colum cille</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">manus odonnells life of colum cille</span></span></span> This does not prove that the miracle happened as described, but it shows that the story belongs to a deep Columban literary and devotional tradition, not merely to recent local branding.

## Oak leaves, feast-day customs and pilgrimage

St Columb’s Well is still tied to 9 June, the feast day of St Columba. The modern custom described by Slí Cholmcille includes a procession from Long Tower Church down to the well, the blessing of the well, people filling bottles with water, and the use of oak leaves as decoration and personal tokens. The water is traditionally said to cure disease, particularly eye complaints.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.colmcille.org/en/map-marker/4-5-st-columbs-well/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: colmcille.org">[colmcille.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">colmcille.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">According to Manus O&#x27;Donnell&#x27;s Life, a child was brought to Colmcille to be baptised but there was...</span></span></span>

The oak leaves are not decorative in a random way. Derry’s name comes from the Irish word for an oak grove, and Slí Cholmcille describes early Derry as a wooded island or hill surrounded by water and wetland. National Churches Trust likewise explains Derry’s name through the oak-grove meaning and presents St Columba’s foundation as the beginning of Derry’s long Christian identity.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.colmcille.org/en/map-marker-route/derry/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: colmcille.org">[colmcille.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">colmcille.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Derry – Slí Cholmcille, St Columba Way</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Derry, from the Irish Doire, means &#x27;oak grove&#x27;, and in the first days of settlement here the...Read more...</span></span></span>

This gives the feast-day custom a local force that a generic saint’s procession would not have. The oak leaf links three things:

* **The place-name:** Derry as an oak-grove place.<span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://othemts.wordpress.com/2007/06/09/saint-columba/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: othemts.wordpress.com">[saint columba]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">othemts.wordpress.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">saint columba</span></span></span>:** Columba or Columcille as Derry’s patron and founding religious figure.
* **The living custom:** pilgrims and local worshippers marking the feast with leaves, water and procession.

The result is a compact piece of public folklore. A person wearing an oak leaf on 9 June is not retelling the whole Life of Columba, but they are carrying a small sign of it. The custom turns the city’s name into something visible on the body.

The annual observance has also adapted to modern conditions. During the 2020 pandemic period, the Diocese of Derry reported that Mass for St Columba’s feast would be broadcast from Long Tower and that water from St Columb’s Well would be blessed and made available afterwards.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.derrydiocese.org/images/2020/05/the-net-june-2020.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: derrydiocese.org">[Diocese of Derry]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">derrydiocese.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on derrydiocese.org.</span></span></span> That small detail shows how the tradition survives by changing form: the well remains central, but access, worship and distribution can be adjusted for contemporary circumstances.

## Why the well belongs to Derry’s sacred landscape

St Columb’s Well is not an isolated curiosity. It sits within a dense sacred geography around Long Tower, Áras Cholmcille, the old city walls, St Columb’s Cathedral, St Augustine’s and the Foyle-side approaches to medieval Derry. Slí Cholmcille places the well on a route through Derry that begins with the medieval port area and follows Columban memory through churches, streets and surviving markers.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.colmcille.org/en/map-marker/4-5-st-columbs-well/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: colmcille.org">[colmcille.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">colmcille.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">According to Manus O&#x27;Donnell&#x27;s Life, a child was brought to Colmcille to be baptised but there was...</span></span></span>

The Long Tower connection is especially important. Visit Derry describes Áras Cholmcille, the St Columba Heritage Centre, as promoting the life of Derry’s patron saint and being set on the site of the monastery associated with 546 AD.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://visitderrypass.com/product/saint-columba-heritage-centre/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: visitderrypass.com">[Visit Derry Pass]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">visitderrypass.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on visitderrypass.com.</span></span></span> The National Churches Trust describes Long Tower Church as a living link between past and present, with a bullaun stone in the church grounds connected with St Columba’s Holy Well.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nationalchurchestrust.org/church/derry-londonderry-long-tower-st-columba" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nationalchurchestrust.org">[National Churches Trust]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nationalchurchestrust.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Churches TrustDerry/Londonderry Long Tower St ColumbaA unique feature of the church grounds is a bullaun stone, now part of the...</span></span></span>

This is where folklore and urban history overlap. Derry’s later identity is often dominated by the seventeenth-century walls and the story of siege, Plantation and political naming. The wells pull the reader’s eye to an older layer. They suggest a Derry before the walls: a hill, an oak grove, a monastic foundation, paths to water, and devotional movement through the landscape.

That older layer is not erased by the walled city. It survives alongside it. A visitor can pass through Butcher’s Gate, walk near Magazine Street, find the steps by Áras Cholmcille and reach the well among a row of houses called St Columb’s Wells.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.colmcille.org/en/map-marker/4-5-st-columbs-well/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: colmcille.org">[colmcille.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">colmcille.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">According to Manus O&#x27;Donnell&#x27;s Life, a child was brought to Colmcille to be baptised but there was...</span></span></span> The route itself becomes part of the meaning. Sacred geography here is not only a matter of ancient ruins; it is also the way old holy places remain tucked into modern streets.

## Lost wells, bullaun stones and sacred geography

One reason the tradition is richer than a single spring is that medieval Derry appears to have remembered more than one well at this site. Slí Cholmcille states that in medieval times there were three wells here, dedicated to St Colmcille, St Adhamhnán and St Martin of Tours.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.colmcille.org/en/map-marker/4-5-st-columbs-well/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: colmcille.org">[colmcille.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">colmcille.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">According to Manus O&#x27;Donnell&#x27;s Life, a child was brought to Colmcille to be baptised but there was...</span></span></span> St Adhamhnán, or Adomnán, was a successor of Columba as abbot of Iona and his famous biographer, so his presence in the group reinforces the Columban character of the place. St Martin of Tours adds a wider European Christian link, suggesting that Derry’s sacred landscape was local but not provincial.

The wells were known early enough to appear in historical mapping. The Derry Walls heritage site notes that St Columb’s Wells were marked on Docwra’s 1600 map and Neville’s 1689 map.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://thederrywalls.com/tag/columcille/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: thederrywalls.com">[The Derry Walls]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">thederrywalls.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Derry Wallscolumcille – The Derry Walls10 Jun 2017 — Here are some photographs which illustrate this. Map from 1600 showing St Columb...</span></span></span> That is valuable evidence because it shows the wells were not invented as a modern heritage feature. They were sufficiently recognised to be plotted in the city’s early modern landscape.

The bullaun stone deepens the picture. A bullaun is a stone with one or more hollowed basins, often associated in Ireland with early Christian sites, water, blessing and local healing traditions. A Department for Communities heritage page on another Northern Irish bullaun describes such stones as large boulders with water-filled hollows, often regarded as fonts and indicating early Christian use of a site.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.communities-ni.gov.uk/heritage-sites/ballintemple-bullaun-stone" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: communities-ni.gov.uk">[Department for Communities]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">communities-ni.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Department for Communities Ballintemple Bullaun Stone</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Department for Communities Ballintemple Bullaun Stone</span></span></span> In Derry, National Churches Trust states that a bullaun stone from close to St Columba’s Holy Well now forms part of the grotto at Long Tower Church.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nationalchurchestrust.org/explore/story/walled-city" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nationalchurchestrust.org">[National Churches Trust]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nationalchurchestrust.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nationalchurchestrust.org.</span></span></span>

That movement from the well area to the church grounds is significant. It shows how sacred objects can be preserved by relocation, but also how their meaning changes when their original setting is altered. At the well, the stone belonged to a water-place in the open urban landscape. At Long Tower, it becomes part of a curated devotional and heritage environment. The tradition survives, but in a more managed form.

There is also a note of loss. The decorative pump now associated with the well dates from 1897, when it served as the main water supply for houses on the slope below the city walls.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.colmcille.org/en/map-marker/4-5-st-columbs-well/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: colmcille.org">[colmcille.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">colmcille.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">According to Manus O&#x27;Donnell&#x27;s Life, a child was brought to Colmcille to be baptised but there was...</span></span></span> This is a useful reminder that holy wells were not always neatly separated from ordinary life. Sacred water could also be practical water. Urban improvement, road changes, housing and public health all affected how wells were used, seen and remembered.

## What is old, what is preserved, and what is retold today?

The St Columb’s Well tradition is best understood as a layered inheritance rather than a single unchanged custom from the sixth century. Several layers can be separated without stripping away the story’s power.

The oldest layer is the Columban place-memory: Derry as an oak-grove monastic settlement associated with St Columba. This is strongly embedded in church, heritage and route-based accounts of the city.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nationalchurchestrust.org/explore/story/walled-city" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nationalchurchestrust.org">[National Churches Trust]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nationalchurchestrust.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nationalchurchestrust.org.</span></span></span>

The literary-devotional layer is the miracle story preserved through Manus O’Donnell’s Life of Colum Cille. It gives the well a narrative origin and ties it to baptismal water.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.colmcille.org/en/map-marker/4-5-st-columbs-well/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: colmcille.org">[colmcille.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">colmcille.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">According to Manus O&#x27;Donnell&#x27;s Life, a child was brought to Colmcille to be baptised but there was...</span></span></span>

The mapped and antiquarian layer is the early modern visibility of St Columb’s Wells, including their appearance on maps from 1600 and 1689, and later references to the bullaun stone and changing well-site.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://thederrywalls.com/tag/columcille/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: thederrywalls.com">[The Derry Walls]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">thederrywalls.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Derry Wallscolumcille – The Derry Walls10 Jun 2017 — Here are some photographs which illustrate this. The living layer is the annual feast-day practice: procession, blessing, oak leaves, water taken home and devotional retelling. This is the part most obviously alive today, especially around Long Tower and the St Columba Heritage Centre.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.colmcille.org/en/map-marker/4-5-st-columbs-well/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: colmcille.org">[colmcille.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">colmcille.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">According to Manus O&#x27;Donnell&#x27;s Life, a child was brought to Colmcille to be baptised but there was...</span></span></span>

Holding these layers together prevents two common mistakes. One mistake is to flatten the well into a tourist stop with a charming story attached. The other is to treat every present-day detail as if it can be projected unchanged back to Columba’s lifetime. The stronger reading is more interesting: Derry’s well tradition has been repeatedly renewed, mapped, moved, blessed, interpreted and visited.

## Why this matters within County Londonderry folklore

Within County Londonderry’s wider folklore, St Columb’s Well shows how saintly tradition can do the work that fairy hills, standing stones or haunted houses do elsewhere: it gathers memory around a visible place. The difference is that the well’s story is not primarily frightening or uncanny. It is about blessing, healing, founding and belonging.

It also helps explain why Derry’s folklore cannot be separated from the city’s names and landscapes. The oak grove is not just etymology. The feast-day oak leaves make the name wearable. The spring from stone is not just miracle literature. The surviving well makes the story visitable. The bullaun stone is not just an archaeological object. Its relocation to Long Tower shows how a community preserves a sacred fragment when the surrounding urban world changes.

For a county-level folklore project, that is the real value of St Columba’s Wells. They join the early Christian north-west, the River Foyle city, medieval pilgrimage, post-Reformation church life, modern heritage trails and local feast-day practice into one compact landscape. In County Londonderry, the sacred is often read through walls, churches and contested names; at St Columb’s Well, it is also read through water, oak leaves and a stone said to have answered a saint’s need.
