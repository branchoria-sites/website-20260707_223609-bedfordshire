## Introduction

Highland [Perthshire]({{ 'where-perthshires-strange-stories-meet/' | relative_url }})’s Gaelic tale tradition is unusually valuable because it survives not as a vague memory of “old Highland stories”, but as a named body of collected evidence: more than 240 tales and songs written down in and around Atholl by Lady Evelyn Stewart Murray in 1891. The material matters for Perthshire folklore because it catches a local storytelling culture at the point where everyday Gaelic was already retreating in the county. It also preserves people, places and speech patterns that would otherwise be far harder to trace: estate workers, tenants, women narrators, parish locations, fairy stories, ghost stories, wonder tales, songs and local beliefs. The best way to read these tales is not as tourist legend, but as a late nineteenth-century record of living oral tradition, later edited and translated for modern readers.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gaelicbooks.org/explore-the-shop/non-fiction/academic/tales-from-highland-perthshire?lang=en" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gaelicbooks.org">[Comhairle nan Leabhraichean]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gaelicbooks.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Comhairle nan Leabhraichean Tales From Highland Perthshire</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Comhairle nan LeabhraicheanTales From Highland Perthshire - Gaelic Books CouncilOver 240 Perthshire Gaelic folktales and songs collected...</span></span></span>

## Why Atholl became the key Perthshire case

Atholl gives this topic its centre of gravity. It is a historical Highland district forming the northern part of Perthshire, and its position helps explain why it held on to Gaelic tale material longer than many more anglicised parts of the county. Wikishire describes Atholl as a large Highland district in northern Perthshire, bordering other districts including Breadalbane and Strathearn, while its wider Perthshire profile places the county between Strathmore, Drumochter, Rannoch Moor, Ben Lui and Aberfoyle. That mixed geography matters: Highland Perthshire was not an isolated island of tradition, but part of a county where upland Gaelic-speaking communities, estate life, old roads and Lowland-facing market towns all overlapped.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://wikishire.co.uk/wiki/Atholl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wikishire.co.uk">[Wikishire]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wikishire.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Atholl30 Aug 2011 — Atholl or Athole (Gaelic: Athall and Old Gaelic Athfhotla) is a large historical highland district which for...</span></span></span>

The Gaelic tales collected around Atholl therefore sit at a county threshold. They are Perthshire stories, but they are also Highland stories shaped by glens, farms, service households, churchyards, moorland, cattle, kinship and seasonal labour. This makes them different from a single spectacular legend attached to one castle or loch. They are a record of a storytelling environment: the kind of tradition in which fairy encounters, comic tricksters, dangerous stepmothers, enchanted animals, graveyard warnings and local place-memory could all live side by side.

That is why the collection is so important for a Perthshire folklore map. Without it, Highland Perthshire might be represented mainly by later scenic myths: fairy mountains, haunted castles and romantic Highland atmosphere. With it, readers can see a deeper layer: stories told by named or partly named local people, written down in a particular year, in a local form of Gaelic, and preserved in manuscript before being brought into print much later.

## Lady Evelyn Stewart Murray’s Atholl collecting

Lady Evelyn Stewart Murray was not an outside tourist passing through Highland Perthshire in search of picturesque material. She was the daughter of the 7th Duke of Atholl, born into the world of Blair Castle, and she learned Gaelic in youth. The National Library of Scotland catalogue describes her as having collected 240 Gaelic folk tales in Perthshire, with manuscripts now held at Blair Castle, and also notes related notebooks of Gaelic songs and other material.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://manuscripts.nls.uk/repositories/2/resources/21979" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: manuscripts.nls.uk">[manuscripts.nls.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">manuscripts.nls.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Papers of Lady Evelyn Stewart-Murray (1868-1940).Having learned Gaelic in her youth, she collected 240 Gaelic folk tales in Perthshire (t...</span></span></span>

The key fieldwork year was 1891. The Gaelic Books Council’s entry for *Tales from Highland Perthshire* describes the published volume as containing more than 240 Perthshire Gaelic folktales and songs collected and recorded in 1891 by Lady Evelyn Stewart Murray, with facing-page English translations and notes on dialect, people and area. The modern edition, translated and edited by Sylvia Robertson and Tony Dilworth, was published by the Scottish Gaelic Texts Society in 2009, turning a long-archived manuscript corpus into a source usable by readers beyond specialist manuscript rooms.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gaelicbooks.org/product/tales-from-highland-perthshire?add-to-cart=5326&amp;lang=en" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gaelicbooks.org">[Comhairle nan Leabhraichean]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gaelicbooks.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Comhairle nan Leabhraichean Tales From Highland Perthshire</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Comhairle nan Leabhraichean Tales From Highland Perthshire</span></span></span>

Her collecting also has a human tension that matters. A History Press account says she pursued Gaelic study by correspondence with Professor MacKinnon of Edinburgh University, then between February and December 1891 visited staff and tenants on her father’s Perthshire estate, recording stories and songs by hand in diaries. The same account stresses that publication was intended but repeatedly delayed until the 2009 edition finally appeared.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://thehistorypress.co.uk/article/finding-the-folk-tales/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: thehistorypress.co.uk">[The History Press]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">thehistorypress.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">finding the folk tales</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">finding the folk tales</span></span></span>

This social setting complicates the evidence in a useful way. Murray was an aristocratic collector with unusual access to estate households, but the oral knowledge she preserved came largely from the people around that estate world. The archive therefore records both privilege and dependence: a young woman of rank had the education, leisure and paper to write the material down, while local speakers carried the stories, songs and dialect that made the collection worth preserving.

## What kinds of tales were preserved?

The Atholl collection is not just a handful of famous fairy legends. It crosses several kinds of traditional narrative, which is why it is so useful for understanding Perthshire’s wider story culture. A scholarly companion to Scottish traditional literature describes Murray’s 1891 collecting as including Atholl tales, historical tales from other parts of Perthshire, fairy lore, brownies, witches and wizards, songs, ghost stories and other material.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://dokumen.pub/download/the-edinburgh-companion-to-scottish-traditional-literatures-9780748645411.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: dokumen.pub">[dokumen.pub]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">dokumen.pub</span><span class="citation-popover-title">the edinburgh companion to scottish traditional literatures 9780748645411</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">the edinburgh companion to scottish traditional literatures 9780748645411</span></span></span>

One striking feature is that the tales can be read at two levels. At the local level, they belong to Perthshire places and narrators: Foss, Dull, Strathtay, Logierait and other settings appear in catalogue records. At the international folklore level, some stories match tale types found across Europe and beyond. Hidden Heritages, a project connected with major academic partners and archive data, indexes examples from Murray’s material by Aarne-Thompson tale type, a classification system used to compare recurring tale patterns. One Foss example from Dull parish is listed as “The Robber Bridegroom”, collected by Lady Evelyn Stewart Murray from Mrs Campbell in 1891.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.hiddenheritages.ai/en/sct/497" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: hiddenheritages.ai">[Decoding Hidden Heritages]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">hiddenheritages.ai</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on hiddenheritages.ai.</span></span></span>

That double identity is important. A tale could be both intensely local and widely travelled. A story told in Highland Perthshire might share a plot-shape with stories elsewhere, but its language, narrator, setting, humour and moral emphasis made it part of Perthshire’s own oral world. For general readers, this is one of the most rewarding ways to approach the material: not by asking whether a story was “original” to Perthshire, but by asking how Perthshire speakers made inherited story-patterns their own.

The range also prevents a narrow idea of Gaelic folklore. Murray’s collection includes supernatural material, but it is not only about fairies. Hidden Heritages records a 1891 manuscript from Foss in Dull parish called “Skull invited to dinner”, indexed as “The Offended Skull”, with Mrs Campbell as narrator; another Perthshire item is “The Twining Branches”, narrated by Annie MacDonald; another is “The Ogre Blinded”, collected from John Robertson. These examples show a living mix of death lore, lovers’ legend, wonder tale and comic danger rather than a single “Celtic myth” category.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.hiddenheritages.ai/en/sct/223" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: hiddenheritages.ai">[Decoding Hidden Heritages]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">hiddenheritages.ai</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on hiddenheritages.ai.</span></span></span>

## Why language and dialect matter

The collection’s biggest value may be linguistic as well as folkloric. By the late nineteenth century, Perthshire Gaelic was under pressure, and the local dialect was disappearing from daily life. The University of Edinburgh’s heritage collections page identifies the Lady Evelyn Stewart Murray manuscripts as nineteenth-century tales in Perthshire Gaelic, held among important manuscript collections connected with Scottish tradition.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://library.ed.ac.uk/heritage-collections/collections-and-search/archives/archives/manuscripts-collections/manuscript-collection" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: library.ed.ac.uk">[Library]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">library.ed.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">File Manuscript Collection &#124; Library</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">File Manuscript Collection &#124; Library</span></span></span>

This matters because folklore is changed by translation. A plot summary can tell readers what happens in a story, but it cannot fully preserve local idiom, rhythm, word choice, humour or the social feel of a performance. The 2009 edition’s facing-page translations are therefore especially useful: they allow non-Gaelic readers to follow the tales while keeping the Gaelic text visible as evidence, not as an invisible source behind a smooth English retelling.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gaelicbooks.org/explore-the-shop/non-fiction/academic/tales-from-highland-perthshire?lang=en" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gaelicbooks.org">[Comhairle nan Leabhraichean]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gaelicbooks.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Comhairle nan Leabhraichean Tales From Highland Perthshire</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Comhairle nan LeabhraicheanTales From Highland Perthshire - Gaelic Books CouncilOver 240 Perthshire Gaelic folktales and songs collected...</span></span></span>

The dialect also connects Perthshire to a wider language history. Perthshire historically straddled the Highland line, with Gaelic stronger in the north and west and English or Scots more dominant in the south and east. That makes Highland Perthshire’s tale record particularly valuable: it preserves material from the Gaelic side of a county that modern visitors may know mainly through English-language tourism, walking routes and heritage interpretation.

For folklore readers, this is more than a technical point. A fairy story written down in local Gaelic is not the same cultural object as a later English paraphrase in a guidebook. The manuscript form lets us see that the tale belonged to speakers, households and social memory before it became a printed “Perthshire legend”. The language is part of the evidence.

## Named storytellers and local places

One of the strengths of Murray’s material is that it often comes with collector, narrator and place metadata. Even when a narrator’s full identity is uncertain, the records point back towards real people rather than anonymous “folk”. Hidden Heritages entries name or partly name narrators such as Mrs Campbell of Foss, Annie MacDonald, John Robertson, Alex Small, Hugh Cameron and Mrs MacMillan, with associated parishes or towns including Dull, Foss, Appin of Dull, Strathtay and Logierait.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.hiddenheritages.ai/en/sct/347" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: hiddenheritages.ai">[Decoding Hidden Heritages]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">hiddenheritages.ai</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on hiddenheritages.ai.</span></span></span>

That level of detail changes how the tales should be read. A ghostly churchyard story from Foss is not just “a Scottish ghost story”. A fairy or wonder tale from Dull parish is not merely a floating myth. It is part of a social landscape in which stories travelled through families, workers, neighbours and estate networks. Some entries even preserve the chain of transmission: the Strathtay and Logierait “Snow-White” and “Wonder-Child” record gives Mrs MacMillan as narrator and says her source was her mother and grandmother.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.hiddenheritages.ai/en/sct/375" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: hiddenheritages.ai">[Decoding Hidden Heritages]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">hiddenheritages.ai</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on hiddenheritages.ai.</span></span></span>

This intergenerational detail is precious. It reminds readers that oral tradition is not simply old content. It is a relationship between tellers, listeners and remembered tellers. A story may have a medieval-looking motif, but its survival in Perthshire depended on someone telling it to a daughter or granddaughter, someone remembering it, and someone else deciding it was worth writing down.

## Oral tale, antiquarian note or tourist story?

Highland Perthshire’s Gaelic tales are best understood by separating three layers that are often blurred together in local folklore writing.

First, there is oral tradition: stories and songs remembered by local speakers and performed in ordinary social settings. Murray’s 1891 collection belongs closest to this layer because it records tales from living narrators at a known time and in a local language. The presence of named narrators, places, manuscript references and tale types makes it far stronger evidence than a later unattributed retelling.

Second, there is antiquarian and scholarly preservation. Once Murray wrote the tales down, they entered notebooks, manuscripts, catalogues and eventually printed scholarship. The National Library of Scotland, the University of Edinburgh and the Scottish Gaelic Texts Society all matter here because they show how fragile oral material became durable evidence: archived, catalogued, edited and translated.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://manuscripts.nls.uk/repositories/2/resources/21979" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: manuscripts.nls.uk">[nls.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">manuscripts.nls.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Papers of Lady Evelyn Stewart-Murray (1868-1940).Having learned Gaelic in her youth, she collected 240 Gaelic folk tales in Perthshire (t...</span></span></span>

Third, there is modern retelling. A tale may now appear in storytelling events, local-history writing, educational resources or online databases. That does not make it false, but it does change the reader’s question. A modern retelling may be vivid and accessible; an archive record may be less polished but closer to the historical evidence. The most reliable approach is to ask: who told it, who collected it, when was it written down, in what language, and how much has been reshaped for present-day audiences?

This distinction is especially important in Perthshire, where landscape tourism can easily turn complex traditions into scenic atmosphere. Highland Perthshire’s Gaelic tales are not just misty decoration for mountains and castles. They are evidence of a speech community, a social world and a set of remembered narratives that survived long enough to be caught on paper.

## How the archive changed the county’s folklore map

Before collections like Murray’s were accessible, Perthshire folklore could be too easily dominated by a few famous motifs: fairy hills, haunted castles, saints’ wells, witch memories and romantic Highland scenery. The Atholl manuscripts broaden that picture. They show that the county’s folklore was also domestic, comic, moral, musical, local and linguistically specific.

The University of Edinburgh places the Lady Evelyn Stewart Murray manuscripts alongside other major Scottish tradition holdings, including field notes, Highland airs and the MacLagan folklore survey. That context matters because it places Highland Perthshire within a national collecting movement, while still preserving its local distinctiveness.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://library.ed.ac.uk/heritage-collections/collections-and-search/archives/archives/manuscripts-collections/manuscript-collection" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: library.ed.ac.uk">[Library]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">library.ed.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">File Manuscript Collection &#124; Library</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">File Manuscript Collection &#124; Library</span></span></span>

The archive also helps correct a common misunderstanding: that folklore survives only as fixed legends attached to famous places. Murray’s material shows folklore as a repertoire. The same community could hold stories of fairies, ghosts, enchanted lovers, wicked relatives, helpful or dangerous strangers, religious warnings, songs and comic reversals. Some tales were attached to named places; others were portable story-patterns carried in local speech.

For Perthshire’s identity, that is a richer inheritance. The county’s folklore is not only a map of strange sites. It is also a record of who was still speaking Gaelic, who remembered old tale patterns, how women and men transmitted stories, and how a Highland district within a mixed county kept a distinctive voice into the late nineteenth century.

## Reading the tales today

Modern readers usually encounter Highland Perthshire’s Gaelic tales through the 2009 printed edition, archive catalogues or digital projects such as Hidden Heritages. Each form offers a different kind of access. The printed edition gives a sustained bilingual reading experience with notes; catalogues show where manuscripts sit and how they are described; digital tale records make individual stories searchable by place, narrator, collector and tale type.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gaelicbooks.org/product/tales-from-highland-perthshire?add-to-cart=5326&amp;lang=en" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gaelicbooks.org">[Comhairle nan Leabhraichean]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gaelicbooks.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Comhairle nan Leabhraichean Tales From Highland Perthshire</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Comhairle nan Leabhraichean Tales From Highland Perthshire</span></span></span>

The best reading habit is to keep the evidence chain visible. A polished English version may be enjoyable, but the deeper value lies in knowing that a story came from a particular manuscript, collector, narrator and locality. That is what separates Highland Perthshire’s Gaelic material from generic “Celtic folklore” packaging.

There is also a useful caution. Murray’s work is invaluable, but it is still a collected record, not a transparent recording of every performance detail. She wrote by hand, chose what to collect, worked within estate relationships, and belonged to a very different social class from many of her informants. Those limits do not weaken the collection’s importance; they make it more important to read it carefully.

## Why this tradition still matters in Perthshire

Highland Perthshire’s Gaelic tales matter because they preserve a part of the county’s voice that nearly vanished from everyday life. They show that Perthshire folklore was not only a set of picturesque legends later attached to tourist landscapes. It was also spoken, sung, remembered, translated, catalogued and sometimes nearly lost.

Lady Evelyn Stewart Murray’s Atholl collecting gives the county a rare evidence-rich folklore case: a dated collection, a known collector, identifiable narrators, local places, manuscript survival and a modern scholarly edition. Few local folklore topics offer such a clear chain from oral telling to archive to public reading.

For the wider Perthshire project, these tales form a bridge between landscape legend and documentary evidence. They sit naturally beside fairy hills, saints’ wells, witch traditions and haunted places, but they do something different: they show the working machinery of tradition. They reveal how stories survived through language, households, notebooks, archives and later translation — and why Highland Perthshire remains one of the county’s most important folklore regions.
