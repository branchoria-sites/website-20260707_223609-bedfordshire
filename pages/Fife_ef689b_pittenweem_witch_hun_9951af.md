## Introduction

Pittenweem’s witch story should be told first as a human tragedy, not as a spooky harbour attraction. In 1704, accusations made by 16-year-old Patrick Morton in the East Neuk fishing burgh led to the imprisonment and abuse of local people, the death in custody of Thomas Brown, and the lynching of Janet Cornfoot at the harbour in January 1705. The episode belongs in Fife folklore because it shows how belief, rumour, religious fear and local authority could turn everyday conflict into a deadly communal story. It also matters because Pittenweem still has to decide how to remember it: as “witch history”, as heritage tourism, as women’s history, as legal injustice, or as a warning about what careless storytelling can do.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://blog.historicenvironment.scot/2023/03/pittenweem-witch-hunt/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: blog.historicenvironment.scot">[Historic Environment Scotland Blog]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">blog.historicenvironment.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Historic Environment Scotland Blog Murder, Malice and Misogyny</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Historic Environment Scotland BlogMurder, Malice and Misogyny - The Pittenweem Witch HuntMarch 31, 2023 — 31 Mar 2023 — The witchcraft al...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 31, 2023</span></span></span>

## Why Pittenweem Matters in Fife’s Witchcraft Memory

Pittenweem is not simply another place with a witch legend attached to it. Its story is unusually well anchored in named people, civic places and recorded consequences. The village sits in Fife’s East Neuk, but the memory of the case is concentrated around recognisable locations: the tolbooth and church complex on the High Street, the harbour, and the wider network of nearby communities such as Leuchars, where Janet Cornfoot fled before being returned to Pittenweem. Historic Environment Scotland identifies the parish church and tolbooth steeple as a Category A listed building; the 1588 tower had a vaulted basement originally used as the tolbooth, making the built landscape itself part of the memory of confinement and burgh authority.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://portal.historicenvironment.scot/apex/f?p=1505%3A300%3A%3A%3A%3A%3AVIEWTYPE%2CVIEWREF%3Adesignation%2CLB39868" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: portal.historicenvironment.scot">[Historic Environment Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">portal.historicenvironment.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">1588 4-storey tower with vaulted basement (originally the tolbooth) and stair turret, ashlar bell-chamber with balustraded...Read more...</span></span></span>

The case also comes late in Scotland’s witch-hunting period. The Survey of Scottish Witchcraft, created from archive material by University of Edinburgh researchers, records nearly 4,000 people known to have been accused of witchcraft in Scotland between 1563 and 1736. The Scottish Witchcraft Act was repealed in 1736, and Historic Environment Scotland notes that by the early 18th century there was already growing scepticism about older witch-hunting methods. That timing makes Pittenweem especially stark: this was not a remote medieval event but a late, local collapse of justice in a community where formal belief, popular fear and civic weakness still had lethal force.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://witches.hca.ed.ac.uk/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: witches.hca.ed.ac.uk">[ed.ac.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">witches.hca.ed.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on ed.ac.uk.</span></span></span>

For folklore readers, the important point is not that the accusations were “true” in a supernatural sense. It is that the accusations used familiar witchcraft motifs that people around Pittenweem could recognise: malice after a quarrel, illness interpreted as bewitchment, a suspected pact with the Devil, charms involving household objects, and coerced confessions that turned neighbourly suspicion into a shared narrative. Pittenweem’s witch story is therefore part of Fife’s belief culture, but it must be handled as an episode of persecution rather than as colourful proof of local magic.

## The 1704 Accusations and Named Victims

The chain of events is usually traced to a dispute between Beatrix Layng and Patrick Morton, a 16-year-old working at his father’s smithy. According to Historic Environment Scotland’s account, Layng asked Morton to make nails; after he refused, he later became ill with convulsions and seizures that local physicians could not explain. In that atmosphere, suspicion focused on Layng, who already had a difficult local reputation, and the minister Patrick Cowper became heavily involved in interpreting Morton’s suffering as witchcraft.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://blog.historicenvironment.scot/2023/03/pittenweem-witch-hunt/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: blog.historicenvironment.scot">[Historic Environment Scotland Blog]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">blog.historicenvironment.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Historic Environment Scotland Blog Murder, Malice and Misogyny</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Historic Environment Scotland BlogMurder, Malice and Misogyny - The Pittenweem Witch HuntMarch 31, 2023 — 31 Mar 2023 — The witchcraft al...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 31, 2023</span></span></span>

Morton’s allegations widened beyond one neighbour. The named women most closely associated with the 1704 accusations were Beatrix Layng, Isobel Adam, Janet Cornfoot, Nicolas or Nicholas Lawson, Lillie Wallace and Janet Horseburgh. Isobel Adam’s confession then implicated Thomas Brown, who was questioned but did not confess. The Survey of Scottish Witchcraft’s Janet Cornfoot case records the accusation as part of a group of seven people charged with causing the tormenting of Patrick Morton, the smith’s son in Pittenweem.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://witches.hca.ed.ac.uk/case/C/EGD/2446" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: witches.hca.ed.ac.uk">[witches.hca.ed.ac.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">witches.hca.ed.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">the case of Janet CornfootA group of seven people were charged with causing the tormenting of Patrick Morton, the sixteen year old son of...</span></span></span>

The pattern is a useful warning about how witchcraft stories spread. A single explanation for one boy’s illness became a net cast over several people. Some were women already marked by reputation, age, poverty, quarrels or supposed knowledge. Others were drawn in through statements made under pressure. Thomas Brown’s place in the story is especially important because it prevents a simplified “women only” retelling: Scottish witch hunts overwhelmingly targeted women, but men could also be accused, imprisoned and killed by the machinery of suspicion. Brown died of starvation in the Pittenweem tolbooth despite not confessing to the allegations.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://blog.historicenvironment.scot/2023/03/pittenweem-witch-hunt/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: blog.historicenvironment.scot">[Historic Environment Scotland Blog]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">blog.historicenvironment.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Historic Environment Scotland Blog Murder, Malice and Misogyny</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Historic Environment Scotland BlogMurder, Malice and Misogyny - The Pittenweem Witch HuntMarch 31, 2023 — 31 Mar 2023 — The witchcraft al...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 31, 2023</span></span></span>

A responsible version of the story therefore names people as accused people, not as witches. The distinction is more than politeness. “Witch” can sound like a character type, a Halloween image or a supernatural identity. “Accused person” restores the legal and social reality: these were neighbours caught in an accusation system where torture, imprisonment, clerical pressure and local rumour shaped what was later remembered as “confession”.

## Janet Cornfoot and the Harbour Lynching

Janet Cornfoot’s death is the centre of Pittenweem’s darkest memory. After months of imprisonment, several of the accused were released or bailed, but Cornfoot remained confined. Historic Environment Scotland recounts that she escaped from the tolbooth, only to be captured and returned to Pittenweem on 30 January 1705. When news of her return spread, a mob formed, took her from a bailie’s house, dragged her towards the harbour, abused her, and killed her. Accounts vary in detail, but the commonly cited version says she was hoisted on a rope, stoned, then crushed under a door weighted with heavy stones.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://blog.historicenvironment.scot/2023/03/pittenweem-witch-hunt/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: blog.historicenvironment.scot">[Historic Environment Scotland Blog]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">blog.historicenvironment.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Historic Environment Scotland Blog Murder, Malice and Misogyny</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Historic Environment Scotland BlogMurder, Malice and Misogyny - The Pittenweem Witch HuntMarch 31, 2023 — 31 Mar 2023 — The witchcraft al...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 31, 2023</span></span></span>

The harbour setting matters because it turns the story from a hidden legal proceeding into a public spectacle. Pittenweem’s port was not a marginal place; Historic Environment Scotland notes that the harbour appears in records in 1228 and was historically connected with fishing and trade with the Low Countries. In folklore terms, harbours often gather stories because they are busy thresholds between land and sea, home and danger, local life and outside contact. In this case, however, the harbour is not a romantic supernatural stage. It is the place where communal violence became visible.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://blog.historicenvironment.scot/2023/03/pittenweem-witch-hunt/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: blog.historicenvironment.scot">[Historic Environment Scotland Blog]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">blog.historicenvironment.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Historic Environment Scotland Blog Murder, Malice and Misogyny</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Historic Environment Scotland BlogMurder, Malice and Misogyny - The Pittenweem Witch HuntMarch 31, 2023 — 31 Mar 2023 — The witchcraft al...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 31, 2023</span></span></span>

The aftermath deepens the sense of civic failure. Anonymous pamphlets and letters circulated after Cornfoot’s murder, questioning Patrick Morton’s statements and raising suspicions about the minister’s role and the treatment of the accused women. Beatrix Layng later spoke out about the torture that had produced confessions. The Privy Council investigated Cornfoot’s murder and identified suspects, but no one was ultimately charged or punished for killing her. That failure is one reason the story still unsettles Pittenweem: the violence was not just a mob action but a breakdown of protection, accountability and lawful process.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://blog.historicenvironment.scot/2023/03/pittenweem-witch-hunt/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: blog.historicenvironment.scot">[Historic Environment Scotland Blog]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">blog.historicenvironment.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Historic Environment Scotland Blog Murder, Malice and Misogyny</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Historic Environment Scotland BlogMurder, Malice and Misogyny - The Pittenweem Witch HuntMarch 31, 2023 — 31 Mar 2023 — The witchcraft al...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 31, 2023</span></span></span>

The most careful retellings avoid lingering over the physical details for shock value. The method of Cornfoot’s death must not be sanitised, because the brutality is part of the historical truth. But it should be described in order to explain the failure of justice, not to turn her into a horror-scene prop. The question is not “how gruesome can the story be made?” but “what did a community, its ministers and its officials allow to happen to a vulnerable accused woman?”

## What Is Folklore Here, and What Is History?

Pittenweem’s witch story sits at the boundary between documented history and folklore. The imprisonments, named accusations, deaths and investigations belong to historical record. The beliefs that drove the accusations — bewitchment, devilish bargains, charm-working, illness caused by malice — belong to the folklore and religious imagination of early modern Scotland. A good public retelling needs both layers, but it should not confuse them.

The Survey of Scottish Witchcraft records categories familiar from Scottish witch-trial material, including accusations of human illness, maleficium or harmful magic, ritual objects, religious motifs and claims of meetings with the Devil. Historic Environment Scotland’s Pittenweem account explains how Patrick Morton’s illness was interpreted through witchcraft suspicion when no ordinary cause was identified. That is the folklore mechanism: an unexplained misfortune becomes legible through stories a community already knows.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://witches.hca.ed.ac.uk/case/C/EGD/2446" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: witches.hca.ed.ac.uk">[witches.hca.ed.ac.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">witches.hca.ed.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">the case of Janet CornfootA group of seven people were charged with causing the tormenting of Patrick Morton, the sixteen year old son of...</span></span></span>

This distinction changes the tone of the page. Pittenweem is not best served by asking whether Beatrix Layng, Janet Cornfoot or Nicholas Lawson “really were witches”. That question repeats the logic of the accusation. The better folklore question is: what ideas about women, illness, quarrels, poverty, piety and the Devil made the accusation believable to enough people that law and mob action followed?

It also helps to separate different kinds of evidence. There are official or database records of accusations. There are later historical summaries. There are pamphlet traditions and local retellings. There are modern novels, tours, artworks and campaign materials. Each can be valuable, but they do different jobs. A novel may recover atmosphere and empathy; a database may preserve names and case structure; a mural may reveal modern attitudes; a local tour may keep memory alive. None should be treated as interchangeable proof.

## Why “Witch” Imagery Can Retell the Story Badly

The Pittenweem case shows why modern witch imagery can be harmful even when it is meant to acknowledge local history. A pointed hat, hooked nose, clawed fingers or cackling figure may feel like a familiar shorthand, but it can turn accused victims into the very caricature that helped justify suspicion. In Pittenweem, this is not an abstract concern. A large witch mural on the Larachmhor Tavern became the subject of objections, enforcement action and appeal because critics argued that it was gaudy, inaccurate and insensitive to women persecuted under witchcraft laws.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.theguardian.com/uk-news/2023/jun/09/fife-council-to-remove-menacing-witch-mural-pittenweem" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theguardian.com">[The Guardian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theguardian.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Guardian&#x27;Rather insensitive&#x27;: Fife council to remove menacing witch</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Guardian&#x27;Rather insensitive&#x27;: Fife council to remove menacing witch</span></span></span>

The planning issue had two layers. One was heritage management: the mural was painted on the south-western gable wall of a Category C listed building within the Pittenweem Conservation Area, and the Scottish Government’s planning case record identifies the matter as an enforcement appeal about a painted witch mural without listed building consent. The other was memory: objectors argued that the image repeated false popular-media ideas about “witches” rather than honouring real people who were accused, tortured or killed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.dpea.scotland.gov.uk/CaseDetails.aspx?T=1&amp;id=124571" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: dpea.scotland.gov.uk">[dpea.scotland.gov.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">dpea.scotland.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on scotland.gov.uk.</span></span></span>

This controversy is useful because it turns “responsible folklore retelling” from a vague ethical phrase into a practical test. A retelling can attract attention and still be wrong for the place. It can be visually memorable and still mislead. It can claim to preserve history while actually pushing the accused back into the role their persecutors assigned them.

A better approach is not to erase the word “witch” entirely. The term is historically unavoidable because it is the accusation under which people suffered. But the emphasis should move from monster to mechanism: accusation, coercion, fear, social reputation, clerical authority, civic failure and the difficulty of repair.

## Modern Tourism, Murals and Respectful Memory

Pittenweem’s witch history now exists in a wider Scottish movement to acknowledge those accused under witchcraft laws. The Witches of Scotland campaign says it seeks justice for nearly 4,000 people, predominantly women, accused between 1563 and 1736, and notes that a formal apology was issued by then First Minister Nicola Sturgeon on International Women’s Day 2022. A 2025 Scottish Parliament petition briefing similarly refers to the campaign’s figure of 3,837 accused people and explains that an apology is different from a legal pardon, which would require legislation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.witchesofscotland.com/about" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: witchesofscotland.com">[Witches of Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">witchesofscotland.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on witchesofscotland.com.</span></span></span>

That national context matters, but Pittenweem’s local memory has its own tensions. A proposed memorial was reported in 2012, but local support was divided and the community council did not back it after a split result. The later mural dispute showed that disagreement was not simply about whether the past should be mentioned, but about how it should be represented and who gets to decide what counts as respectful memory.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.deadlinenews.co.uk/2012/04/13/pittenweem-say-no-to-witch-memorial/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: deadlinenews.co.uk">[Deadline News]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">deadlinenews.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Deadline News Pittenweem say &quot;no&quot; to witch memorial</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Deadline News Pittenweem say &quot;no&quot; to witch memorial</span></span></span>

For tourism, the safest principle is not “avoid dark history”. Avoidance can become another kind of harm, especially when it leaves victims unnamed. The better principle is interpretation with care. A walking route, plaque, exhibition, audio guide or local-history page can discuss the witch hunt without turning it into a ghost-train attraction. The focus should be on named people, locations, records, uncertainty and consequences.

Responsible retelling in Pittenweem would usually mean:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--caution" markdown="1">

* **Name the accused as people first.** Janet Cornfoot, Beatrix Layng, Isobel Adam, Nicholas Lawson, Lillie Wallace, Janet Horseburgh and Thomas Brown should not be reduced to “the Pittenweem witches”.
* **Explain the accusation system.** Show how illness, rumour, clerical pressure and coerced confession combined.
* **Mark uncertainty honestly.** Where accounts vary, say so rather than smoothing the story into a single dramatic script.
* **Avoid caricature.** A monstrous witch image may be eye-catching, but it risks repeating the persecutors’ imagination.
* **Connect place to responsibility.** The tolbooth, High Street and harbour are not just atmospheric backdrops; they are places where confinement, authority and public violence intersected.

## How Should Pittenweem’s Witch Story Be Told?

Pittenweem’s witch story should be told as a case of accusation that became folklore because the community gave fear a story shape. It began with illness and suspicion, moved through named accusations and coerced confessions, and ended with real deaths and a failed search for justice. That makes it part of Fife’s supernatural tradition, but also part of Fife’s civic memory.

The best retelling is neither denial nor spectacle. It does not pretend witch belief was unimportant, because without those beliefs the accusations would not have made sense to the people involved. It also does not decorate the village with fantasy witches as though the accused were storybook villains. The heart of the matter is that people who were called witches were, in this case, neighbours, prisoners, fugitives, widows, workers and scapegoats.

Pittenweem therefore has a difficult but valuable role in the folklore of Fife. It reminds readers that local legends are not always harmless curiosities. Some preserve fear, blame and injustice. Retold well, the story of Janet Cornfoot and the other accused can help modern visitors understand how supernatural belief worked in a small burgh, why accusations stuck to particular people, and why memory needs more than atmosphere. It needs names, context and care.
