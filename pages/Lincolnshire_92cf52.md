## Introduction

The county’s folklore matters because it preserves a memory of work, water, fear and local identity. Lincolnshire legends often ask practical questions in supernatural form: what happens when wetlands are drained, when strangers arrive, when the dead are badly treated, when winter has to be turned back, or when a community needs a story to explain a stone carving, a hoof mark, a well, a custom or a dangerous stretch of road.

## Where Lincolnshire folklore belongs on the map

For this project, Lincolnshire is treated as a historic county rather than only as a modern administrative area. The historic county is traditionally divided into Lindsey in the north, Kesteven in the south-west and Holland in the south-east; the Association of British Counties describes Lincolnshire as England’s largest county after Yorkshire and notes those three historic Parts as central to its identity.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://abcounties.com/counties/county-profiles/lincolnshire/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: abcounties.com">[Association of British Counties]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">abcounties.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Association of British CountiesLincolnshireJune 26, 2013 — It is divided into the three parts; Holland in the south-east, Kesteven in the...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 26, 2013</span></span></span> The Wikishire map used as a county index states that its mapping conforms to the Historic Counties Standard and uses Historic County Borders Project data, which is useful because folklore often follows older county, parish, wapentake and landscape identities rather than present council lines.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://wikishire.co.uk/map/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wikishire.co.uk">[Wikishire]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wikishire.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Wikishire Great Britain and Ireland</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Great Britain and Ireland - interactive county map...</span></span></span>

This matters in Lincolnshire more than in many counties because several key traditions sit near borders or in districts whose everyday identity is older than modern administration. The Isle of Axholme looks west towards the Trent and old wetland routes; the Vale of Belvoir links Lincolnshire stories with Leicestershire aristocratic history; the fenland tales of drainage overlap with Cambridgeshire and the Isle of Ely; and black-dog traditions connect Lincolnshire with the wider eastern English world of Shuck, Barguest and spectral hounds. Good local folklore reading therefore keeps Lincolnshire at the centre while allowing stories to cross watery, ecclesiastical, estate and road boundaries where the tradition itself does so.

The most important printed foundation for the county is *County Folk-Lore Vol. V: Examples of Printed Folk-Lore Concerning Lincolnshire*, collected by Mrs Gutch and Mabel Peacock and published for the Folk-Lore Society in 1908. Its title page explicitly frames the book as printed folklore “concerning Lincolnshire”, collected by Gutch and Peacock, and it remains one of the major gateways into the county’s older charms, customs, beliefs, legends and local sayings.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" ## Tiddy Mun and the wetland imagination

The strongest Lincolnshire “fairy” or bog-spirit tradition is Tiddy Mun, a small, white-bearded being of the carrs and fen edges. In modern retellings he is often called a boggart, but the tale is more specific than a generic goblin story. The Lincolnshire Folk Tales Project summarises him as a toddler-sized, grey-clad figure of the marshy Carrs of the Ancholme Valley, laughing like a peewit and answering calls for help when floodwater rose near villages.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://lincolnshirefolktalesproject.com/2023/09/05/hello-world/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: lincolnshirefolktalesproject.com">[Lincolnshire Folk Tales Project]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">lincolnshirefolktalesproject.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Lincolnshire Folk Tales Project Tiddy Mun – Lincolnshire Folk Tales Project</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Lincolnshire Folk Tales Project Tiddy Mun – Lincolnshire Folk Tales Project</span></span></span>

The memorable point is that Tiddy Mun is not simply a monster. He belongs to an ecology. In the story, drainage of the Fens and Carrs from the seventeenth century onwards, associated with Dutch engineer Cornelius Vermuyden and Dutch workers, damages his watery home and brings bad omens, sickness, collapsing houses and misfortune. Locals placate him by going out at the new moon, pouring water into the dykes and apologising for the harm done to the wetland. The Lincolnshire Folk Tales Project notes that Marie Clothilde Balfour recorded the tale in her “Legends of the Cars”, published in *Folklore* in 1891, and that later scholarship has read the story as a folk memory of the social and ecological consequences of land reclamation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://lincolnshirefolktalesproject.com/2023/09/05/hello-world/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: lincolnshirefolktalesproject.com">[Lincolnshire Folk Tales Project]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">lincolnshirefolktalesproject.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Lincolnshire Folk Tales Project Tiddy Mun – Lincolnshire Folk Tales Project</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Lincolnshire Folk Tales Project Tiddy Mun – Lincolnshire Folk Tales Project</span></span></span>

That makes Tiddy Mun one of the county’s most useful legends for modern readers. It turns drainage, malaria, local resentment, engineering, wetland loss and agricultural prosperity into a story about a spirit whose anger must be negotiated. The tale does not prove that people “worshipped” a fen god in any simple sense. It does show that Lincolnshire folklore could express environmental change in a vivid and memorable form. It also links naturally with wider English fairy and boggart traditions, especially stories in which small beings punish greed, reward offerings, or embody the rights of a place.

## The Lincoln Imp: a medieval carving becomes a city mascot

The Lincoln Imp is the county’s most famous piece of legendary stonework. The physical figure is a small grotesque in Lincoln Cathedral’s Angel Choir, and the Lincolnshire Folk Tales Project identifies it as a late thirteenth-century cross-legged carving above the tomb of St Hugh.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://lincolnshirefolktalesproject.com/folktales/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: lincolnshirefolktalesproject.com">[Lincolnshire Folk Tales Project]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">lincolnshirefolktalesproject.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on lincolnshirefolktalesproject.com.</span></span></span> Visit Lincoln gives the familiar modern version: the Devil sends two imps to cause mischief; after trouble at Chesterfield and in Lincoln Cathedral, an angel turns one imp to stone, leaving it perched high in the choir while the other imp escapes into the wind outside.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.visitlincoln.com/blog/post/the-legend-of-the-lincoln-imp/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: visitlincoln.com">[Visit Lincoln]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">visitlincoln.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Visit Lincoln The Legend of the Lincoln Imp &#124; Visit Lincoln</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Visit Lincoln The Legend of the Lincoln Imp &#124; Visit Lincoln</span></span></span>

The Imp is a good example of the difference between a medieval object and a fully developed tourist legend. The carving is genuinely medieval. The story attached to it has been repeated, polished and popularised through guidebooks, local pride, souvenirs and city branding. Visit Lincoln notes that the Imp can now be found not only in the cathedral but across the city, including on jewellery, door knockers, gift-shop items and Lincoln City Football Club’s “Red Imps” identity.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.visitlincoln.com/blog/post/the-legend-of-the-lincoln-imp/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: visitlincoln.com">[Visit Lincoln]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">visitlincoln.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Visit Lincoln The Legend of the Lincoln Imp &#124; Visit Lincoln</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Visit Lincoln The Legend of the Lincoln Imp &#124; Visit Lincoln</span></span></span>

That does not make the legend worthless. It shows how folklore keeps working after the Middle Ages. A cathedral grotesque becomes a moral tale about mischief punished, then a children’s story, then a civic symbol. The Imp is less a fossil of one ancient belief than a living example of how Lincoln turns architecture into identity.

## Black Dogs, Hairy Jack and the county’s haunted roads

Lincolnshire has a strong black-dog tradition, but it is not always as deadly as the better-known Black Shuck of Norfolk and Suffolk. The Lincolnshire Folk Tales Project notes that phantom dogs in many traditions are sinister or deathly, yet in many Lincolnshire stories they are harmless or even companionable.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://lincolnshirefolktalesproject.com/folktales/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: lincolnshirefolktalesproject.com">[Lincolnshire Folk Tales Project]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">lincolnshirefolktalesproject.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on lincolnshirefolktalesproject.com.</span></span></span> This matters because it prevents a lazy “hellhound” reading of every black-dog report. In Lincolnshire, the same broad motif can appear as warning, guardian, road-haunter, uncanny animal or familiar presence.

These hounds sit well in a county of long lanes, drains, field roads and isolated crossings. Older black-dog traditions across England often attach themselves to liminal places: crossroads, churchyards, execution sites, bridges, hollow ways and lonely tracks. Lincolnshire versions belong to that same family, but local retellings often soften the creature into a repeated feature of the landscape rather than a single catastrophe. A black dog may still be frightening, but it can also be something walkers expect to hear about: a shape on the road, a companion at night, a sign that a place has a memory.

The neighbouring folklore links are obvious. Lincolnshire’s Black Dog, Hairy Jack and Shuck material connects with East Anglian black-dog legends to the south-east and with Barguest and Padfoot traditions farther north and west. The county’s version is useful because it shows the motif’s range: not every spectral hound is a death omen, and not every death omen belongs to one fixed creature.

## Witches, cunning folk and the Belvoir scandal

The Witches of Belvoir are Lincolnshire’s most famous witchcraft case, although the geography is slightly awkward in the way real history often is. The women at the centre of the story, Joan Flower and her daughters Margaret and Philippa, were from Bottesford and worked as servants connected with Belvoir Castle near Grantham. They were accused of causing the deaths of two sons of Francis Manners, Earl of Rutland. The Lincolnshire Folk Tales Project calls it Lincolnshire’s most famous witch trial and one of the most widely discussed and embellished witchcraft cases of the early seventeenth century.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://lincolnshirefolktalesproject.com/2024/02/21/the-witches-of-belvoir/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: lincolnshirefolktalesproject.com">[Lincolnshire Folk Tales Project]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">lincolnshirefolktalesproject.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on lincolnshirefolktalesproject.com.</span></span></span>

The case survives through hostile printed material. A contemporary pamphlet, *The Wonderful Discoverie of the Witchcrafts of Margaret and Phillip Flower*, appeared in 1619, and a ballad titled *Damnable Practises of Three Lincolnshire Witches* followed in 1620; the University of Melbourne’s execution-ballads archive summarises that ballad as a version of the Belvoir story conforming to English witch stereotypes, including familiars and revenge for slights.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://lincolnshirefolktalesproject.com/2024/02/21/the-witches-of-belvoir/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: lincolnshirefolktalesproject.com">[Lincolnshire Folk Tales Project]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">lincolnshirefolktalesproject.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on lincolnshirefolktalesproject.com.</span></span></span><span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://omeka.cloud.unimelb.edu.au/execution-ballads/items/show/876" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: omeka.cloud.unimelb.edu.au">[Research Gateway]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">omeka.cloud.unimelb.edu.au</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on edu.au.</span></span></span>

The details are grim. Joan is said to have died on the way to trial at Lincoln after choking on bread, while Margaret and Philippa were executed in March 1619; the Lincolnshire Folk Tales Project also notes that confessions in witch trials were commonly encouraged by torture and that later retellings altered the bread episode into a more overtly religious sign involving the eucharist.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://lincolnshirefolktalesproject.com/2024/02/21/the-witches-of-belvoir/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: lincolnshirefolktalesproject.com">[Lincolnshire Folk Tales Project]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">lincolnshirefolktalesproject.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on lincolnshirefolktalesproject.com.</span></span></span> The most responsible way to tell the story today is not as proof of witchcraft, but as a mixture of elite household politics, illness, gendered suspicion, print sensationalism and later folklore. Modern interpretations, including Tracy Borman’s argument that the women may have been framed in a succession plot, show how far the case has moved from supernatural certainty into historical doubt.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://lincolnshirefolktalesproject.com/2024/02/21/the-witches-of-belvoir/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: lincolnshirefolktalesproject.com">[Lincolnshire Folk Tales Project]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">lincolnshirefolktalesproject.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on lincolnshirefolktalesproject.com.</span></span></span>

Lincolnshire also has humbler witch and wise-person stories: bewitched animals, charms, counter-magic, folk healing, and local figures consulted when illness or misfortune needed an explanation. These are less spectacular than Belvoir but closer to everyday belief. In the county’s older folklore, witchcraft is not only a courtroom drama; it is a language for damaged milk, sick cattle, failed crops, grudges, neighbours and remedies.

## Byard’s Leap and the heroic horse

Byard’s Leap, near the old heathland roadscape south of Lincoln, is one of Lincolnshire’s most dramatic place legends. The story usually centres on Old Meg, a dangerous witch or hag, and a returning soldier or knight whose old blind horse, Byard, makes a miraculous leap during the fight. In Lincolnshire Life’s retelling, Byard jumps about sixty feet, throws the witch from his back, and leaves hoof marks still associated with the site; Old Meg is then staked and buried at the crossroads.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.lincolnshirelife.co.uk/heritage/the-legend-of-byards-leap/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: lincolnshirelife.co.uk">[Lincolnshire Life]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">lincolnshirelife.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Lincolnshire Life The legend of Byard’s Leap</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Lincolnshire Life The legend of Byard’s Leap</span></span></span>

The story has several classic ingredients: a hag at the edge of settled life, a veteran hero, an extraordinary animal, a crossroads burial, and physical marks in the landscape. That last detail is important. Place legends often survive because they explain something visible or visitable: hollows in stone, unusual ground, a name on a map, a roadside monument, a local inn sign, or a story told while passing through. Byard’s Leap works because it makes the landscape legible. A traveller does not merely see a place-name; they inherit a fight between witchcraft, courage and animal loyalty.

It also shows how Lincolnshire legend can be earthy rather than ethereal. This is not courtly romance in a faraway kingdom. It is a road legend: rough, memorable, violent, tied to a crossing and retold because people could point to where it happened.

## Seasonal customs that still gather people

Lincolnshire folklore is not only about ghosts and beings. Some of its strongest traditions are customs performed in public, especially in winter.

The Haxey Hood is the best-known. It takes place at Haxey on the Isle of Axholme and is commonly explained by the story of Lady de Mowbray losing her hood in the wind in the fourteenth century, after which local men chased it and the event was supposedly re-enacted each year. Haxey Hood’s own information site describes it as one of England’s oldest local traditions, probably dating back to the fourteenth century, and taking place each year on 6 January.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://haxeyhood.weebly.com/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: haxeyhood.weebly.com">[The Haxey Hood]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">haxeyhood.weebly.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on weebly.com.</span></span></span> Earlier folklore discussion also connects the Haxey custom with Lincolnshire Twelfth-tide mummers and Plough Jags, showing that the custom belongs to a wider winter-performance world rather than standing entirely alone.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Folk-Lore/Volume_7/The_Hood-Game_at_Haxey%2C_Lincolnshire" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: en.wikisource.org">[Wikisource]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">en.wikisource.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wikisource.org.</span></span></span>

Plough Jags are the other key winter custom. North Lincolnshire Museum explains that Plough Jags, Plough Plays or related plough customs were once found in many English counties and were performed on Plough Monday, the first Monday after Twelfth Night.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://northlincolnshiremuseum.co.uk/discover/the-plough-jags-a-living-tradition/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: northlincolnshiremuseum.co.uk">[North Lincolnshire Museum]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">northlincolnshiremuseum.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">North Lincolnshire Museum The Plough Jags: A Living Tradition</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">North Lincolnshire Museum The Plough Jags: A Living Tradition</span></span></span> The museum links the tradition to farm labourers, old Plough Lights in churches, village-to-village performance, requests for money or goods, and the threat that those who refused might have their doorstep turned over or garden ploughed up.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://northlincolnshiremuseum.co.uk/discover/the-plough-jags-a-living-tradition/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: northlincolnshiremuseum.co.uk">[North Lincolnshire Museum]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">northlincolnshiremuseum.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">North Lincolnshire Museum The Plough Jags: A Living Tradition</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">North Lincolnshire Museum The Plough Jags: A Living Tradition</span></span></span>

The play itself is not just comic begging. North Lincolnshire Museum summarises the Coleby Plough Jag plot as involving a recruiting sergeant, a farm labourer, a woman, Rag Fool, Beelzebub and a doctor, with a death-and-resurrection pattern linked to the farming year and the land coming back to life after winter.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://northlincolnshiremuseum.co.uk/discover/the-plough-jags-a-living-tradition/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: northlincolnshiremuseum.co.uk">[North Lincolnshire Museum]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">northlincolnshiremuseum.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">North Lincolnshire Museum The Plough Jags: A Living Tradition</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">North Lincolnshire Museum The Plough Jags: A Living Tradition</span></span></span> The Coleby tradition is also a good case of revival: the museum states that the group was resurrected in 1973 after a long absence, with Maurice “Mo” Ogg collecting songs, stories and costumes, and that the team still performs annually.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://northlincolnshiremuseum.co.uk/discover/the-plough-jags-a-living-tradition/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: northlincolnshiremuseum.co.uk">[North Lincolnshire Museum]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">northlincolnshiremuseum.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">North Lincolnshire Museum The Plough Jags: A Living Tradition</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">North Lincolnshire Museum The Plough Jags: A Living Tradition</span></span></span>

These customs matter because they are not simply “old things remembered”. They are social performances. They turn village space into theatre, make winter communal, and keep agricultural memory visible in a county where farming remains central to identity.

## Holy wells, saints and local healing places

Lincolnshire’s holy-well tradition is quieter than its imp, witch and black-dog stories, but it is still part of the county’s sacred landscape. British Holy Wells lists a range of Lincolnshire sites, including St Clement’s Well and St George’s Well at Stamford, St Guthlac’s Well at Crowland, several St Helen’s wells, Canwick Holy Well, Barnetby-le-Wold Holy Well and others marked as extant, lost, destroyed or uncertain.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.britishholywells.co.uk/england/lincolnshire/lincolnshirelist.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: britishholywells.co.uk">[British Holy Wells]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">britishholywells.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on britishholywells.co.uk.</span></span></span>

These wells need careful handling. A well’s name does not automatically prove an unbroken pre-Christian cult or a dramatic healing tradition. Some are medieval saintly places, some are later field-name survivals, some are lost sites, and some are known mainly through antiquarian notes. Even so, they matter because they show how water, parish memory and sanctity cluster together. In a county shaped by drainage and water management, the idea of a spring or well as blessed, healing, named or dangerous has obvious force.

One useful example is the holy well at Sempringham, associated with St Gilbert and the Gilbertines. The Megalithic Portal describes the well as rising in a circular stone basin in the churchyard and notes a curative reputation and possible saintly association.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.megalithic.co.uk/article.php?sid=18416" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: megalithic.co.uk">[Megalithic Portal]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">megalithic.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on megalithic.co.uk.</span></span></span> That is exactly the level at which many Lincolnshire holy wells should be read: not as spectacular miracle factories, but as small sacred sites where local Christianity, water and healing belief meet.

## Ghosts, dialect tales and the ordinary uncanny

Lincolnshire’s ghost tradition is broad, but its special flavour often comes through ordinary settings: rectories, inns, railway lines, manor houses, churches, lanes and fields. The Lincolnshire Folk Tales Project’s map includes Harlaxton Manor ghosts, the Holbeach Gamesters, the Grantham Railway Disaster, Old Jeffrey of Epworth Rectory, phantom calves and other uncanny animals, showing the range from well-known historical hauntings to localised road and animal legends.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://lincolnshirefolktalesproject.com/folktales/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: lincolnshirefolktalesproject.com">[Lincolnshire Folk Tales Project]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">lincolnshirefolktalesproject.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on lincolnshirefolktalesproject.com.</span></span></span>

Old Jeffrey is especially significant beyond Lincolnshire because the Epworth Rectory poltergeist became one of England’s most famous domestic haunting cases, linked with the childhood home of John and Charles Wesley. Its importance lies partly in documentation: it entered written religious and family history, then later ghost literature. That pattern is common in Lincolnshire folklore. A local disturbance becomes a written account; the written account becomes a county legend; the legend becomes part of a wider national ghost tradition.

Mabel Peacock’s dialect writing is important here because it preserved tone as well as content. North Lincolnshire Museum notes that her books contained stories, riddles, poems and retellings in North Lindsey folk speech, and that her work combined dialect writing with a serious interest in folklore.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://northlincolnshiremuseum.co.uk/discover/otchins-ghosts-and-a-tater-for-rheumatism-mabel-peacocks-dialect-tales/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: northlincolnshiremuseum.co.uk">[North Lincolnshire Museum]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">northlincolnshiremuseum.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on northlincolnshiremuseum.co.uk.</span></span></span> For readers, that means Lincolnshire folklore is not only a list of creatures. It is also a sound-world: local speech, rural humour, practical charms, teasing, understatement and the strange slipping into the everyday.

## What is old tradition, and what is modern retelling?

Lincolnshire folklore is strongest when the different layers are kept separate.

**Old oral and customary tradition** includes Plough Jags, Haxey Hood, charms, local sayings, black-dog reports and stories collected from local tellers. These traditions may have changed repeatedly, but they were rooted in performance, speech and community memory.

**Antiquarian and folklore collection** includes the work of Mabel Peacock, Mrs Gutch, Marie Clothilde Balfour and later collectors. These sources are invaluable, but they are not neutral recordings of a timeless past. They reflect the interests, methods and assumptions of the collectors, and sometimes standardise or frame stories for print.

**Pamphlet and ballad sensationalism** is crucial for the Belvoir witches. The surviving early sources are not calm court transcripts written for modern fairness; they are part of a culture that sold witchcraft, murder and moral horror to readers.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://lincolnshirefolktalesproject.com/2024/02/21/the-witches-of-belvoir/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: lincolnshirefolktalesproject.com">[Lincolnshire Folk Tales Project]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">lincolnshirefolktalesproject.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on lincolnshirefolktalesproject.com.</span></span></span><span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://omeka.cloud.unimelb.edu.au/execution-ballads/items/show/876" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: omeka.cloud.unimelb.edu.au">[Research Gateway]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">omeka.cloud.unimelb.edu.au</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on edu.au.</span></span></span>

**Tourist and civic folklore** is visible in the Lincoln Imp and, to a lesser degree, in many named haunted sites. The physical carving is medieval, but the popular tale, souvenir culture and football identity are part of later city-making.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.visitlincoln.com/blog/post/the-legend-of-the-lincoln-imp/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: visitlincoln.com">[Visit Lincoln]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">visitlincoln.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Visit Lincoln The Legend of the Lincoln Imp &#124; Visit Lincoln</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Visit Lincoln The Legend of the Lincoln Imp &#124; Visit Lincoln</span></span></span>

**Modern revival and adaptation** keep the county’s folklore active. The revived Coleby Plough Jag, the Lincolnshire Folk Tales Project, museum exhibitions, local-history writing and new storytelling all show that folklore is not simply preserved in books; it is selected, performed and reinterpreted for present audiences.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://northlincolnshiremuseum.co.uk/discover/the-plough-jags-a-living-tradition/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: northlincolnshiremuseum.co.uk">[North Lincolnshire Museum]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">northlincolnshiremuseum.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">North Lincolnshire Museum The Plough Jags: A Living Tradition</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">North Lincolnshire Museum The Plough Jags: A Living Tradition</span></span></span><span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://lincolnshirefolktalesproject.com/folktales/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: lincolnshirefolktalesproject.com">[Lincolnshire Folk Tales Project]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">lincolnshirefolktalesproject.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on lincolnshirefolktalesproject.com.</span></span></span>

## Why Lincolnshire’s folklore feels different

Lincolnshire does not need mountains, ruined castles on every ridge, or a dense fairy mythology to have powerful folklore. Its stories draw strength from flatness, exposure and work. The supernatural often appears where people meet uncertainty: a fen drain, a winter field, a dark road, a cathedral roof, a noble household, a holy spring, a crossroads, a village game, a railway bend, a rectory wall.

The county’s most memorable traditions also resist one simple mood. Tiddy Mun is angry but necessary. [Black Dogs]({{ 'black-dogs-26a615/' | relative_url }}) may be frightening or friendly. The Lincoln Imp is demonic mischief turned into civic affection. Plough Jags are comic, threatening, charitable and ritualised at once. The Belvoir witches are presented in old print as villains, but modern readers are more likely to see accused women caught in fear, politics and misogyny. Byard’s Leap is a witch-killing legend, but its emotional hero is a horse.

That mixture is Lincolnshire’s real folklore identity: practical, uncanny, landscape-bound and often morally complicated. Its legends are not escapist decorations added to the county from outside. They are ways of remembering water, labour, danger, faith, suspicion, humour and pride in a place where the land itself has always had to be read carefully.

    return 'fr-ebay-market-flag fr-ebay-market-flag--' + String(marketId || '').toLowerCase().replace(/_/g, '-').replace(/[^a-z0-9-]+/g, '');
  }
  function closeMenu(section) {
    var menu = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-menu]');
    var trigger = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-trigger]');
    var picker = section.querySelector('.fr-ebay-market-picker');
    if (menu) menu.hidden = true;
    if (trigger) trigger.setAttribute('aria-expanded', 'false');
    if (picker) picker.classList.remove('fr-ebay-market-picker--open');
  }
  function availableMarkets(section) {
    var select = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-select]');
    if (select) {
      return Array.prototype.slice.call(select.options)
        .map(function (option) { return option.value; })
        .filter(function (marketId) { return !!markets[marketId]; });
    }
    return Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]'))
      .map(function (button) { return button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button'); })
      .filter(function (marketId) { return !!markets[marketId]; });
  }
  function applyMarket(section, marketId, persist) {
    var available = availableMarkets(section);
    if (available.indexOf(marketId)< 0) marketId = available[0] || defaultMarket;
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-localized-link]')).forEach(function (link) {
      var query = link.getAttribute('data-ebay-query') || '';
      var reference = link.getAttribute('data-ebay-reference') || '';
      link.setAttribute('href', buildUrl(query, reference, marketId));
    });
    var select = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-select]');
    if (select && available.indexOf(marketId) >= 0) select.value = marketId;
    var selectedText = marketId;
    if (select && select.selectedIndex >= 0) selectedText = select.options[select.selectedIndex].text;
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-selected-market-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = selectedText;
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-selected-market-flag], [data-ebay-trigger-market-flag]')).forEach(function (flag) {
      flag.className = flagClass(marketId);
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-trigger-market-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = selectedText;
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-domain-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = domainLabel(marketId);
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-option]')).forEach(function (button) {
      button.setAttribute('aria-selected', button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-option') === marketId ? 'true' : 'false');
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      var active = button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button') === marketId;
      button.setAttribute('aria-pressed', active ? 'true' : 'false');
    });
    section.setAttribute('data-ebay-selected-market', marketId);
    if (persist) {
      try { if (window.localStorage) localStorage.setItem('phoenix-ebay-market', marketId); } catch (err) {}
    }
    closeMenu(section);
  }
  sections.forEach(function (section) {
    if (section.getAttribute('data-ebay-localized-init') === '1') return;
    section.setAttribute('data-ebay-localized-init', '1');
    var available = availableMarkets(section);
    var marketId = defaultMarket;
    if (window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation && window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation.inferMarket) {
      marketId = window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation.inferMarket(available, {
        storageKey: 'phoenix-ebay-market',
        defaultMarket: defaultMarket
      });
    } else if (available.indexOf(defaultMarket)< 0) {
      marketId = available[0] || defaultMarket;
    }
    var select = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-select]');
    if (select) {
      select.addEventListener('change', function () {
        applyMarket(section, select.value, true);
      });
    }
    var trigger = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-trigger]');
    var menu = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-menu]');
    if (trigger && menu) {
      trigger.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
        event.stopPropagation();
        var expanded = trigger.getAttribute('aria-expanded') === 'true';
        menu.hidden = expanded;
        trigger.setAttribute('aria-expanded', expanded ? 'false' : 'true');
        var picker = trigger.closest('.fr-ebay-market-picker');
        if (picker) picker.classList.toggle('fr-ebay-market-picker--open', !expanded);
      });
      Array.prototype.slice.call(menu.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-option]')).forEach(function (button) {
        button.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
          event.stopPropagation();
          applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-option'), true);
        });
      });
      document.addEventListener('click', function () { closeMenu(section); });
    }
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      button.addEventListener('click', function () {
        applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button'), true);
      });
    });
    applyMarket(section, marketId, false);
  });
})();
</script>
