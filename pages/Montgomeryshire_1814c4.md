## Introduction

Montgomeryshire should be treated here as the historic county, not simply as a modern council area. The Wikimedia Commons historic-counties map identifies Montgomeryshire as one of the thirteen historic counties of Wales, while modern Powys was formed in 1974 by bringing together Montgomeryshire, Radnorshire and Brecknockshire. That matters for folklore because older tales attach themselves to parishes, valleys, roads and county identities that do not always match present-day administrative boundaries.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File%3AWales_Historic_Counties_map_Montgomeryshire.svg" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: commons.wikimedia.org">[wikimedia.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">commons.wikimedia.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wikimedia.org.</span></span></span>

## Where Montgomeryshire Folklore Belongs

Historic Montgomeryshire lies between the high country of mid Wales and the English border. Its neighbours include Denbighshire to the north, Shropshire to the east and south-east, Radnorshire to the south, Cardiganshire to the south-west, and Merionethshire to the west and north-west. Wikishire describes it as largely mountainous, with more fertile valleys towards the Shropshire side; that mixed terrain helps explain the county’s folklore texture, which is both upland Welsh and borderland Marcher.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://wikishire.co.uk/wiki/Montgomeryshire" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wikishire.co.uk">[Wikishire]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wikishire.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wikishire.co.uk.</span></span></span>

The county was created as part of the Tudor settlement of Wales: the Laws in Wales Acts abolished the Marcher lordships and created the thirteen historic counties, including Montgomeryshire. Earlier identities still matter, especially the old Powys Wenwynwyn background and the cantrefs and commotes that later became folded into the shire. In folklore terms, that means Montgomeryshire stories often feel older than the county name itself: they belong to saints’ cults, river valleys, upland grazing, border conflict and parish memory as much as to formal county administration.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://datamap.gov.wales/layers/geonode%3Ahistoric_counties_bng_rcahmw_ply" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: datamap.gov.wales">[datamap.gov.wales]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">datamap.gov.wales</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Historic County Boundaries of Wales</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Historic County Boundaries of Wales</span></span></span>

This also explains why some Montgomeryshire traditions overlap naturally with neighbouring counties. A tale collected just across a boundary may preserve the same motif as one told in Montgomeryshire; a saint’s cult may draw pilgrims from wider Wales; and a stone-circle legend on the Shropshire side may still belong to the shared border imagination of the old Montgomeryshire marches. Good county folklore does not pretend that stories stopped at a line on a map.

## The County’s Folklore Keepers

Montgomeryshire is unusually well served by local antiquarian and historical collecting. The Powysland Club, founded in 1867, describes itself as the historical society for Montgomeryshire and the senior county historical society in Wales. It publishes *Montgomeryshire Collections*, supports lectures and excursions, and maintains a library in Welshpool. Its library is said to hold around 10,000 books, maps and journals, with rare and valuable material relating especially to Montgomeryshire and Wales.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.powyslandclub.co.uk/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: powyslandclub.co.uk">[powyslandclub.co.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">powyslandclub.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on powyslandclub.co.uk.</span></span></span>

For folklore readers, the importance of this is simple: many county traditions survived because Victorian and later [collectors]({{ 'collectors/' | relative_url }}) treated local memory as worth printing. *Montgomeryshire Collections* was not only a vehicle for archaeology and genealogy; the Powysland Club notes that current issues range from prehistory to memories of people still living, while the Montgomeryshire Genealogical Society describes the journal’s founding purpose as collecting and printing the historical, ecclesiastical, genealogical, topographical and literary remains of Montgomeryshire.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.powyslandclub.co.uk/montgomeryshire-collections/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: powyslandclub.co.uk">[powyslandclub.co.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">powyslandclub.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on powyslandclub.co.uk.</span></span></span>

One of the most important figures connected with Welsh folklore was Elias Owen, a Montgomeryshire-born cleric and antiquary. The National Library of Wales notes that his *Welsh Folk-Lore* began as a prize essay at the 1887 National Eisteddfod in London and was published as a single volume in 1896. Owen gathered much of his material while working as a diocesan school inspector, visiting parishes and asking clergy to introduce him to older local informants. That method gives his work a strong oral-tradition flavour, though it also reflects the assumptions and language of late Victorian antiquarianism.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.library.wales/discover-learn/digital-exhibitions/books/history-books/welsh-folk-lore-a-collection-of-the-folk-tales-and-legends-of-north-wales" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: library.wales">[National Library of Wales]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">library.wales</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on library.wales.</span></span></span>

Wirt Sikes’s *British Goblins* is another major source, though one to use carefully. Published in 1880, it gathered Welsh fairy, spirit, custom, well, stone and dragon lore, but later scholars have criticised some of its sourcing habits. For Montgomeryshire, however, it preserves a famous Trefeglwys changeling story and helps show how county tales circulated in wider Welsh and English-language folklore collections.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/files/34704/34704-h/34704-h.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on gutenberg.org.</span></span></span>

## Fairies, Changelings and the Trefeglwys “Place of Strife”

The clearest Montgomeryshire fairy tale is the changeling story located at Trefeglwys, near Llanidloes. In Sikes’s version, a shepherd’s cottage in the parish was known as the “Place of Strife” because of a strange quarrel connected with twins, a lonely house, and the fear that the children had been exchanged by the fairies. The story has the classic changeling pattern: a mother leaves infants unattended, anxiety gathers around their altered behaviour, and local knowledge supplies a test or remedy.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/files/34704/34704-h/34704-h.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on gutenberg.org.</span></span></span>

The tale matters because it turns a general European fairy motif into a Montgomeryshire landscape story. [Changelings]({{ 'changelings/' | relative_url }}) appear across Britain and Ireland, but here the story is not told vaguely “in Wales”; it is fixed to Trefeglwys, near Llanidloes, and to a cottage with a remembered name. That localising habit is central to county folklore. The more exact the place, the more the supernatural story becomes part of local memory, route knowledge and parish identity.

It also shows the darker side of fairy belief. Modern retellings often make fairies charming or decorative, but older Welsh fairy traditions could express real fear: fear of infant sickness, isolation, maternal vulnerability, and the unseen dangers around an upland home. In the Trefeglwys story, the fairies are not just pretty beings from a children’s book; they are an explanation for a terrifying domestic crisis.

Elias Owen’s wider Welsh fairy material helps place Montgomeryshire within the broader tradition of the “fair family” or “fair folk”. His book treats fairy stories not as one neat mythology but as a cluster of beliefs about beautiful otherworldly people, household interference, music, dancing, animals, sickness and uncanny encounters. For Montgomeryshire, the Trefeglwys changeling tale is the most useful local doorway into that larger Welsh fairy world.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/files/20096/20096-h/20096-h.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on gutenberg.org.</span></span></span>

## Saints, Hares and Holy Wells

Montgomeryshire’s supernatural landscape is not only fairy-haunted. Some of its most enduring traditions are Christianised landscape legends, especially around saints, healing and sanctuary.

The most famous is Saint Melangell at Pennant Melangell, in the Tanat Valley. The modern shrine site tells the story of Melangell as an Irish king’s daughter who fled an unwanted marriage and lived as a hermit in Cwm Pennant. When Brochwel Ysgithrog, Prince of Powys, was hunting, a hare fled to Melangell for protection; his hounds retreated, and the prince granted her the valley as a sanctuary. The church became associated with refuge, healing and counsel, and the hare became central to her identity.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.landoflegends.wales/location/pennant-melangell" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: landoflegends.wales">[Land of Legends]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">landoflegends.wales</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on landoflegends.wales.</span></span></span>

The legend is powerful because it joins three things readers can still understand immediately: a remote valley, a vulnerable animal, and a sacred claim that violence should stop at this place. The National Churches Trust describes St Melangell’s as a 12th-century Norman church on a Bronze Age site and a place of pilgrimage for nearly 1,000 years, with an important shrine dated to the later 12th century. Modern visitors therefore encounter both story and material survival: church, shrine, yews, valley and pilgrimage practice.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nationalchurchestrust.org/church/st-melangell-pennant-melangell" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nationalchurchestrust.org">[National Churches Trust]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nationalchurchestrust.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nationalchurchestrust.org.</span></span></span>

Saint Myllin’s Well at Llanfyllin is another strong example. The town’s name preserves the saint, and local tradition links the well with early baptism. British Holy Wells notes that very little is securely known about Myllin and that even his period is contested, while local and heritage accounts associate him with the sixth or seventh century and with baptism by immersion. The uncertainty is part of the honest story: the saint’s historical identity is blurred, but the well’s role in local sacred geography is clear.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://britishholywells.co.uk/wales/montgomeryshire/myllinswellllanfyllin/myllinswellllanfyllin.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: britishholywells.co.uk">[British Holy Wells]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">britishholywells.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on britishholywells.co.uk.</span></span></span>

The well also preserves older healing and wishing customs. Later summaries record visits on Trinity Sunday, drinking sweetened water, dipping rags in the water and hanging them nearby, and linking wishes or fortunes to the decay of the cloth. These rag-well customs are widespread in Britain and Ireland, but the Llanfyllin example gives Montgomeryshire its own holy-well focus: a place where saintly memory, water, healing and communal festivity overlap.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St_Myllin%27s_Well" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">St Myllin&#x27;s Well</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">St Myllin&#x27;s Well</span></span></span>

## Witches, Cunning Folk and Everyday Protection

Montgomeryshire folklore is especially revealing when it turns from grand legends to practical magic. In Owen’s material, charms and counter-charms appear not as theatrical wizardry but as rural problem-solving: people sought help when cattle sickened, when witchcraft was suspected, or when misfortune seemed to have a hidden cause.

A striking example comes from Llansilin parish. Owen records that a farmer who had lost several cattle went, or sent, to “Shon Gyfarwydd” of Llanbrynmair, described as a well-known conjuror, for an explanation and a charm against further loss. The charm was said to work, and the grateful farmer acknowledged the benefit. Another passage describes a charm against foot-and-mouth disease, tied to cattle horns as both cure and prevention.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.academia.edu/49085497/Rev_Elias_Owen_and_the_Folklore_of_the_Tanat_Valley_Full_" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: academia.edu">[Academia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">academia.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on academia.edu.</span></span></span>

These stories are valuable because they show how “witchcraft” traditions were often about livestock, livelihood and fear of loss. In a rural county, cattle illness was not an abstract superstition; it could threaten a household’s survival. The cunning person or conjuror functioned as a specialist in hidden causes, offering a charm where ordinary remedies seemed insufficient.

Owen also records beliefs about “witch’s butter”, a fungus on decayed wood into which pins could be thrust to force a witch to undo harm. The logic is harsh but clear: the object stands in for the suspected witch, and pain applied to the charm compels the wrongdoer to appear or relent. Such traditions are uncomfortable to modern readers, but they are part of the county’s belief culture: they reveal how people imagined blame, illness, social tension and justice.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.academia.edu/123539517/Elias_Owen_Welsh_Folk_Lore_1896" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: academia.edu">[Academia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">academia.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Elias Owen Welsh Folk Lore 1896</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Elias Owen Welsh Folk Lore 1896</span></span></span>

It is important not to romanticise this material. These are not neutral “spooky customs”; accusations of witchcraft could damage real people, especially marginal women. But neither should the stories be dismissed as foolishness. They belong to a world where disease, failed crops, infant illness and animal death demanded explanations, and where charms offered a sense of agency when official medicine or law felt distant.

## Border Robbers, Red Bandits and Dangerous Roads

Not all Montgomeryshire folklore is supernatural. Some of its best-known narrative material belongs to the borderland tradition of robbers, outlaws and dangerous roads. The “Red Bandits of Montgomery” appear in later Welsh fairy-tale retellings as a gang of thieves and cut-throats who climbed down chimneys to rob houses. One version, popularised in William Elliot Griffis’s *Welsh Fairy Tales*, tells of a widow who outwits or survives the bandits, with the story carrying the brisk moral drama of a fireside tale.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ztevetevans.wordpress.com/2018/10/02/welsh-folklore-the-widow-the-red-bandits-of-montgomery-and-silly-doot/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ztevetevans.wordpress.com">[Under the influence!]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ztevetevans.wordpress.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Under the influence!Welsh Folklore: The Widow, the Red Bandits of Montgomery</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Under the influence!Welsh Folklore: The Widow, the Red Bandits of Montgomery</span></span></span>

The Red Bandits are useful because they sit between folklore and social memory. They may echo real fears of robbery in isolated houses and on border roads, but the tale as commonly encountered is literary and retold. That distinction matters. A reader should not treat every colourful robber story as a court record; it is better understood as a narrative shaped by fear, humour, justice and local reputation.

Montgomeryshire’s position on the Welsh-English border encouraged this kind of story. Roads to Shrewsbury, Welshpool, Montgomery and the uplands were routes of trade and movement, but also routes of vulnerability. In folklore, the robber is often the human equivalent of the fairy or witch: a danger from outside the household, appearing at night, testing courage and cunning.

## Stones, Cows and the Wider Border Imagination

Montgomeryshire itself has ancient sites and archaeological memory, but one of the most relevant neighbouring legends sits just over the border at Mitchell’s Fold in Shropshire. English Heritage records the local legend: during famine, a fairy gave a magic cow that produced endless milk; an evil witch milked it into a sieve, the cow vanished, and the witch was turned to stone inside a circle raised to imprison her.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.english-heritage.org.uk/visit/places/mitchells-fold-stone-circle/history/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: english-heritage.org.uk">[English Heritage]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">english-heritage.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on english-heritage.org.uk.</span></span></span>

Why include a Shropshire stone circle on a Montgomeryshire folklore page? Because border folklore often works by shared upland imagination rather than modern county filing. Mitchell’s Fold lies close to the Welsh border, and the magical cow motif links strongly with Welsh fairy-cow traditions. Elias Owen himself compared Welsh stories of an abundant fairy cow with the White Cow of Mitchell’s Fold, making the tale relevant to Montgomeryshire’s eastern edge and to the wider Welsh-border pattern of stones, witches and miraculous cattle.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buwch_Frech" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Buwch Frech</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Buwch Frech</span></span></span>

The story also helps readers recognise a common folklore mechanism: prehistoric monuments invite explanatory tales. People who did not know who built a stone circle, or why, made sense of it through memorable narrative. A witch turned to stone, a fairy cow, famine and punishment are not archaeological explanations; they are cultural explanations, giving an ancient, silent structure a moral and emotional plot.

## Ghosts, Black Dogs and What Is Thinly Attested

Readers often ask whether Montgomeryshire has famous black dog legends or major ghost traditions. The honest answer is that Montgomeryshire has plenty of uncanny material in the broader Welsh tradition, but it is not as strongly branded by one famous county black dog as East Anglia is by Black Shuck or parts of northern England are by the Barghest. British black-dog folklore is widespread and varied, ranging from death omens to guardians, but strong Montgomeryshire-specific examples are less prominent in the standard accessible sources than the county’s fairy, saint, charm and holy-well material.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://tetzoo.com/blog/2022/8/13/legend-of-the-black-dog" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: tetzoo.com">[Tetrapod Zoology]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">tetzoo.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Tetrapod Zoology Legend of the Black Dog</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Tetrapod Zoology Legend of the Black Dog</span></span></span>

That absence is useful rather than disappointing. It prevents a common mistake: forcing every county into the same checklist of black dogs, giants, witches, dragons and ghosts. Montgomeryshire’s strongest evidence points elsewhere. Its folklore centre of gravity is not a single hellhound but a pattern of rural belief: changelings at Trefeglwys, charms at Llansilin and Llanbrynmair, saintly refuge at Pennant Melangell, holy water at Llanfyllin, and border tales of danger and cunning.

There are ghostly and spirit traditions in Welsh folklore more generally, and collectors such as Owen and Sikes include apparitions, death omens and uncanny beings across Wales. But for a county page, it is better to keep confidence proportionate. ## Old Tradition, Antiquarian Print and Modern Retelling

A Montgomeryshire folklore page needs one important caution: many stories reach us through Victorian and Edwardian collectors. That does not make them false, but it does mean they have passed through editors, translators, literary taste and antiquarian theories. Owen gathered oral testimony from older inhabitants and clergy, but he still shaped it for a published essay. Sikes preserved valuable material, but his work has been criticised for uneven citation and reliance on earlier compilations.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/files/20096/20096-h/20096-h.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on gutenberg.org.</span></span></span>

Modern heritage retellings add another layer. Pennant Melangell is now a living pilgrimage and visitor site; Llanfyllin promotes Saint Myllin and his well as part of local identity; tourism pages present mid Wales through myths and legends. These retellings are not “fake” simply because they are modern. They show how folklore continues to work: stories are re-spoken for walkers, pilgrims, families, schools and visitors.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://stmelangell.org/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: stmelangell.org">[stmelangell.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">stmelangell.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on stmelangell.org.</span></span></span>

The key is to ask what kind of evidence is being used:

* **Oral tradition preserved in print:** the Trefeglwys changeling and Llansilin charm material, as mediated by collectors.
* **Saintly legend attached to a real cult site:** Melangell and Myllin, where hagiography, church history and local tradition overlap.
* **Landscape explanation:** Mitchell’s Fold and the magical cow, where story gives meaning to ancient stones.
* **Modern heritage retelling:** visitor-facing versions that keep the tale alive but may simplify uncertainty.
* **Literary adaptation:** polished fairy-tale versions such as the Red Bandits, where entertainment and moral storytelling are part of the appeal.

That layered approach lets the reader enjoy the mystery without confusing every retelling with a medieval document or every legend with a literal historical claim.

## Why Montgomeryshire’s Folklore Matters

Montgomeryshire’s folklore matters because it preserves a county identity that is easy to blur inside modern Powys. The tales point to a distinctive mid-Wales world: upland parishes, border roads, market towns, saints’ wells, isolated cottages, cattle farms, old chapels, and the long memory of Powys before and after the county was formally created. Its stories are not merely decorative; they show what people feared, protected, celebrated and explained.

The county’s most memorable traditions are also unusually humane. Melangell protects a hare and turns a valley into sanctuary. Myllin’s well links water with healing and baptism. The Trefeglwys changeling tale gives supernatural form to parental terror. The Llansilin cattle charms show families trying to defend their livelihood. Even the magical cow of the border stones is a famine story before it is a witch story: it begins with hunger and the hope of enough milk for all.

For modern readers, Montgomeryshire offers a quieter but deeper kind of folklore landscape. It is not built around one blockbuster monster. It is a web of local belief, preserved by county collectors and still visible in places that can be visited, mapped and remembered: Trefeglwys, Llanidloes, Llanfyllin, Pennant Melangell, Welshpool, Montgomery and the border hills. Its folklore asks us to look at ordinary rural places twice — first as history, and then as story.
