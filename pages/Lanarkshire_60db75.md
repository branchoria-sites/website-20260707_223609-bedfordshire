## The county behind the stories

Lanarkshire is a historic county in the heart of the Scottish Lowlands. In older county geography it is, in effect, Clydesdale: the valley of the River Clyde, rising in the Lowther Hills and running through Lanarkshire before leaving the county for the Firth of Clyde. That matters for folklore because many traditions follow the river, its burns, its market towns and its routes, rather than the boundaries of modern council areas. Wikishire describes the county as bounded by Dumbartonshire and Stirlingshire to the north, West Lothian, Midlothian and Peeblesshire to the east, Dumfriesshire to the south, and Ayrshire, Renfrewshire and Dumbartonshire to the west.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://wikishire.co.uk/wiki/Lanarkshire" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wikishire.co.uk">[Wikishire]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wikishire.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Wikishire Lanarkshire</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wikishire Lanarkshire</span></span></span>



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/Lanarkshire_60db75-overview.webp" | relative_url }}" alt="Overview image for What Makes Lanarkshire&#x27;s Folklore So..." loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

The historic county also contains a sharp contrast that gives its folklore a distinctive feel. Upper Clydesdale has hills, farms, old burghs, standing stones and saintly wells; the lower and northern parts include Glasgow and the industrial towns shaped by coal, textiles, iron, steel and engineering. This means Lanarkshire folklore is not only rural fairy-lore or romantic ruin-lore. It also includes customs kept alive in busy towns, stories attached to industrial communities, and modern retellings that sit beside medieval pilgrimage and early modern witch records.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://wikishire.co.uk/wiki/Lanarkshire" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wikishire.co.uk">[Wikishire]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wikishire.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Wikishire Lanarkshire</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wikishire Lanarkshire</span></span></span>

## Saints, wells and the sacred landscape

The most important saintly figure in Lanarkshire tradition is St Kentigern, better known as St Mungo, Glasgow’s patron saint. Historic Environment Scotland describes Glasgow Cathedral as dedicated to St Kentigern and notes that the building dates mostly from the 1200s. Kentigern is believed to have been the first bishop of the area that became Strathclyde, and it is thought that he was buried on the cathedral site around 612. The cathedral’s lower church and shrine made the site a major medieval focus of devotion.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.historicenvironment.scot/visit/all/glasgow-cathedral/history-and-stories/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: historicenvironment.scot">[Historic Environment Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">historicenvironment.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on historicenvironment.scot.</span></span></span>

St Mungo’s Well, in the cathedral crypt, turns Glasgow’s foundation story into a physical place. Modern interpretation of the well presents it as one of the city’s oldest sacred sites, later built into the cathedral fabric when the lower church was expanded as a pilgrimage centre. The folklore value here is not that every miracle story can be verified, but that water, burial, sainthood and city identity all meet in one remembered spot.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.aproxima.co.uk/the-well-overview" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: aproxima.co.uk">[Aproxima]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">aproxima.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Aproxima The Well</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Aproxima The Well</span></span></span>

Lanarkshire’s [holy wells]({{ 'holy-wells-a9f943/' | relative_url }}) are not confined to Glasgow. The county has a dense scatter of wells dedicated to saints or to Our Lady, including St Bride’s, St Patrick’s, St Ninian’s, St Oswald’s and St Catherine’s wells in different parts of the old county. Some have only slight surviving traditions; others preserve stronger local memory. Stonehouse tradition, for example, links St Patrick’s Well with healing properties for tuberculosis and skin diseases, while Motherwell’s very name is commonly connected with the Lady Well or Mother Well, a Marian holy well formerly marked in the landscape near Ladywell Road.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.stonehouseonline.org.uk/holywells.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: stonehouseonline.org.uk">[stonehouseonline.org.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">stonehouseonline.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on stonehouseonline.org.uk.</span></span></span>

These well traditions show a familiar Scottish pattern. A spring might be remembered as holy, healing, dangerous, blessed, or simply old. After the Reformation, official attitudes to pilgrimage wells hardened, and some practices were denounced as superstition. Yet local memory often proved more durable than official disapproval. In Lanarkshire, wells survive as place-names, plaques, road names, restored features, and fragments of story rather than as a single continuous ritual system.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.thenorthernantiquarian.org/2014/06/28/st-mungos-well-glasgowc/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: thenorthernantiquarian.org">[thenorthernantiquarian.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">thenorthernantiquarian.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">st mungos well glasgowc</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">st mungos well glasgowc</span></span></span>

## Witchcraft, cunning folk and the records of fear

Lanarkshire’s witch traditions are unusually well grounded in documentary history. The University of Edinburgh’s Survey of Scottish Witchcraft is the key modern research tool for early modern witch accusations in Scotland, and a FutureLearn case study on Lanarkshire places the county inside the national witch-hunt of 1649–50. That period formed part of one of the great Scottish witch-hunt peaks identified by historian Christina Larner, when more than 500 people were investigated across Scotland.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://witches.hca.ed.ac.uk/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: witches.hca.ed.ac.uk">[Witches]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">witches.hca.ed.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on ed.ac.uk.</span></span></span>

The records matter because they show how folklore, religion, law and fear overlapped. In seventeenth-century Lanarkshire, accusations did not usually begin as grand tales of broomsticks or storybook covens. They often started in kirk sessions and presbyteries, where complaints about neighbours, healing, malefice, charms, suspicious illness, or supposed contact with devils and spirits could be formalised into legal danger. The Presbytery of Lanark included parishes such as Carluke, Carmichael, Carnwath, Carstairs, Crawford, Douglas, Lanark and Lesmahagow.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.futurelearn.com/info/courses/ems-palaeography/0/steps/48444" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: futurelearn.com">[FutureLearn]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">futurelearn.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Future Learn Witch-hunting in Lanarkshire: part 1</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Future Learn Witch-hunting in Lanarkshire: part 1</span></span></span>

For folklore readers, the important distinction is between belief and prosecution. Ordinary people might have believed in charms, uncanny harm, fairies, blessings, second sight or healing knowledge; authorities increasingly forced such beliefs into the harsher legal language of witchcraft and diabolism. The result is that Lanarkshire’s witch-lore is partly preserved through hostile records. These sources tell us what accusers, ministers and courts chose to write down, not necessarily the full texture of everyday belief.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://doc.ukdataservice.ac.uk/doc/4667/mrdoc/pdf/guide.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: doc.ukdataservice.ac.uk">[UK Data Service]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">doc.ukdataservice.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">UK Data Service Survey of Scottish Witchcraft</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UK Data Service Survey of Scottish Witchcraft</span></span></span>

This is why modern retellings should be careful. Lanarkshire had accused witches, suspected healers and a place in national witch-hunting history, but the surviving evidence should not be flattened into Halloween caricature. It is a record of social fear, religious pressure and local conflict, with women disproportionately targeted: the Scottish witchcraft evidence cited in the Lanarkshire case study notes that 84% of those accused nationally were women.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.futurelearn.com/info/courses/ems-palaeography/0/steps/48444" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: futurelearn.com">[FutureLearn]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">futurelearn.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Future Learn Witch-hunting in Lanarkshire: part 1</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Future Learn Witch-hunting in Lanarkshire: part 1</span></span></span>

## Lanark’s living customs

Lanark is one of the best places in the county to see custom rather than just read about legend. Two traditions stand out: Whuppity Scoorie in March and the Lanimers in June. Both are public, local and strongly tied to the old royal burgh.

Whuppity Scoorie takes place at Lanark Cross. In the modern version, children gather at the Town Steeple at 6pm on 1 March, each carrying a ball of paper on a string. When the small bell rings, they run three times clockwise around the church, swinging the paper balls above their heads, before a scramble for small change. Local accounts are honest about the uncertainty: the custom’s exact origin and age are unlikely ever to be conclusively proven.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.lanark.co.uk/history/ancient-traditions/whuppity-scoorie" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: lanark.co.uk">[Discover Lanark]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">lanark.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on lanark.co.uk.</span></span></span>

That uncertainty is part of its appeal. Interpretations include a spring ritual to say goodbye to winter, a celebration of lighter evenings after the bell had been silent through winter darkness, a rougher nineteenth-century youth custom, or a much older ceremony later reshaped by children’s play. Recent reporting has also highlighted the custom’s modern role as an intergenerational community event, not just a quaint survival.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.lanark.co.uk/history/ancient-traditions/whuppity-scoorie" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: lanark.co.uk">[Discover Lanark]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">lanark.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on lanark.co.uk.</span></span></span>

The Lanimers are a different kind of tradition: civic, ceremonial and bound to land rights. Lanark Lanimers explains that Lanark’s royal burgh charter under David I required the burgesses to inspect the town’s march or boundary stones each year. That duty of checking the Land Marches became an annual festival, with the Lord Cornet, processions, horses, schoolchildren, a Lanimer Queen and the Perambulation of the Marches.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.lanarklanimers.co.uk/what-is-lanimers/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: lanarklanimers.co.uk">[lanarklanimers.co.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">lanarklanimers.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">What Is Lanimers?</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">What Is Lanimers?</span></span></span>

Together, Whuppity Scoorie and Lanimers show how Lanarkshire folklore can be practical rather than fantastical. These customs are about bells, boundaries, children, civic memory, weather, daylight and belonging. They are folklore because they are inherited, performed and locally meaningful, even where their earliest origins are debated.

## Tinto Hill and fire on the skyline

Tinto Hill is one of Lanarkshire’s great folklore landmarks because it is visible, isolated and memorable. The hill rises near Biggar and Thankerton, and its summit cairn has long attracted both archaeological and legendary interest. Older lore links Tinto with fire: J. M. Mackinlay’s *Folklore of Scottish Lochs and Springs* states that Beltane fires were associated with the Lanarkshire hills of Tinto and Dechmont, and says Tinto was used for beacon-fires as well as for nature-festival fires.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/files/56034/56034-h/56034-h.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Project Gutenberg Folklore of Scottish Lochs and Springs</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Project Gutenberg Folklore of Scottish Lochs and Springs</span></span></span>

Tinto also has the kind of treasure-rhyme that often clings to conspicuous hills. A Scots rhyme tells of a mist, a chest, a cup and a drop on “Tintock tap”. Such rhymes do not prove hidden treasure, but they reveal how people imagined the hilltop as a place where weather, danger and reward might meet.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tinto" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wikipedia.org.</span></span></span>

Another strand is literary folklore. The History Press retells “The Spirit of Tinto Hill”, adapted from Sir Alexander Boswell’s 1803 ballad “The Spirit of Tintoc, or Johnie Bell and the Kelpie”. This is a useful example of the boundary between oral legend and literary shaping: the tale uses local landscape and supernatural motifs, but its known form is strongly connected with a named antiquarian-era writer.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://thehistorypress.co.uk/article/the-spirit-of-tinto-hill-a-story-from-lanarkshire/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: thehistorypress.co.uk">[The History Press]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">thehistorypress.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">the spirit of tinto hill a story from lanarkshire</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">the spirit of tinto hill a story from lanarkshire</span></span></span>

For readers exploring Lanarkshire, Tinto is a reminder that folklore often begins with a landmark that demands explanation. The name, the red rock, the beacon associations, the cairn, the rhymes and the wide view all make the hill feel storied, even when different explanations compete.

## Stones, old places and local explanations

Lanarkshire has many prehistoric and early historic sites, but their folklore is often fragmentary. Standing stones, cairns and ancient tracks were frequently explained in later tradition as graves, battle memorials, Druidical remains, petrified people, or traces of vanished giants. Modern archaeology asks different questions, but local folklore tells us how later communities made sense of strange old objects in fields and on ridges.

At Glassford, the Glesart Stanes are a good example. Nineteenth-century descriptions variously interpreted the three upright stones as ancient remains, memorials of noblemen, martyrs, or remnants of “Druidical” superstition. A modern local interpretation notes that the stones still remain mysterious, surrounded by later burial slabs and yew trees.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.thenorthernantiquarian.org/2018/12/09/glesart-stanes/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: thenorthernantiquarian.org">[thenorthernantiquarian.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">thenorthernantiquarian.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Glesart Stanes, Glassford, Lanarkshire</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Glesart Stanes, Glassford, Lanarkshire</span></span></span>

At Lesmahagow and Carluke, records of destroyed or displaced standing stones show how much of this landscape folklore has been lost with agricultural improvement, quarrying, building and road changes. The Northern Antiquarian’s Lanarkshire entries preserve references to lost stones, wells and traditions from sources such as the *New Statistical Account*, Robert Chambers and early archaeological reports. Such material is valuable, but it should be read as a layered record: antiquarian interpretation, local memory and archaeological fact are not always the same thing.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.thenorthernantiquarian.org/2023/01/21/cairney-mount/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: thenorthernantiquarian.org">[thenorthernantiquarian.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">thenorthernantiquarian.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">cairney mount</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">cairney mount</span></span></span>

The payoff is not a simple claim that every stone has a surviving legend. In Lanarkshire, the more honest pattern is one of partial survival. Some stories remain vivid; others survive only as names like Standing Stone Well, Three Stanes, Ladywell Road, or St Patrick’s Well. Place-names become the county’s folklore archive.

## Ghosts, Covenanters and remembered violence

Lanarkshire’s haunted and tragic traditions often attach to moments of conflict. The county was deeply marked by Covenanting history, including the Battle of Drumclog and the Battle of Bothwell Bridge in 1679. Wikishire notes that Lanarkshire gave constant support to the Covenanters, who defeated Claverhouse at Drumclog but later suffered defeat at Bothwell Brig.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://wikishire.co.uk/wiki/Lanarkshire" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wikishire.co.uk">[Wikishire]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wikishire.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Wikishire Lanarkshire</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wikishire Lanarkshire</span></span></span>

One compact example is Trumpeter’s Well at Strathaven. Local folklore says the well gained its name after the Battle of Drumclog, when John Graham of Claverhouse, retreating after his horse had been killed, stole the horse of his young trumpeter; the boy was then killed and his body thrown into the well. The tale is morally charged, casting Claverhouse as cowardly and the trumpeter as victim. Whether every detail is historical is less important than how the story fixes political memory to a named water-source.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.thenorthernantiquarian.org/2020/07/02/trumpeters-well/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: thenorthernantiquarian.org">[thenorthernantiquarian.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">thenorthernantiquarian.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">trumpeters well</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">trumpeters well</span></span></span>

This is a recurring Lanarkshire pattern: ghost stories and tragic legends often act as emotional footnotes to real historical upheaval. Battlefields, wells, ruined castles, old inns and roads become places where the past is imagined as still unsettled. In that sense, Lanarkshire’s ghost-lore is not separate from its history; it is one way local communities remember injustice, danger and loyalty.

## Fairies, kelpies and the wider Scottish imagination

Lanarkshire is not as strongly identified with fairy hills as parts of the Highlands, Perthshire or the Borders, but fairy belief still matters as a background to Lowland Scottish folklore. Witchcraft records across Scotland show how talk of fairies, healing knowledge and uncanny assistance could be pulled into accusations of sorcery. In Lanarkshire, the evidence is often indirect rather than a neat collection of named fairy tales.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.spookyisles.com/the-dark-side-of-magic-witches-and-the-sidhe/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: spookyisles.com">[Spooky Isles]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">spookyisles.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Spooky Isles The Dark Side Of Magic: Witches And The Sidhe</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Spooky Isles The Dark Side Of Magic: Witches And The Sidhe</span></span></span>

Water spirits also enter the county through literary and regional retellings. “The Spirit of Tinto Hill” brings in a kelpie, the dangerous Scottish water-horse familiar from wider national folklore. The famous modern Kelpies sculptures are outside Lanarkshire at Falkirk, but they connect back to Lanarkshire identity through the Clydesdale horse, the heavy draught breed founded in Lanarkshire’s Clydesdale district.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://thehistorypress.co.uk/article/scottish-storytelling-tradition-the-devil-and-the-minister/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: thehistorypress.co.uk">[thehistorypress.co.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">thehistorypress.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">scottish storytelling tradition the devil and the minister</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">scottish storytelling tradition the devil and the minister</span></span></span>

That connection is more than decorative. Clydesdales were working animals of agriculture, haulage and industry, and the breed’s later symbolic life turns Lanarkshire labour history into a kind of modern folk image: the mighty horse of the Clyde valley transformed into public sculpture, parade animal and Scottish icon. The Clydesdale Horse Society describes the breed as native to Scotland and founded in Lanarkshire, with its development dating from the mid-eighteenth century.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://clydesdalehorsesociety.com/breed-history" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: clydesdalehorsesociety.com">[Clydesdale Horse Society]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">clydesdalehorsesociety.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on clydesdalehorsesociety.com.</span></span></span>

## What is old tradition, and what is later retelling?

A good Lanarkshire folklore page needs to separate several layers. Some material is medieval or early modern in origin, some is preserved in nineteenth-century antiquarian books, some is civic custom maintained by committees, and some is modern creative retelling.

St Mungo’s cult and Glasgow Cathedral belong to medieval religious history, though modern interpretation continues to reshape how visitors understand the site. Witchcraft material is early modern, but preserved through legal and church records written by authorities. Whuppity Scoorie has uncertain roots, with the strongest written trail in the Hamilton Advertiser and local historical writing. Tinto’s “Spirit” tale is connected to an 1803 ballad by Sir Alexander Boswell rather than being a purely anonymous oral survival.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.historicenvironment.scot/visit/all/glasgow-cathedral/history-and-stories/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: historicenvironment.scot">[historicenvironment.scot]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">historicenvironment.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on historicenvironment.scot.</span></span></span>

That does not make later retellings worthless. Folklore is not only ancient material. It is also the process by which communities keep stories, customs and places meaningful. A Lanark child swinging a paper ball at Whuppity Scoorie, a family watching the Lanimer Queen, a visitor standing at St Mungo’s Well, or a walker repeating the Tinto treasure rhyme are all taking part in the county’s continuing story culture.

## Why Lanarkshire folklore matters

Lanarkshire’s folklore matters because it refuses to fit a single romantic picture of Scotland. It is not only Highland fairies, island second sight or ruined castles. It is Lowland, civic, industrial, riverine and stubbornly local. It belongs to market burghs, parish records, chapel wells, working horses, boundary stones, children’s games and hilltop fires.

The county’s strongest traditions also show how folklore holds together things that official history often separates. Religion and water meet at St Mungo’s Well and Motherwell’s Lady Well. Law, fear and belief meet in the witchcraft records. Land rights become festival in the Lanimers. Seasonal change becomes children’s ritual in Whuppity Scoorie. Geology, beacon-fire and treasure rhyme meet on Tinto Hill.

For a modern reader, Lanarkshire is therefore a county of thresholds: between sacred and ordinary water, old county and modern council, rural Clydesdale and industrial Glasgow, legal record and oral belief, history and performance. Its folklore survives not as one grand myth, but as a network of named places where people have repeatedly asked the same questions: what happened here, why does this place matter, and what story should we carry forward?
