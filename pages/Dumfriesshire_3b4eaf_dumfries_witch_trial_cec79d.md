## Introduction

Dumfries witch lore is not only a matter of eerie fireside stories. In this part of [Dumfriesshire]({{ 'where-dumfriesshires-strange-stories/' | relative_url }}), witch belief passed through kirk sessions, burgh accounts, presbytery minutes and courts, leaving records of suspicion, neighbourly fear, legal punishment and everyday protection. The starkest event was the execution of nine women on the Sands of Dumfries in April 1659, after a trial in which ten women were brought before commissioners and nine were condemned to be strangled and burned. J. Maxwell Wood’s 1911 collection preserves extracts from the High Court of Justiciary and local church records, while the University of Edinburgh’s Survey of Scottish Witchcraft places such cases within a wider national database of nearly 4,000 accused people.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/files/43966/43966-h/43966-h.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Witchcraft and Superstitious Record in the South-Western District of Scotland, by J. Maxwell Wood—A Project Gutenberg eBook...</span></span></span>



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/Dumfriesshire_3b4eaf_dumfries_witch_trial_cec79d-overview.webp" | relative_url }}" alt="Overview image for Dumfries Witch Lore" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

What makes the Dumfries material so powerful for folklore is the way it joins two worlds that are often kept apart: official punishment and domestic folk belief. The same culture that could send accused women to execution also used rowan branches, red thread, horseshoes, salt, running water and divination rites to protect cattle, homes and neighbours from supposed witchcraft. In Dumfriesshire, “witch stories” became court records, and ordinary protective customs became evidence of fear, discipline and survival.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/files/43966/43966-h/43966-h.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Witchcraft and Superstitious Record in the South-Western District of Scotland, by J. Maxwell Wood—A Project Gutenberg eBook...</span></span></span>

## The 1659 burnings on the Sands of Dumfries

The central Dumfries witch-trial memory is the mass execution of 13 April 1659 on the Sands of Dumfries. Wood’s account, drawn from High Court of Justiciary material, says the court opened at Dumfries on 2 April 1659 before commissioners in criminal cases, and that ten women were tried over proceedings lasting until 5 April. One of them, Helen Tait, escaped conviction because the indictment was not clearly proved, though even she had to find security for good behaviour and banish herself from the parish. The other nine were condemned.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/files/43966/43966-h/43966-h.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Witchcraft and Superstitious Record in the South-Western District of Scotland, by J. Maxwell Wood—A Project Gutenberg eBook...</span></span></span>

The sentence is chilling because it is so administrative. The named women were Agnes Comenes, Janet M‘Gowane, Jean Tomson, Margaret Clerk, Janet M‘Kendrig, Agnes Clerk, Janet Corsane, Helen Moorhead and Janet Callon. The court ordered them to be taken to the ordinary place of execution for the burgh between two and four in the afternoon, strangled at stakes until dead, and then burned to ashes, with their movable goods forfeited.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/files/43966/43966-h/43966-h.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Witchcraft and Superstitious Record in the South-Western District of Scotland, by J. Maxwell Wood—A Project Gutenberg eBook...</span></span></span>

That detail also corrects a common misconception. In Scottish witchcraft executions, the usual pattern was not simply “burning alive”; English Heritage’s discussion of witchcraft myths notes that in Scotland witches’ bodies were burned after they had first been strangled. The Dumfries sentence fits that wider Scottish legal practice, even though later local memory often compresses the event into the phrase “burning of the nine women”.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.english-heritage.org.uk/learn/histories/eight-witchcraft-myths/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: english-heritage.org.uk">[English Heritage]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">english-heritage.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">English Heritage Witchcraft: Eight Myths and Misconceptions &#124; English Heritage</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">English Heritage Witchcraft: Eight Myths and Misconceptions &#124; English Heritage</span></span></span>

The kirk was not merely a background presence. Wood records that Dumfries Kirk-Session had already resolved in 1658 that the minister should ask from the pulpit for evidence against anyone suspected of “the heinous and abominable sin of witchcraft”, while also warning that baseless accusers could be disciplined. After the April 1659 convictions, Dumfries Presbytery appointed named ministers to attend the condemned women before execution and to assist on the day itself.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/files/43966/43966-h/43966-h.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Witchcraft and Superstitious Record in the South-Western District of Scotland, by J. Maxwell Wood—A Project Gutenberg eBook...</span></span></span>

For a modern reader, the human shock of the 1659 case lies in this combination: public fear, church involvement, court procedure and bodily punishment all converged in one riverside place. The Sands, or Whitesands, is now part of the familiar townscape beside the Nith, but in local witch-trial memory it remains attached to the moment when suspicion became a scheduled civic event.

## Why Dumfries witchcraft was about neighbours, cattle and daily risk

Dumfries witch belief was not mainly about theatrical sabbaths or elaborate occult systems. The regional pattern in south-west Scotland was often much more domestic and local: quarrels, sickness, livestock losses, failed butter-making, theft, cursing, suspected malice and attempts to explain why harm had fallen on a household. Future Museum’s exhibition on witchcraft and lore in south-west Scotland notes that accusations in the region were strongly concerned with agriculture, family and neighbours, including alleged killing of cattle, crop damage, livestock bewitchment and curses causing death.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.futuremuseum.co.uk/collections/features/online-exhibitions/witchcraft-and-lore-in-south-west-scotland" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: futuremuseum.co.uk">[Future Museum]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">futuremuseum.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Future Museum Witchcraft and Lore in South West Scotland</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Future Museum Witchcraft and Lore in South West Scotland</span></span></span>

This matters because it changes how the folklore should be read. A Dumfries witch accusation was rarely just a fantasy about a sinister outsider. It often grew from close social contact: someone refused charity, a neighbour spoke angrily, a cow failed, a child sickened, butter would not come in the churn, or a suspected person was believed to have an uncanny reputation. The belief system gave ordinary misfortune a human target.

Wood’s examples repeatedly show this everyday texture. In one traditional Sanquhar story, a minister’s servant crossed the Nith with a churn in the hope that running water would break a witch’s spell; rowan was fixed in the byre and a horseshoe nailed behind the door, but the tale says these measures failed until the minister’s wife intervened. Whether taken as oral tradition rather than court fact, the story shows what people thought witchcraft threatened: milk, cows, domestic work and household security.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/files/43966/43966-h/43966-h.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Witchcraft and Superstitious Record in the South-Western District of Scotland, by J. Maxwell Wood—A Project Gutenberg eBook...</span></span></span>

The south-western record is also notable for what it often lacks. Future Museum observes that surviving trial material from south-west Scotland contains relatively little emphasis on the Devil or demonology compared with some continental witchcraft traditions. Local anxieties mattered more: in fishing communities witches might be blamed for lost fish, while in agricultural communities they were imagined as harming crops and livestock.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.futuremuseum.co.uk/collections/features/online-exhibitions/witchcraft-and-lore-in-south-west-scotland" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: futuremuseum.co.uk">[Future Museum]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">futuremuseum.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Future Museum Witchcraft and Lore in South West Scotland</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Future Museum Witchcraft and Lore in South West Scotland</span></span></span>

That does not make the accusations harmless. The very ordinariness of the fears made them dangerous. A bad harvest or dead animal could become a moral accusation; a disliked woman’s words could become evidence; a protective charm could become proof that a household thought itself under attack.

## Later accusations and surviving records

The 1659 executions were not the end of Dumfries-area witchcraft records. Wood gives a 1664 Dumfries Town Council edict concerning Janet Burnes, described as commonly reputed a witch and accused of cheating people by pretending knowledge of past and future events. The council ordered that no inhabitant should receive her or give her food or drink after she had returned despite earlier banishment.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/files/43966/43966-h/43966-h.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Witchcraft and Superstitious Record in the South-Western District of Scotland, by J. Maxwell Wood—A Project Gutenberg eBook...</span></span></span>

In 1671, a Court of Justiciary sitting in the Tolbooth of Dumfries issued a warrant for the execution of Janet Muldritche and Elspeth Thomsone, who had been found guilty of witchcraft and sentenced to be “worried” at a stake until dead and then burned to ashes. Henderson’s study of witchcraft prosecutions in south-west Scotland later interprets those fatal cases as arising from comparatively minor incidents involving cattle grazing, alleged theft of fodder and family or neighbour tensions, followed by unexplained deaths.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/files/43966/43966-h/43966-h.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Witchcraft and Superstitious Record in the South-Western District of Scotland, by J. Maxwell Wood—A Project Gutenberg eBook...</span></span></span>

The 1690s show a region where belief remained strong, but official responses were becoming more uneven. Wood records a 1692 Dumfries Presbytery case in which several women from Blackshaw, Locherwood and Mouswaldbank were suspected of witchcraft and accused of repeated harmful acts against neighbours. Local men apprehended them and sought a commission for trial, but the Privy Council ordered the women to be transported to Edinburgh rather than simply allowing local punishment to proceed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/files/43966/43966-h/43966-h.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Witchcraft and Superstitious Record in the South-Western District of Scotland, by J. Maxwell Wood—A Project Gutenberg eBook...</span></span></span>

By the early eighteenth century, the legal climate had shifted further. Scotland’s People states that the last person prosecuted before the Lords of Justiciary for witchcraft was Elspeth Rule, tried at Dumfries Circuit on 3 May 1709, burned on the cheek and banished from Scotland for life. The same source explains that growing judicial scepticism, doubts about torture-derived confessions and suspicion of fraudulent “pricking” helped weaken prosecutions before the Witchcraft Act was repealed in 1736.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.scotlandspeople.gov.uk/article/tales-witchcraft-witch-hunting-kirkcaldy-kirk-session-minutes" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: scotlandspeople.gov.uk">[scotlandspeople.gov.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">scotlandspeople.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on scotlandspeople.gov.uk.</span></span></span>

Elspeth Rule is important for Dumfries memory because her case stands near the end of Scottish high-court witchcraft prosecution. Wood also describes her as the accused in the last trial for witchcraft in Scotland by the Court of Justiciary, though modern summaries are careful to distinguish that from later local prosecutions and from the debated final execution traditions elsewhere in Scotland.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/files/43966/43966-h/43966-h.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Witchcraft and Superstitious Record in the South-Western District of Scotland, by J. Maxwell Wood—A Project Gutenberg eBook...</span></span></span>

## Charms, suspicion and folk protection

Folk protection in Dumfriesshire was not a decorative add-on to witch stories. It was part of the same belief-world that made accusation plausible. If neighbours believed witchcraft could sour milk, kill cattle, injure children or bring bad luck, they also believed that certain materials and actions could resist it.

Wood’s south-west Scottish examples give a compact picture of the protective toolkit:

* **Rowan and red thread:** Rowan, or mountain ash, was treated as a strong defence against witchcraft. Branches could be plaited and fixed above byre doors to protect cows, while young women wore rowan berries on red thread. Wood records the rhyme “Rowan tree and red threid” as part of this belief.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/files/43966/43966-h/43966-h.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Witchcraft and Superstitious Record in the South-Western District of Scotland, by J. Maxwell Wood—A Project Gutenberg eBook...</span></span></span>
* **Byre and stable protection:** Rowan could be bent into a cow’s tail, horseshoes nailed over stable or byre doors, and naturally holed stones fixed above stable doors as protective objects.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/files/43966/43966-h/43966-h.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Witchcraft and Superstitious Record in the South-Western District of Scotland, by J. Maxwell Wood—A Project Gutenberg eBook...</span></span></span>
* **Salt and dairy work:** Salt was believed to resist evil, especially in churning, where a handful might be added to the cream before the work began.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/files/43966/43966-h/43966-h.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Witchcraft and Superstitious Record in the South-Western District of Scotland, by J. Maxwell Wood—A Project Gutenberg eBook...</span></span></span>
* **Gestures against the evil eye:** Wood records the practice of putting the thumb into the palm and closing the fingers over it when passing the hut of a suspected old woman, interpreting it as a relic of the sign of the cross.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/files/43966/43966-h/43966-h.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Witchcraft and Superstitious Record in the South-Western District of Scotland, by J. Maxwell Wood—A Project Gutenberg eBook...</span></span></span>
* **Divination to find thieves:** The Caerlaverock case of John Fergusson in 1697 shows a different kind of protection: not warding off witchcraft, but using a Bible and key to identify a supposed thief. The presbytery treated this as scandalous charming and ordered public rebuke.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/files/43966/43966-h/43966-h.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Witchcraft and Superstitious Record in the South-Western District of Scotland, by J. Maxwell Wood—A Project Gutenberg eBook...</span></span></span>

These practices were not all understood in the same way. Some were protective, some curative, some divinatory and some sat uneasily between folk custom and church discipline. National Museums Scotland notes that before modern medicine and easy access to doctors, people used charms to cure and ward off illness, because misfortune was often thought to have supernatural as well as natural causes and belief in witchcraft and the evil eye was deeply rooted.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nms.ac.uk/discover-catalogue/from-amulets-to-elf-bolts-10-scottish-charms" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nms.ac.uk">[National Museums Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nms.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nms.ac.uk.</span></span></span>

The key point is that folk protection was not necessarily “witchcraft” in the minds of those using it. A household might see rowan, salt or a horseshoe as sensible defence, while a kirk session might see certain charms, divination rites or spoken formulas as dangerous superstition. The boundary between protection and offence was unstable.

## Cunning folk, healers and the risk of helping

The Dumfries material also warns against a simple story in which all accused witches were merely healers or midwives. That popular claim is too broad. English Heritage cautions that the idea of witch-hunts as persecution of goddess-worshipping herbalist midwives is a modern myth, and that midwives were not the typical accused group in English material.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.english-heritage.org.uk/learn/histories/eight-witchcraft-myths/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: english-heritage.org.uk">[English Heritage]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">english-heritage.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">English Heritage Witchcraft: Eight Myths and Misconceptions &#124; English Heritage</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">English Heritage Witchcraft: Eight Myths and Misconceptions &#124; English Heritage</span></span></span>

Yet it is also true that folk healing could be risky. The RCN Foundation-backed “Witches’ Stories” project, based on secondary analysis of the Survey of Scottish Witchcraft, focused on accused people whose cases involved folk healing or midwifery practices, and notes that records reflect what judges and investigators considered important, not neutral biographies of the accused. It also records that sleep deprivation was common and that some accused people were tortured to obtain confessions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.rcn.org.uk/-/media/RCN-Foundation/Documents/Witches-Project---Witch-List.pdf?hash=61AEA542FDCF3949860D7957700A93D5&amp;la=en" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: rcn.org.uk">[The Royal College of Nursing]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">rcn.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Royal College of Nursing</span></span></span>

For Dumfriesshire and the wider south-west, the danger lay in ambiguity. A person who knew charms for animals, claimed to lift sickness, offered divination, or had a reputation for uncanny knowledge might be useful in one context and terrifying in another. Henderson’s study points to Bessie Paine’s case as involving charming and folk remedies for animals and humans, mixed with accusations of harmful magic; she might have faced execution had she not died in prison.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://eprints.gla.ac.uk/7706/1/Lizanne2.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: eprints.gla.ac.uk">[Enlighten Publications]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">eprints.gla.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on gla.ac.uk.</span></span></span>

That ambiguity is one reason Dumfries witch lore has such lasting force. The accused were not always imagined as distant monsters. They could be neighbours, healers, poor women, quarrelsome figures, elderly people living alone, or people believed to have powers that others both feared and sought.

## How Dumfries remembers the trials today

Modern Dumfries witch-trial memory tends to gather around three images: the nine women on the Sands in 1659, the survival of court and kirk records, and the folk practices used to guard homes and animals. This is a more grounded form of supernatural heritage than a simple ghost story, because the records show how belief moved through institutions as well as oral tradition.

The University of Edinburgh’s Survey of Scottish Witchcraft is especially valuable here because it keeps Dumfries cases inside the broader Scottish pattern rather than isolating them as local curiosities. It records accused people, trial processes, places, themes and outcomes across Scotland, allowing readers to see that prosecutions often came in bursts in particular localities.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://witches.hca.ed.ac.uk/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: witches.hca.ed.ac.uk">[Witches]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">witches.hca.ed.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Witches The Survey of Scottish Witchcraft</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Witches The Survey of Scottish Witchcraft</span></span></span>

At the same time, Wood’s book remains central to local storytelling because it preserves the flavour of Dumfries and south-west Scottish records: burgh payments for execution materials, kirk-session rebukes, presbytery anxieties, charms in byres, Bible-and-key divination, rowan branches, horseshoes and old tales of suspected women. It must be read critically as an early twentieth-century antiquarian compilation, but it preserves extracts and traditions that still shape how the county’s witch lore is retold.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/files/43966/43966-h/43966-h.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Witchcraft and Superstitious Record in the South-Western District of Scotland, by J. Maxwell Wood—A Project Gutenberg eBook...</span></span></span>

The strongest modern reading is therefore neither sensational nor dismissive. Dumfries witch lore matters because it shows how supernatural belief could organise real social action: neighbours watched one another, churches demanded evidence, courts punished bodies, and families tried to protect milk, cattle, children and thresholds. In Dumfriesshire folklore, the witch was not only a figure of night rides and curses. She was also a legal category, a neighbourly fear, a household risk and a memory fixed to the town’s own ground.
