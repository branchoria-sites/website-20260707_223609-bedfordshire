## Where County Antrim folklore belongs on the map

Historic County Antrim lies in north-east Ulster, with the sea to the north and east, the River Bann and Lough Neagh to the west, and the Lagan and Belfast Lough to the south. Belfast is partly within the historic county, while the north coast, the Antrim Hills, the Nine Glens, Larne, Ballycastle, Bushmills, Dunluce and Rathlin Island give the county much of its legendary geography. Modern Northern Ireland local government no longer works by the old county system, but the historic county remains a strong cultural and geographical identity, especially for folklore, tourism and local history.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://abcounties.com/counties/county-profiles/county_antrim/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: abcounties.com">[Association of British Counties]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">abcounties.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Association of British Counties County Antrim &#124; Association of British Counties</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Association of British Counties County Antrim &#124; Association of British Counties</span></span></span>



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/County_Antrim_405799-overview.webp" | relative_url }}" alt="Overview image for County Antrim" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

That matters because Antrim’s traditions are not evenly spread. The coast and glens carry a different imaginative weight from the Lough Neagh shore or urban Belfast. The Antrim Coast and Glens Area of Outstanding Natural Beauty includes Rathlin Island, the Glens and the coastline from Ballycastle to Larne; its official description stresses the Antrim Plateau, post-glacial glens, maritime history, archaeological sites and settlement history. Those same features explain why Antrim stories so often concern thresholds: cliffs, caves, wells, roads across wet ground, hills with hidden dwellers, and ferry routes towards Scotland.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ccght.org/antrimcoastglensaonb/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ccght.org">[CCGHT]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ccght.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Antrim Coast &amp; Glens AONB</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Antrim Coast &amp; Glens AONB</span></span></span>

## Why the Giant’s Causeway became Antrim’s signature legend

The Giant’s Causeway is Antrim’s most internationally recognisable legend because it gives a striking natural formation a memorable human-scale story. In the familiar version, the Irish giant Finn McCool builds a causeway across the sea to confront the Scottish giant Benandonner; after their encounter, Benandonner tears up the crossing as he flees, leaving the broken basalt road on the Antrim coast. The National Trust also notes a local feature known as Finn McCool’s “giant boot” at Port Noffer, reinforcing how the wider landscape has been read through the giant story.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nationaltrust.org.uk/visit/northern-ireland/giants-causeway/history-of-giants-causeway" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nationaltrust.org.uk">[National Trust]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nationaltrust.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Trust Giant&#x27;s Causeway&#x27;s history &#124; County Antrim &#124; National Trust</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Trust Giant&#x27;s Causeway&#x27;s history &#124; County Antrim &#124; National Trust</span></span></span>

The geology tells a different kind of story without cancelling the folklore. The Giant’s Causeway and Causeway Coast World Heritage Site contains more than 40,000 basalt columns formed by volcanic activity and erosion around 60 million years ago; it was inscribed as Northern Ireland’s first World Heritage Site in 1986 for its natural heritage and geological value. For readers, the useful distinction is simple: the giant is a traditional explanation and cultural emblem, while the basalt is a geological record. The power of the site comes from the way both stories now sit together in public memory.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ccght.org/giantscausewaywhs/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ccght.org">[CCGHT]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ccght.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Giant’s Causeway World Heritage Site</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Giant’s Causeway World Heritage Site</span></span></span>

This is also a good example of how County Antrim folklore crosses the water. The tale points towards Scotland, and the county’s geography does the same. The Antrim coast faces the North Channel; the official AONB description notes the area’s long maritime history and Rathlin Island’s place in that coastal world. In folklore terms, the sea is not just a boundary. It is a route by which giants, saints, raiders, mermaids, migrants and stories move.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ccght.org/antrimcoastglensaonb/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ccght.org">[CCGHT]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ccght.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Antrim Coast &amp; Glens AONB</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Antrim Coast &amp; Glens AONB</span></span></span>

## Fairy Antrim: thorns, hills and “the Gentry”

Antrim’s fairy tradition is especially strong in the Glens and north coast. Local oral history collected by the Glens of Antrim Historical Society records memories of fairy thorns, or skeagh bushes, left standing because families believed it was dangerous to cut or disturb them. One informant described fairy belief as something “handed down” from older generations, while another recalled being warned away from a particular thorn bush and later linking its cutting with misfortune.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://antrimhistory.net/oral-history-project/superstitions-customs/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: antrimhistory.net">[antrimhistory.net]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">antrimhistory.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Superstitions &amp; Customs – Glens Of Antrim Historical Society</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Superstitions &amp; Customs – Glens Of Antrim Historical Society</span></span></span>

The best Antrim fairy places are not vague “enchanted forests” but named local features. A Causeway Coast and Glens folklore project records nineteenth-century testimony that fairies were not especially concentrated at the Giant’s Causeway itself, but were believed to have haunts between the Causeway, Knocklayde and Carrick-a-Rede. The same project describes the Glens of Antrim as especially renowned as the home of “the Gentry”, a respectful local name for fairy beings, and links Breen in Glenshesk with a place-name interpreted as a fairy dwelling.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://niarchive.org/wp-content/uploads/2019/07/Lore-of-the-Land-web-ready.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: niarchive.org">[niarchive.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">niarchive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Lore of the Land web ready</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Lore of the Land web ready</span></span></span>

Tiveragh Hill, between Glenaan and Glenballyeamon near Cushendall, is one of the county’s clearest fairy-landscape anchors. The folklore project describes it as a major fairy dwelling, associated with fairy thorns, strange lights and sounds, and an underground otherworld. A local poem calls it “The Fairy Hill” and says nobody goes there at night. This is exactly the kind of place where Antrim folklore turns landscape into behaviour: people avoid the hill after dark, leave trees alone, or explain unusual lights and sounds through inherited story.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://niarchive.org/wp-content/uploads/2019/07/Lore-of-the-Land-web-ready.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: niarchive.org">[niarchive.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">niarchive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Lore of the Land web ready</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Lore of the Land web ready</span></span></span>

The stories also show that fairies were not simply cute or harmless. In the same collected material, fairy beings disrupt houses, object to buildings placed across their hidden routes, complain that farm waste is running down their chimney, and punish interference with their dwellings. These traditions preserve practical rural anxieties about land use, luck, cattle, milk, boundaries and neighbourly caution. They also explain why “fairy road” and “fairy thorn” stories remain powerful: they are not just supernatural tales, but rules about how to live carefully in a place.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://niarchive.org/wp-content/uploads/2019/07/Lore-of-the-Land-web-ready.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: niarchive.org">[niarchive.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">niarchive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Lore of the Land web ready</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Lore of the Land web ready</span></span></span>

## Haunted roads, lakes and houses

Antrim ghost lore is strongly attached to roads, water and sudden death. Loughareema, the Vanishing Lake in the Glens of Antrim, is a good example. The lake fills from underground springs and can appear or disappear, which gave it a natural strangeness before any ghost story was attached to it. A folklore account says that a nineteenth-century road was built across the lake bed and that tragic incidents there contributed to ghost sightings in the area.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://niarchive.org/wp-content/uploads/2019/07/Lore-of-the-Land-web-ready.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: niarchive.org">[niarchive.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">niarchive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Lore of the Land web ready</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Lore of the Land web ready</span></span></span>

One well-attested local version links Loughareema to the death of Colonel John Magee McNeill and his coachman David McNeill in 1898, when they attempted to cross as the waters were rising. The account describes the horses panicking, the men and horses drowning, and later tradition saying their ghosts still haunt the lough. A separate oral version remembers a breadman who ignored warnings not to cross in winter weather and was never seen alive again, with his ghost also said to appear at the site.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://niarchive.org/wp-content/uploads/2019/07/Lore-of-the-Land-web-ready.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: niarchive.org">[niarchive.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">niarchive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Lore of the Land web ready</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Lore of the Land web ready</span></span></span>

That pattern is typical of local ghost tradition. A death, accident or frightening landscape feature becomes the hook on which later sightings hang. The same Causeway Coast and Glens collection includes the story of Dr Aaron Westall, a late nineteenth-century North Antrim doctor and golfer, whose friends later believed they heard his voice and the sound of a golf shot after his fatal accident. The point is not that a reader must accept the apparition as fact, but that Antrim ghost stories often preserve the emotional shock of an unfinished journey, game or duty.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://niarchive.org/wp-content/uploads/2019/07/Lore-of-the-Land-web-ready.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: niarchive.org">[niarchive.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">niarchive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Lore of the Land web ready</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Lore of the Land web ready</span></span></span>

## Witches, hare-shapes and folk blame

Antrim witch lore is less about formal witch trials and more about local suspicion, misfortune and shape-shifting. A striking example comes from Limavallaghan on the Cloughwater, where Jock Smylie told Sam Henry of a woman known as Peggy W. who was said to turn into a hare. The collected account gives the charm associated with the transformation and says that, in hare form, she could only be caught by a black greyhound bitch or wounded with special shot.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://niarchive.org/wp-content/uploads/2019/07/Lore-of-the-Land-web-ready.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: niarchive.org">[niarchive.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">niarchive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Lore of the Land web ready</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Lore of the Land web ready</span></span></span>

The hare-witch story belongs to a wider Irish and Ulster motif in which an animal wounded in the field is later linked to an injured woman at home. In one Antrim version, the pursued hare enters a byre and is then found again as a woman picking shot from her ankles; another memory says that after a hare was shot, an old woman in black was later seen lame with a sore leg. Such stories are best read as folklore about suspicion and explanation: when milk failed, animals sickened, or luck turned bad, a community might attach blame to a marginal or feared person.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://niarchive.org/wp-content/uploads/2019/07/Lore-of-the-Land-web-ready.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: niarchive.org">[niarchive.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">niarchive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Lore of the Land web ready</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Lore of the Land web ready</span></span></span>

The Witch of Glentow shows how older local legend can be remade for modern audiences. A 2025 Irish News report describes a short folk-horror film based on a north Antrim story from Glentow near Ballycastle, where folklore told of a woman murdered after being blamed for bringing supernatural misfortune on locals. The story had already been popularised in a poem by Glens of Antrim poet James Stoddard Moore, who died in 1903. This makes it a useful example of Antrim folklore moving from local tale to poem to contemporary screen adaptation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.irishnews.com/news/northern-ireland/us-film-festival-debut-for-ulster-scots-folk-horror-short-based-on-witch-of-glentow-tale-PP3XMGTYRBFDZJFRS4LC3SXR3Y/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: irishnews.com">[The Irish News]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">irishnews.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on irishnews.com.</span></span></span>

## Saints, wells and healing customs

County Antrim’s sacred folklore is not separate from its supernatural landscape. Slemish Mountain near Ballymena is promoted today as the legendary first known Irish home of Saint Patrick, where tradition says he tended animals during his captivity. The annual St Patrick’s Day climb keeps the mountain in public ritual use, so Slemish works both as a saint’s landscape and as a living seasonal custom.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://discovernorthernireland.com/listing/slemish-mountain/67347101/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: discovernorthernireland.com">[Discover Northern Ireland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">discovernorthernireland.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Discover Northern Ireland Slemish Mountain</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Discover Northern Ireland Slemish Mountain</span></span></span>

Cranfield Church and Holy Well, on the Lough Neagh shore at Churchtown Point, gives a more intimate example. The site has the ruins of a thirteenth-century church, St Olcan’s shrine, and a holy well a few yards east of the church. Local heritage information records annual pilgrimages between May Eve and 29 June, amber pebbles from the well believed to hold healing power, protection traditions for childbirth, drowning, fire and burglary, and the continuing practice of tying rags to nearby trees and bushes.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://visitantrimandnewtownabbey.com/things-to-do/heritage-attractions/cranfield-church-holy-well/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: visitantrimandnewtownabbey.com">[Visit Antrim &amp; Newtownabbey]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">visitantrimandnewtownabbey.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cranfield Church and Holy Well - Visit Antrim &amp; Newtownabbey...</span></span></span>

These customs show how Antrim belief culture blends Christian devotion, older sacred-water practices and household protection. The important point for a modern reader is not to treat every well story as ancient in the same way. Some holy wells may preserve medieval devotion, some customs may be much later, and some details survive because local families kept doing them. The broader Irish context supports this mixture: holy wells are widely recognised as sacred places associated with long-held traditions, prayer and healing, often linked to Christian devotion from the medieval period while sometimes occupying older sacred landscapes.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.heritagecouncil.ie/content/files/Holy-Wells-of-Ireland.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: heritagecouncil.ie">[The Heritage Council]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">heritagecouncil.ie</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Heritage Council Holy wells in Ireland</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Heritage Council Holy wells in Ireland</span></span></span>

## Mermaids, caves and the sea-edge imagination

Antrim’s coast also carries mermaid and sea-being traditions. In the Causeway Coast and Glens folklore material, Sam Henry’s notes link merrows with the Irish north coast and the Scottish Hebrides, describing them as aquatic fairy beings. The same account connects Dunluce Castle with a mermaid figure, Liban, said to have lived at the bottom of Lough Neagh before haunting the coast and being caught off Larne in a Christianised version of the story.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://niarchive.org/wp-content/uploads/2019/07/Lore-of-the-Land-web-ready.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: niarchive.org">[niarchive.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">niarchive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Lore of the Land web ready</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Lore of the Land web ready</span></span></span>

Dunluce matters because it is already a dramatic place: a ruined castle on a cliff-edge, with caves and sea below. Folklore thrives where the physical setting seems to invite narrative. A cave beneath a castle can become a mermaid’s sea-palace; a dangerous road across a vanishing lake can become a ghost road; basalt columns can become a giant’s causeway. In Antrim, landscape is not background. It is the engine of the story.

## Banshees, charms and everyday belief

Some of Antrim’s most revealing folklore is not attached to famous tourist sites at all. Oral-history material from the Glens records banshee traditions in ordinary family terms: the banshee is said to be heard rather than easily seen, to call for certain families, and to warn of deaths even across distance. One account links a heard banshee with news of a family death in New Zealand, showing how old belief travelled through migration as well as staying in the Glens.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://antrimhistory.net/oral-history-project/superstitions-customs/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: antrimhistory.net">[antrimhistory.net]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">antrimhistory.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Superstitions &amp; Customs – Glens Of Antrim Historical Society</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Superstitions &amp; Customs – Glens Of Antrim Historical Society</span></span></span>

The same oral-history page records local cures and charms for ailments such as ringworm, warts, bleeding and shingles. These are not “magic” in a theatrical sense; they are part of a rural healing culture in which prayer, gesture, inherited words and trusted local specialists sat alongside practical care. One informant describes using human spittle and the sign of the cross for ringworm and warts, while another recalls people travelling between Antrim, Down, Tyrone and the Lough Neagh shore in search of particular cures.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://antrimhistory.net/oral-history-project/superstitions-customs/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: antrimhistory.net">[antrimhistory.net]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">antrimhistory.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Superstitions &amp; Customs – Glens Of Antrim Historical Society</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Superstitions &amp; Customs – Glens Of Antrim Historical Society</span></span></span>

These traditions are important because they stop Antrim folklore from becoming only a set of scenic legends. Folklore also lived in kitchens, sickrooms, byres, fields and family warnings. It shaped what people feared, whom they trusted, what they avoided, and how they explained misfortune before modern medicine, insurance and official record-keeping covered more of everyday life.

## How old and well-attested are Antrim’s traditions?

Antrim folklore has several layers of evidence. Some traditions were recorded in nineteenth-century antiquarian and literary collections, including the Fairy Annals of Ulster material reprinted from the Ulster Journal of Archaeology. Some were preserved by collectors such as Sam Henry and later community projects. Some survive as oral-history testimony gathered from people in the Glens. Others are modern heritage or tourism retellings, such as the Giant’s Causeway legend in visitor interpretation, or creative adaptations such as The Witch of Glentow film.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://niarchive.org/wp-content/uploads/2019/07/Lore-of-the-Land-web-ready.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: niarchive.org">[niarchive.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">niarchive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Lore of the Land web ready</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Lore of the Land web ready</span></span></span>

The evidence is therefore uneven, but not weak in the same way everywhere. The Giant’s Causeway legend is firmly embedded in public heritage interpretation, even though the geological explanation is clear. Fairy thorn and banshee traditions are well represented in local oral-history material. Tiveragh and Loughareema have strong place-specific folklore through community and heritage projects. Individual ghost sightings or named apparitions are harder to verify historically, but they still matter as traditions if they can be traced to local collection, newspapers, books or repeated oral accounts.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nationaltrust.org.uk/visit/northern-ireland/giants-causeway/history-of-giants-causeway" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nationaltrust.org.uk">[nationaltrust.org.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nationaltrust.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Trust Giant&#x27;s Causeway&#x27;s history &#124; County Antrim &#124; National Trust</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Trust Giant&#x27;s Causeway&#x27;s history &#124; County Antrim &#124; National Trust</span></span></span>

Modern readers should also notice what Antrim folklore is not. It is not a single ancient Celtic system preserved unchanged. It is a living mixture: Gaelic and Ulster-Scots speech, coastal contact with Scotland, Protestant and Catholic local memory, tourism, antiquarian collecting, family storytelling, landscape interpretation and modern media. That mixture is exactly what makes the county distinctive.

## Why County Antrim still feels folkloric today

Antrim’s folklore endures because the landscape still makes the stories legible. A visitor can understand why basalt columns invited a giant story, why an unpredictable lake invited ghost stories, why isolated hawthorns could become taboo, why a mountain associated with Patrick became a pilgrimage climb, and why a ruined cliff-top castle could gather mermaid and haunting traditions. The county’s official heritage bodies now manage much of this landscape through conservation, tourism and archaeological records; Northern Ireland’s Historic Environment Record, for example, holds information on thousands of archaeological sites and monuments, while local heritage trusts promote both natural and cultural landscape value.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.communities-ni.gov.uk/services/sites-and-monuments-record" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: communities-ni.gov.uk">[Department for Communities]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">communities-ni.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on communities-ni.gov.uk.</span></span></span>

The county also matters because it connects several branches of UK and Irish folklore at once. Its giant lore links to heroic Finn traditions and to Scottish-facing sea routes. Its fairy lore belongs with wider Ulster fairy hills, fairy thorns and fairy roads. Its holy wells connect Antrim to saintly and healing landscapes across Ireland and Britain. Its witch-hare and banshee stories belong to broader rural belief culture, while its modern ghost books, oral-history projects and films show folklore still being reworked rather than merely preserved. In County Antrim, mystery is not an added attraction. It is one of the ways the county has learned to remember its hills, roads, wells, shores and dangerous crossings.
