## Introduction

Lanark’s two best-known living customs, Whuppity Scoorie and Lanimers, show [Lanarkshire]({{ 'what-makes-lanarkshires-folklore-so/' | relative_url }}) folklore in motion. They are not ghost stories or old tales kept only in books: they are public rituals that still use the town centre, the church steeple, boundary stones, children, horses, bells, paper balls, coins, banners and civic offices to make local memory visible. Whuppity Scoorie takes place at the beginning of March, when children run three times clockwise around St Nicholas Church after the evening bell, swinging paper balls to mark the turn from winter to spring. Lanimers comes in June, when Lanark re-enacts the checking of its old burgh boundaries through processions, marches, riding, school participation and the Lord Cornet’s declaration. Together they explain why Lanark’s folklore feels unusually civic: its magic lies in repetition, place and belonging as much as in supernatural belief.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.lanark.co.uk/history/ancient-traditions/whuppity-scoorie" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: lanark.co.uk">[lanark.co.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">lanark.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on lanark.co.uk.</span></span></span>

## Whuppity Scoorie and the First Evening of March

Whuppity Scoorie is held in Lanark on 1 March, with children gathering at Lanark Cross beside the Town Steeple at 6pm. In its present form, each child carries a ball of paper tied to a length of string. When the small bell first sounds, they run three times clockwise around St Nicholas Church, swinging the paper balls over their heads; afterwards there is a scramble for small change in Hyndford Place. Discover Lanark, drawing on Lanark Museum Trust material, stresses that the custom’s exact origin and age are not likely to be conclusively proved.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.lanark.co.uk/history/ancient-traditions/whuppity-scoorie" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: lanark.co.uk">[Discover Lanark]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">lanark.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on lanark.co.uk.</span></span></span>

That uncertainty is part of the custom’s folklore value. Whuppity Scoorie is often described as a spring rite: the bell has been silent through the darker part of the year, and its return at six o’clock announces longer evenings, outdoor play and the social release of spring. A modern Discover Lanark account states that the first of March was regarded as the first day of spring, and that the run is widely thought to welcome spring, celebrate lengthening days and frighten away the evil spirits of winter.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.lanark.co.uk/history/ancient-traditions/whuppity-scoorie" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: lanark.co.uk">[Discover Lanark]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">lanark.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on lanark.co.uk.</span></span></span>

The location matters. St Nicholas Church stands prominently in Lanark town centre, built in 1774 on the site of an earlier chapel. Historic Environment Scotland’s Trove record places it in the former county of Lanarkshire and classifies the site as including a 13th-century chapel and an 18th-century church. Discover Lanark adds that the church contains the town’s great bell, traditionally believed to date from 1130 and recast several times, including in 1659, 1740 and 1983. This makes the bell more than a signal: it is a material piece of town memory around which the custom gathers.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.trove.scot/place/46586" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: trove.scot">[Trove Scot]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">trove.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on trove.scot.</span></span></span>

The earliest written evidence is more modest than many retellings imply. Discover Lanark says the custom does not appear in Town Council minutes and that its written record is found in the Hamilton Advertiser. The name Whuppity Scoorie first appears in connection with the ceremony in an 1890 report on the six o’clock bell, while the ceremony was not headlined as Whuppity Scoorie until 1893. That does not mean the custom began in 1893, but it does mean that claims of great antiquity need to be handled carefully.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.lanark.co.uk/history/ancient-traditions/whuppity-scoorie" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: lanark.co.uk">[Discover Lanark]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">lanark.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on lanark.co.uk.</span></span></span>

Older versions also seem rougher than today’s family event. One 1894 account, summarised by Discover Lanark, said that for the previous 120 years local boys had marched to meet workers coming home from New Lanark, using bonnets on strings and sometimes other weapons. Modern paper balls have softened the custom, turning a noisy youth confrontation into a safe, child-centred ritual. The old pattern still glimmers through: swinging objects, running, shouting, the bell, the boundary between winter darkness and usable evening light.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.lanark.co.uk/history/ancient-traditions/whuppity-scoorie" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: lanark.co.uk">[Discover Lanark]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">lanark.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on lanark.co.uk.</span></span></span>

Several origin theories circulate, but the strongest evidence supports a practical seasonal interpretation rather than a dramatic punishment legend. Discover Lanark notes that there is little evidence for explanations based on whipping and scouring wrongdoers, or punishing witches, because burgh and kirk session records do not record such punishments. A more grounded reading is that the ceremony grew from the return of the evening bell after winter darkness, giving apprentices and young people a public way to mark the beginning of lighter leisure time.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.lanark.co.uk/history/ancient-traditions/whuppity-scoorie" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: lanark.co.uk">[Discover Lanark]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">lanark.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on lanark.co.uk.</span></span></span>

## Lanimers, Boundary Stones and Burgh Rights

Lanimers is Lanark’s great June custom, held on the Thursday between 6 and 12 June, though the wider festival now fills much of a week. It has the feel of a gala day, with processions, bands, decorated lorries, the Lanimer Queen, the Lord Cornet, horses, ceremonies and crowds. But underneath the pageantry is a very old civic duty: checking the burgh’s boundary stones, or march stones, and reporting that the town’s bounds remain intact.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.lanarklanimers.co.uk/what-is-lanimers/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: lanarklanimers.co.uk">[lanarklanimers.co.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">lanarklanimers.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">What Is Lanimers?</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">What Is Lanimers?</span></span></span>

The official Lanimer history presents Lanark as one of Scotland’s oldest royal burghs, with its charter granted by King David I in the 12th century. It says Lanark may be unique in having checked its boundaries annually since around 1140, and that the burgh records described the Lanimers as “ancient and yearly” in 1588. The same account notes that the Crown took boundary checking seriously and that a report on Lanark’s boundaries is still sent to the Crown.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.lanarklanimers.co.uk/history/a-guide-to-lanimers/lanimer-celebrations/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: lanarklanimers.co.uk">[lanarklanimers.co.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">lanarklanimers.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Lanimer Celebrations</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Lanark Lanimers...</span></span></span>

The word itself points to the custom’s function. Lanimer sources explain older names such as Landemuir day, Land Marches and Riding of the Marches as versions of the same idea: inspecting the land marches, meaning the limits of the burgh. The Lord Cornet’s Club describes the Lord Cornet as the holder of an ancient office connected with maintaining the burgh boundaries, and explains that annual inspection of the march stones formed part of the old relationship between the Crown and the burgesses.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.lanarklanimers.co.uk/history/a-guide-to-lanimers/behind-the-scenes-%C2%AD-frequently-asked-questions/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: lanarklanimers.co.uk">[lanarklanimers.co.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">lanarklanimers.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Behind the Scenes ­ Frequently Asked Questions</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Behind the Scenes ­ Frequently Asked Questions</span></span></span>

This is why Lanimers matters as folklore rather than only as local history. It turns an administrative obligation into repeated public theatre. A boundary that could have been a legal line on paper becomes a walked and ridden landscape. The stones are not just markers; they are props in a recurring drama of civic continuity. The town does not merely say it has historic rights: it performs them.

The Perambulation of the Marches is central to that performance. The official Lanimer guide says the Monday perambulation dates back to 1752; before then, riders completed the whole circuit on horseback in one day. Cropping in fields made the full horseback circuit more difficult, so part of the inspection became a walking perambulation. In recent practice, the route begins at Hope Street at 6pm, led by the Lord Cornet and former Lord Cornets, and many townspeople walk roughly 8km through countryside north of the town while checking march stones along the way.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.lanarklanimers.co.uk/history/a-guide-to-lanimers/perambulation-of-the-marches/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: lanarklanimers.co.uk">[lanarklanimers.co.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">lanarklanimers.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Perambulation of the Marches</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Perambulation of the Marches</span></span></span>

The horse tradition remains equally important. Lanimer Week includes ride-outs in which the Lord Cornet and others inspect march stones on other sides of the town, with horse races and stops connected to New Lanark. South Lanarkshire Council’s public procession notice for Lanimer Day 2024 shows how the modern custom is managed in detail, including mounted participants, Police Scotland Mounted Branch, bands, route timings and the Lord Cornet’s Declaration at Lanark Cross. The old ritual survives not as a casual reenactment, but as a heavily organised public event negotiated with modern roads, safety rules and crowd management.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.lanarklanimers.co.uk/history/a-guide-to-lanimers/behind-the-scenes-%C2%AD-frequently-asked-questions/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: lanarklanimers.co.uk">[lanarklanimers.co.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">lanarklanimers.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Behind the Scenes ­ Frequently Asked Questions</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Behind the Scenes ­ Frequently Asked Questions</span></span></span>

## Children, Horses and Civic Belonging Today

What links Whuppity Scoorie and Lanimers is the way both customs recruit the town’s younger people into public tradition. Whuppity Scoorie is explicitly child-centred: children make or carry paper balls, wait for the bell, run the three circuits and take part in the coin scramble. During Covid restrictions, when the public run could not take place, the Community Council kept the custom alive through a decorated paper ball competition, showing that even a physical calendar custom can adapt when gathering is impossible.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.lanark.co.uk/posts/whuppity-scoorie-creative-ball-competition-now-open" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: lanark.co.uk">[Discover Lanark]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">lanark.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on lanark.co.uk.</span></span></span>

Lanimers does this on a larger scale. The first Lanimer Queen was crowned in 1893, and the ceremony and pageantry have remained broadly recognisable since then. The Queen is still elected annually by her peers from local schools, and the modern Lanimer court includes numerous child roles. In 2005, according to the official Lanimer guide, the morning procession included more than 1,450 participants, among them more than 700 children. The same guide records that children in the procession receive Lanimer medals, a practice rooted in the introduction of the Lanimer Queen, when children were originally given a penny coin minted that year.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.lanarklanimers.co.uk/history/a-guide-to-lanimers/behind-the-scenes-%C2%AD-frequently-asked-questions/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: lanarklanimers.co.uk">[lanarklanimers.co.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">lanarklanimers.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Behind the Scenes ­ Frequently Asked Questions</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Behind the Scenes ­ Frequently Asked Questions</span></span></span>

The customs also teach belonging through geography. Whuppity Scoorie teaches the town centre in miniature: Cross, church, bell, Hyndford Place, circular movement. Lanimers teaches the larger burgh: march stones, fields, roads, New Lanark, Castlebank Park, the Cross and the old boundary line. In both, children learn Lanark not as an abstract place-name but as a set of routes, sounds and repeated actions.

New Lanark adds an important Lanarkshire layer. The older Whuppity Scoorie tradition has been linked in some accounts with boys going out to meet New Lanark workers, while Lanimer ride-outs still include a ceremonial stop connected with New Lanark. UNESCO describes New Lanark as an 18th-century mill village where Robert Owen developed a model industrial community in the early 19th century, and South Lanarkshire Council notes its World Heritage status and international importance. In Lanark’s customs, the royal burgh, the market town and the industrial village are not separate heritage boxes; they overlap in the town’s ritual geography.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.lanark.co.uk/history/ancient-traditions/whuppity-scoorie" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: lanark.co.uk">[lanark.co.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">lanark.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on lanark.co.uk.</span></span></span>

The modern survival of these customs depends on organisation as much as sentiment. Whuppity Scoorie is now organised by the Royal Burgh of Lanark Community Council, which took over from the Town Council in 1975. Lanimers is maintained by a Lanimer Committee, the Lord Cornets’ Club, schools, riders, bands, local authorities, volunteers and families. The official Lanimer material is frank about how traditions grow: one page notes that semi-official and unofficial social gatherings, if held for more than three successive years, “become traditions”. That is a revealing local definition of folklore: repetition plus recognition.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.lanark.co.uk/history/ancient-traditions/whuppity-scoorie" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: lanark.co.uk">[lanark.co.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">lanark.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on lanark.co.uk.</span></span></span>

## Why These Customs Survive in Lanarkshire Folklore

Lanark’s customs survive because they do several jobs at once. Whuppity Scoorie gives children a thrilling, noisy way to mark spring. Lanimers gives the whole town a week-long civic drama in which law, land, school life, horses, music and pageantry all meet. One is compact and playful; the other is expansive and formal. Both are rooted in repeated movement through significant places.

They also survive because they are not frozen. Whuppity Scoorie has shifted from bonnets and rough youth confrontation to paper balls, family participation and school-friendly competition. Lanimers has expanded from boundary inspection into a full civic festival with processions, courts, decorated lorries, medals, balls and public entertainment. The core actions remain legible, but the social form adapts to the needs of each generation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.lanark.co.uk/history/ancient-traditions/whuppity-scoorie" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: lanark.co.uk">[Discover Lanark]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">lanark.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on lanark.co.uk.</span></span></span>

For Lanarkshire folklore, their importance is that they make public memory visible without requiring belief in fairies, ghosts or omens. The “folk” element lies in shared performance: knowing where to gather, when to listen for the bell, how many times to circle the church, who carries the standard, why the march stones matter, and why children are placed at the centre of the spectacle. These customs are a reminder that folklore is not only the strange story told after dark. Sometimes it is a town walking its bounds in daylight, or children racing round an old church tower at the first sound of a spring bell.
