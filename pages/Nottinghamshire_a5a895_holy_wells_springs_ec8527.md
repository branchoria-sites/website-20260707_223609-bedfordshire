## Introduction

[Nottinghamshire]({{ 'where-nottinghamshire-turns-history-into/' | relative_url }})’s holy wells promised three things: healing, memory and a sense that ordinary water could mark an extraordinary place. The clearest case is St Ann’s Well on the north-east side of Nottingham, also known as [Robin Hood]({{ 'robin-hood/' | relative_url }})’s Well, where cold spring water was linked with cures for rheumatic pains and other ailments, civic outings, a chapel dedication, and the county’s strongest outlaw legend. Older accounts describe it as a real destination rather than a vague rumour: an arched stone well, a place where visitors came for cures, and a site where supposed Robin Hood relics were once shown.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.british-history.ac.uk/thoroton-notts/vol2/pp157-176" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: british-history.ac.uk">[British History Online]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">british-history.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on british-history.ac.uk.</span></span></span>



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/Nottinghamshire_a5a895_holy_wells_springs_ec8527-overview.webp" | relative_url }}" alt="Overview image for Holy Wells" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

The wider county picture is more fragmentary. Nottinghamshire has named Lady Wells, St Catherine’s Wells, St Mary’s Wells, Holy Wells and healing springs, but many are destroyed, capped, lost, privately enclosed or known mainly through place-names and antiquarian notes. A modern holy-well gazetteer lists sites across the county, including St Ann’s Well in Nottingham, St Catherine’s Well at Newark, Holy Well at Watnall, Lady Well at Nether Headon, St Mary’s Well at Wallingwells and several destroyed or lost examples.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://britishholywells.co.uk/england/nottinghamshire/nottinghamshirelist.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: britishholywells.co.uk">[British Holy Wells]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">britishholywells.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on britishholywells.co.uk.</span></span></span> That uneven evidence is part of the story. Nottinghamshire’s well traditions are not a neat chain of surviving shrines; they are a patchwork of saints, Robin Hood associations, parish memory, field names, lost springs, local cures and modern rediscovery.

## Why St Ann’s Well matters most

St Ann’s Well is the county’s key holy-well tradition because it joins together several layers that rarely survive so clearly in one place: healing water, medieval and early modern record, Robin Hood folklore, civic custom and later urban loss. John Throsby’s 1790 edition of Thoroton’s history places the well “within two miles North East of Nottingham” at the base of a hill, under an arched stone roof, with notably cold water. The account says the water was used by people with rheumatic pains and, like many popular springs, for “a variety of disorders”. It also records that objects said to belong to Robin Hood were shown at the house nearby, reduced by that time to items called his cap or helmet and part of his chair.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.british-history.ac.uk/thoroton-notts/vol2/pp157-176" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: british-history.ac.uk">[British History Online]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">british-history.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on british-history.ac.uk.</span></span></span>

That detail matters because it shows the well working on two levels. For sick visitors, it was a place of bodily hope: cold water, bathing or contact, and a reputation for relief. For visitors drawn by Robin Hood, it was a legendary landmark: a place where the outlaw’s name was attached to objects, landscape and local storytelling. In Nottinghamshire folklore, this is exactly the kind of site where history, belief and tourism blur. The source does not prove that the water cured illness or that Robin Hood used the place. It does show that by the late eighteenth century, the well’s healing reputation and outlaw association were already established enough to be recorded together.

The name history adds another layer. The International Robin Hood Bibliography notes that the site may appear in Nottingham civic records in 1301 as “The Brodewell”, though that identification is uncertain, and that it was also known as Owswell in the Middle Ages. It gives a firmer later pattern: a chapel dedicated to St Ann was built next to the well in 1409; by 1577 the name “Sent Anne Well” appears; and in 1578 the combined form “Robyn Hood Well alias Saynt Anne Well” occurs.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.irhb.org/w/index.php/Robin_Hood%27s_Well_%28Nottingham%29" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: irhb.org">[irhb.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">irhb.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Robin Hood&#x27;s Well (Nottingham</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Robin Hood&#x27;s Well (Nottingham</span></span></span> For folklore readers, that “alias” is important. It suggests not a clean replacement of one name by another, but a living overlap between popular Robin Hood naming and church dedication.

## Saints, healing water and place memory

Holy wells often survive because their names cling to places long after the water itself has vanished. Nottinghamshire is a good example. St Ann’s gave its name to a Nottingham district, even though the well itself is gone. Local reporting in 2021 described the well as lying on the now-demolished Gardeners Arms pub site and noted calls for a plaque, precisely because modern housing and redevelopment had left little visible reminder of the spring that gave St Ann’s its name.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nottinghampost.com/news/story-behind-historic-well-st-4999311" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nottinghampost.com">[Nottingham Post]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nottinghampost.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">story behind historic well st 4999311</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">story behind historic well st 4999311</span></span></span>

The saintly dedications are not all equally secure. Some wells have clear names but thin traditions; others have strong stories but uncertain medieval proof. St Catherine’s Well at Newark has one of the richest legends. In the version discussed by Frank Earp and republished by the In Search of Holy Wells and Healing Springs project, Lady Isabel de Cauldwell is loved by two rival knights, Sir Guy Saucimer and Sir Everard Bevercotes. The men fight near the River Devon on St Catherine’s Eve; Sir Everard is killed; blood and water mingle as a spring bursts from the ground; Sir Guy later suffers leprous sores, dreams that St Catherine directs him back to the spring, bathes there, is cured, and becomes a hermit guardian of the well.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://insearchofholywellsandhealingsprings.wordpress.com/tag/nottinghamshire/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: insearchofholywellsandhealingsprings.wordpress.com">[holyandhealingwells]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">insearchofholywellsandhealingsprings.wordpress.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">holyandhealingwells Nottinghamshire &#124; holyandhealingwells</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">holyandhealingwells Nottinghamshire &#124; holyandhealingwells</span></span></span>

That is vivid folklore, but it needs careful handling. The same discussion notes that the legend claims a fifteenth-century written origin, yet no document of that date has been found, and that W. Dickinson’s 1816 version is one of the fullest known accounts. It also warns that some later writers suspected the tale may have been invented or shaped as a literary joke.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://insearchofholywellsandhealingsprings.wordpress.com/tag/nottinghamshire/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: insearchofholywellsandhealingsprings.wordpress.com">[holyandhealingwells]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">insearchofholywellsandhealingsprings.wordpress.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">holyandhealingwells Nottinghamshire &#124; holyandhealingwells</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">holyandhealingwells Nottinghamshire &#124; holyandhealingwells</span></span></span> A grounded reading does not have to choose between “ancient truth” and “worthless fiction”. St Catherine’s Well matters because it shows how a Nottinghamshire healing spring could attract a full narrative of guilt, blood, saintly instruction, leprosy, bathing and penance. Whether medieval, early modern, antiquarian or partly invented, it belongs to the county’s story culture.

Other sites are quieter but still revealing. Nether Headon’s Lady Well is described by holy-well researcher Ross Parish as an ancient healing well in a wooded ravine, appearing on the village sign and giving its name to a local lane. The same account notes that the spring served as a village drinking-water source until the 1930s, was said never to fail, and was one of the few Nottinghamshire sites to receive well dressing in the late twentieth century and early 2000s.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://insearchofholywellsandhealingsprings.wordpress.com/tag/nottinghamshire/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: insearchofholywellsandhealingsprings.wordpress.com">[holyandhealingwells]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">insearchofholywellsandhealingsprings.wordpress.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">holyandhealingwells Nottinghamshire &#124; holyandhealingwells</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">holyandhealingwells Nottinghamshire &#124; holyandhealingwells</span></span></span> Here the folklore is less dramatic than at Newark, but the continuity of local attachment is strong: a village landmark, a practical water source, a named lane, a sign, and revived ceremonial care.

## St Ann’s Well and Robin Hood’s Well

The Robin Hood connection is what makes St Ann’s Well stand out nationally as well as locally. Nottinghamshire has many Robin Hood place associations, but a healing spring with documentary name overlap is more distinctive than a simple “Robin Hood slept here” tradition. The International Robin Hood Bibliography treats Robin Hood’s Well and St Ann’s Well as the same Nottingham site, noting that the 1578 “alias” form helps remove the possibility that two separate wells were being confused.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://insearchofholywellsandhealingsprings.wordpress.com/the-source-new-series-contents/some-observations-on-the-earliest-spring-called-after-robin-hood/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: insearchofholywellsandhealingsprings.wordpress.com">[holyandhealingwells]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">insearchofholywellsandhealingsprings.wordpress.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">holyandhealingwells Some Observations on the Earliest Spring Called After Robin</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">holyandhealingwells Some Observations on the Earliest Spring Called After Robin</span></span></span>

The well also sat in a Sherwood Forest imaginative landscape. Throsby’s account places it near Nottingham in a section on the Forest of Shirewood, where Robin Hood, forest memory, deer, woodland and antiquities are discussed together.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.british-history.ac.uk/thoroton-notts/vol2/pp157-176" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: british-history.ac.uk">[British History Online]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">british-history.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on british-history.ac.uk.</span></span></span> That setting matters. A spring outside the town, near woodland and common land, could be understood as both useful and liminal: close enough for civic visits and healing use, far enough from the town centre to feel like a threshold into older forest story.

There is, however, a useful caution. Some modern retellings lean heavily into a grand medieval history of hermits, Templars, Easter processions and ecclesiastical control. The International Robin Hood Bibliography summarises these claims but also points out that some of the early-history material relies on secondary sources and still needs better corroboration from primary evidence. It contrasts those claims with firmer civic-record evidence from the mid-sixteenth century, including an Easter Monday payment in 1557/58 to keepers at St Ann’s Well and later payments to musicians or waits performing on the way to and at the well.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.irhb.org/w/index.php/Robin_Hood%27s_Well_%28Nottingham%29" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: irhb.org">[irhb.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">irhb.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Robin Hood&#x27;s Well (Nottingham</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Robin Hood&#x27;s Well (Nottingham</span></span></span>

That distinction improves rather than weakens the story. The strongest version of St Ann’s Well is not “everything ever claimed about it is true”. It is that a spring on Nottingham’s edge accumulated several kinds of authority: civic, devotional, medicinal and legendary. The Robin Hood name gave it popular glamour; the St Ann dedication gave it Christian framing; the cold water gave it healing reputation; and civic outings made it part of public custom.

## The county’s smaller healing springs

Beyond St Ann’s Well, Nottinghamshire’s healing-water traditions are scattered across villages, private land, lost sites and local-history notes. They rarely have the same depth of record, but they show how widespread the idea of curative water was.

At Blidworth, the Ashwell stood near the parish church of St Mary of the Purification. Robert Morrell’s account, republished by Nottingham Hidden History Team, says it was covered up after the First World War, but before that had served for centuries as a village water source and was believed to help rheumatism. The same piece links the name to a large ash tree beside the spring and records a local story that those who cut the tree down suffered bad luck and illness which the water could not relieve.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://nottinghamhiddenhistoryteam.wordpress.com/2024/04/15/nottinghamshire-holy-wells-the-ashwell-blidworth/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nottinghamhiddenhistoryteam.wordpress.com">[Nottingham Hidden History Team]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nottinghamhiddenhistoryteam.wordpress.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Nottinghamshire Holy Wells: The Ashwell, Blidworth &#124; Nottingham Hidden History Team...</span></span></span> That is a classic local-folklore pattern: water, tree, cure and punishment all bound to one small place.

Blidworth also has Friar Tuck’s Well at Fountain Dale, a chalybeate spring, meaning iron-bearing water, which was said to have medicinal properties. A letter from the Reverend R. H. Whitworth, vicar of Blidworth from 1865 to 1908, reportedly said people still visited the spring to use its healing water; Morrell also notes a claim that it had once been called St Lawrence’s Spring, though he had not found the name elsewhere in village source books.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://nottinghamhiddenhistoryteam.wordpress.com/2024/04/15/nottinghamshire-holy-wells-the-ashwell-blidworth/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nottinghamhiddenhistoryteam.wordpress.com">[Nottingham Hidden History Team]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nottinghamhiddenhistoryteam.wordpress.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Nottinghamshire Holy Wells: The Ashwell, Blidworth &#124; Nottingham Hidden History Team...</span></span></span> Its Robin Hood connections are indirect but attractive: Blidworth is tied in local tradition to Maid Marian and Will Scarlet, while Friar Tuck’s Well gives the healing-spring landscape another outlaw-era name.

Willow Rundle Spring near East Stoke belongs to a different kind of memory. Nottingham Hidden History Team describes it as lying in a dip on the old route between East Stoke and Elston, with legends connecting it to the Battle of Stoke Field in 1487. The water was famed locally for medicinal and healing properties, said to be sweet to the taste, and issued through a stone culvert into a shallow trough before overflowing into a ditch.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://nottinghamhiddenhistoryteam.wordpress.com/2015/07/25/willow-rundle-spring/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nottinghamhiddenhistoryteam.wordpress.com">[Nottingham Hidden History Team]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nottinghamhiddenhistoryteam.wordpress.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Willow Rundle Spring &#124; Nottingham Hidden History Team...</span></span></span> The article is careful to say there is no written evidence proving it was a Christian holy well, though local legend connects it with Elston Old Chapel and possible care of lepers.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://nottinghamhiddenhistoryteam.wordpress.com/2015/07/25/willow-rundle-spring/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nottinghamhiddenhistoryteam.wordpress.com">[Nottingham Hidden History Team]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nottinghamhiddenhistoryteam.wordpress.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Willow Rundle Spring &#124; Nottingham Hidden History Team...</span></span></span> This is a useful example of a “healing spring” rather than a securely documented saint’s well.

Watnall’s Holy Well, near Trough Lane, shows how a site can be both physically present and historically uncertain. The Northern Antiquarian gives its grid reference as SK 499 456 and records field-name evidence including “Holy Well Furlong” in a 1653 manor document and “Flatts or Holliwell” in a 1724 survey. Yet the same account warns that the “holy” element may be nineteenth-century romanticism and that some “Holwell” names may derive from “hollow” rather than “holy”.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://megalithix.wordpress.com/2014/09/13/holy-well-watnall/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: megalithix.wordpress.com">[The Northern Antiquarian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">megalithix.wordpress.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Holy Well, Watnall, Nottinghamshire &#124; The Northern Antiquarian...</span></span></span> This is exactly the kind of evidence problem that runs through Nottinghamshire’s well lore: a name can be suggestive without being decisive.

## How strong is the well evidence?

The evidence for Nottinghamshire’s holy wells ranges from relatively strong to extremely thin. St Ann’s Well is the best-attested because it appears in antiquarian description, civic-record discussion, place-name history and Robin Hood scholarship. It was physically described, socially used and repeatedly named. It also left marks on Nottingham’s urban geography, including St Ann’s Well Road and the wider St Ann’s district.

A second group contains wells with surviving structures or visible local memory but less secure early documentation. Nether Headon’s Lady Well, Watnall Holy Well and St Mary’s Well at Wallingwells fit this pattern. They matter because they keep holy-well tradition in the landscape, but claims about medieval origin, saintly dedication or uninterrupted ritual use need to be weighed carefully. The British Holy Wells Nottinghamshire list usefully shows this mixed survival pattern, marking some sites as extant, others as destroyed, covered, capped, lost or unknown.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://britishholywells.co.uk/england/nottinghamshire/nottinghamshirelist.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: britishholywells.co.uk">[British Holy Wells]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">britishholywells.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on britishholywells.co.uk.</span></span></span>

A third group survives mainly as place-name evidence. Radcliffe-on-Trent’s Holy Well is a good example. Parish’s account says the evidence is scant, with a first reference in 1801 to “Holy Well Close” near present Park Road, a later sale reference to Holywell Close around 1844, and uncertainty over whether the name may have meant holy, hallow or hollow.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://insearchofholywellsandhealingsprings.wordpress.com/tag/nottinghamshire/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: insearchofholywellsandhealingsprings.wordpress.com">[holyandhealingwells]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">insearchofholywellsandhealingsprings.wordpress.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">holyandhealingwells Nottinghamshire &#124; holyandhealingwells</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">holyandhealingwells Nottinghamshire &#124; holyandhealingwells</span></span></span> Place-names can preserve old memory, but they can also mislead. “Holywell”, “Halliwell” and “Holwell” may point to sanctity, health, a hollow, a spring, or later antiquarian interpretation.

The most fragile evidence is where a well is lost and later claims cannot be checked against visible remains. Mansfield’s St Mary’s Well, also called the Virgin Mary’s Well or the Old Bath, illustrates this. A Nottingham Hidden History Team article quotes an English Heritage Pastscape entry saying that modern development had left no surface evidence and local enquiry was negative; the same article notes that the earliest record found was Harrold’s 1801 work on Mansfield.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://nottinghamhiddenhistoryteam.wordpress.com/2014/04/12/a-lost-holy-well-found-the-lady-well-of-mansfield/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nottinghamhiddenhistoryteam.wordpress.com">[Nottingham Hidden History Team]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nottinghamhiddenhistoryteam.wordpress.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">A lost holy well found? The Lady well of Mansfield &#124; Nottingham Hidden History Team...</span></span></span> For readers, the lesson is not that such a site should be ignored, but that its status should be stated honestly: remembered, recorded, possibly relocated, but not strongly visible.

## What Nottinghamshire’s wells reveal

Nottinghamshire’s wells reveal a county where sacred and healing water was often local rather than monumental. This is not a landscape dominated by one great surviving pilgrimage shrine. It is a county of cold springs under stone arches, village troughs, wells in wooded lanes, springs near old chapels, lost urban sites and names preserved in roads, fields and parish memory.

They also show how folklore attaches to practical water. These springs were not only symbolic. Some supplied drinking water, served travellers, fed troughs, watered villages or became places where people washed, bathed, sat, gathered or marked a feast. Their healing reputation often grew from that everyday usefulness. A spring that never failed, tasted sweet, ran cold, stained with minerals or flowed beside a church could become more than a water source.

St Ann’s Well remains the essential Nottinghamshire example because it binds the whole pattern together. It was a healing spring, a saint’s well, a Robin Hood site, a civic destination and a lost landmark. Around it, the county’s smaller wells show the same themes in quieter form: St Catherine’s Well with its tragic cure legend at Newark; the Ashwell at Blidworth with rheumatism, ash-tree luck and village memory; Friar Tuck’s Well with chalybeate water and outlaw colouring; Willow Rundle Spring with battle legend and uncertain chapel links; Watnall with field-name ambiguity; and Lady Well at Nether Headon with local pride and modern well dressing.

The promise of Nottinghamshire’s holy wells was never just a medical cure. It was the possibility that water could carry memory: of a saint, an outlaw, a battle, a lost chapel, a village tree, a vanished district or a spring that people once trusted enough to visit when ordinary remedies were not enough.
