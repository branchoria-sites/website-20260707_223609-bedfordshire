## Introduction

Cawdor Castle is one of [Nairnshire]({{ 'where-nairnshires-strange-stories-still/' | relative_url }})’s clearest examples of a real place reshaped by literary fame. The castle is widely associated with Macbeth because Shakespeare gives his tragic hero the title “Thane of Cawdor”, but the link is misleading: the historical Macbeth ruled Scotland from 1040 to 1057, while the present castle belongs to a much later medieval Cawdor story. What Cawdor does have is something just as interesting for folklore: a foundation legend about a donkey, gold and a holly tree sealed into the tower; a landscape that turns local history into myth; and a haunted reputation built around later tales of family inheritance, abduction and ghostly figures. In Nairnshire folklore, Cawdor matters not because Macbeth truly lived there, but because it shows how drama, tourism, estate tradition and local legend can fuse into a powerful modern myth.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://discoverhighlandsandislands.scot/en/story/cawdor-castle-macbeth-and-a-ghost" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: discoverhighlandsandislands.scot">[Discover Highlands and Islands]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">discoverhighlandsandislands.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on discoverhighlandsandislands.scot.</span></span></span>

## The castle, holly tree and Cawdor landscape

Cawdor Castle stands near the village of Cawdor, about five miles south-west of Nairn, in the historic county of Nairnshire. Historic Environment Scotland places it off the B9090, above the alluvial plain of the River Nairn, beside Cawdor Burn and within a designed landscape that looks north and north-east towards the Moray Firth and Highland uplands. That setting matters: the castle’s legend is not attached to an anonymous ruin, but to a wooded, gardened estate at the meeting point of river valley, farmland and rising moorland.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://portal.historicenvironment.scot/apex/f?p=1505%3A300%3A%3A%3A%3A%3AVIEWTYPE%2CVIEWREF%3Adesignation%2CGDL00099" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: portal.historicenvironment.scot">[Historic Environment Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">portal.historicenvironment.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Historic Environment ScotlandCAWDOR CASTLE (GDL00099)...</span></span></span>

The strongest local tradition is the foundation story of the tree. In the version told by Cawdor Castle itself, the Thane of Cawdor wanted a new, stronger tower and followed instructions received in a dream. He loaded a coffer of gold onto a donkey and let it wander; wherever the animal lay down at evening, the castle would be built and would prosper. The donkey rested under a tree, and the tower was raised around it. The preserved trunk can still be seen at the base of the old tower.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cawdorcastle.com/cawdor-castle/castle-history/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cawdorcastle.com">[Cawdor Castle]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cawdorcastle.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cawdor CastleCastle History - Cawdor Castle - A five star visitor attraction near Nairn in the Scottish Highlands...</span></span></span>

This is a good folklore story because it joins practical estate history to an omen-like act of choosing. A fortified house needs a site, but the tale says the site was not chosen only by surveying land or judging military advantage. It was chosen through a dream, an animal and a tree. The donkey becomes an unwitting diviner; the gold hints at fortune and legitimacy; the tree becomes the living heart of the building.

The most striking detail is that the famous “thorn tree” has turned out not to be a thorn at all. Cawdor Castle states that radiocarbon measurement gives the wood an approximate date of AD 1372, and microscopic analysis identifies it as holly rather than hawthorn. Historic Environment Scotland’s designed-landscape record also notes the preserved holly in the vaults and gives the same carbon-dated date of 1372.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cawdorcastle.com/cawdor-castle/castle-history/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cawdorcastle.com">[Cawdor Castle]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cawdorcastle.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cawdor CastleCastle History - Cawdor Castle - A five star visitor attraction near Nairn in the Scottish Highlands...</span></span></span>

That correction does not kill the legend. It makes the story more revealing. Folklore often preserves a memorable structure while changing details over time. The old tale remembered a special tree and a castle built around it; modern analysis revised the species and helped anchor the material object in the later medieval period. For readers interested in Nairnshire’s belief culture, the holly tree is therefore more valuable than a simple “true or false” curiosity. It shows a legend surviving contact with science by changing shape: the tree remains central, but its meaning shifts from miraculous thorn to historically testable holly.

The castle’s architecture also supports the idea of a long-lived, layered place rather than a single Macbeth-era fortress. Historic Environment Scotland describes Cawdor Castle as a large rectangular courtyard castle of varying dates, enclosing an original central mid-15th-century five-storey keep that incorporates earlier work, with 16th-century, 18th-century and 19th-century additions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://portal.historicenvironment.scot/apex/f?p=1505%3A300%3A%3A%3A%3A%3AVIEWTYPE%2CVIEWREF%3Adesignation%2CLB1728" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: portal.historicenvironment.scot">[Historic Environment Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">portal.historicenvironment.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on historicenvironment.scot.</span></span></span> The Cawdor Estate timeline says the present tower was constructed around 1372–1396, while a royal licence from James II authorised fortification in 1454.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cawdor.com/history" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cawdor.com">[Cawdor Estate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cawdor.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cawdor.com.</span></span></span>

This layered building history is important because it separates Cawdor’s own medieval legend from the Shakespearean myth. The holly tree, the donkey and the tower belong to the castle’s local foundation tradition. Macbeth belongs to a different chain of storytelling: chronicle history, Shakespearean tragedy and later visitor expectation.

## Why the Macbeth link is misleading

The popular question is simple: was Cawdor Castle Macbeth’s castle? The answer is no. The connection comes from literature, not from reliable local history. In Shakespeare’s play, Macbeth meets the witches, hears that he will become Thane of Cawdor and later king, and then the title seems to confirm the witches’ power. The Royal Shakespeare Company summarises the prophecy in just those terms: Macbeth is predicted to become Thane of Cawdor and then King of Scotland.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.rsc.org.uk/macbeth/the-plot" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: rsc.org.uk">[Royal Society of Chemistry]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">rsc.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on rsc.org.uk.</span></span></span>

The real historical problem is chronology. Spirit of the Highlands and Islands, using Nairn Museum material, states that Macbeth died around 130 years before the title Thane of Cawdor was granted and more than 300 years before the first stone was laid at Cawdor Castle. The same account gives Macbeth’s rule as 1040 to 1057 and notes that the Cawdor name remains attached to Shakespeare’s play despite the historical mismatch.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://discoverhighlandsandislands.scot/en/story/cawdor-castle-macbeth-and-a-ghost" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: discoverhighlandsandislands.scot">[Discover Highlands and Islands]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">discoverhighlandsandislands.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on discoverhighlandsandislands.scot.</span></span></span>

The castle itself was not built for Shakespeare’s Macbeth, and Shakespeare does not make Cawdor Castle a stage location in the play. The murder of Duncan in the drama takes place at Macbeth’s castle at Inverness, not at Cawdor. The Cawdor association is therefore more subtle than many visitors expect: it is the title “Thane of Cawdor” that binds the place to Macbeth, not an event in the plot that occurs inside the real castle.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://discoverhighlandsandislands.scot/en/story/cawdor-castle-macbeth-and-a-ghost" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: discoverhighlandsandislands.scot">[Discover Highlands and Islands]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">discoverhighlandsandislands.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on discoverhighlandsandislands.scot.</span></span></span>

The literary trail runs through chronicle tradition. Shakespeare’s main source for Macbeth was Raphael Holinshed’s *Chronicles of England, Scotland and Ireland*, first published in 1577, with Shakespeare drawing especially on Holinshed’s accounts of Duncan and Macbeth. A Holinshed index of the Macbeth material explicitly includes the episode in which Macbeth becomes Thane of Cawdor, while the Royal Shakespeare Company notes that Shakespeare borrowed heavily from Holinshed’s 1587 edition but changed important details.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://shakespeare-navigators.ewu.edu/macbeth/Holinshed/index.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: shakespeare-navigators.ewu.edu">[shakespeare-navigators.ewu.edu]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">shakespeare-navigators.ewu.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Source of Macbeth: Holinshed&#x27;s Chronicles...</span></span></span>

This is where Cawdor becomes fascinating as folklore rather than frustrating as bad history. The myth is not just a tourist mistake. It is a chain of retellings:

**Historical Macbeth:** an 11th-century king of Scotland, not a resident of the present Cawdor Castle.

**Chronicle Macbeth:** a figure shaped by late medieval and early modern historical writing.

**Shakespeare’s Macbeth:** a tragic character whose rise begins when the witches hail him with the Cawdor title.

**Tourist Cawdor:** a real Nairnshire castle whose name now carries the atmosphere of prophecy, murder and witchcraft even though the chronology does not fit.

The misleading link has become part of the castle’s cultural identity because Shakespeare changed the imaginative weight of the word “Cawdor”. Before the play, Cawdor was a local thanage and estate name. After the play, it became a word many readers and theatre-goers associate with ambition, supernatural prophecy and the first step towards kingship. That does not make the Macbeth story historically true, but it does make it folklorically powerful.

## Ghost stories, tourism and literary afterlife

Cawdor’s haunted reputation is a later layer added to the castle’s older family history and Shakespearean aura. Spirit of the Highlands and Islands records a tradition that a ghost in a blue velvet dress has been seen around the castle grounds and in the drawing room, gazing at the portrait of the first Baron Cawdor. Local legend identifies this figure as Muriel Calder, while another ghost is said to be her husband, John Campbell.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://discoverhighlandsandislands.scot/en/story/cawdor-castle-macbeth-and-a-ghost" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: discoverhighlandsandislands.scot">[Discover Highlands and Islands]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">discoverhighlandsandislands.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on discoverhighlandsandislands.scot.</span></span></span>

Muriel’s historical story gives the ghost tradition its emotional force. The same Nairn-focused account says the Thanedom of Cawdor passed through the Calder line until Muriel Calder succeeded her father John in 1493; she was abducted as a child and in 1510 was forced to marry Sir John Campbell of Argyll, after which the Campbell connection to Cawdor developed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://discoverhighlandsandislands.scot/en/story/cawdor-castle-macbeth-and-a-ghost" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: discoverhighlandsandislands.scot">[Discover Highlands and Islands]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">discoverhighlandsandislands.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on discoverhighlandsandislands.scot.</span></span></span> The Cawdor Estate history also places Muriel Calder’s birth in 1494 and her 1510 marriage to Sir John Campbell as the point at which control of Cawdor transferred to Campbell stewardship.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cawdor.com/history" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cawdor.com">[Cawdor Estate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cawdor.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cawdor.com.</span></span></span>

This is not the same kind of tradition as the Macbeth myth. Macbeth’s Cawdor link is a literary overlay from a famous play. Muriel’s ghost is rooted in local family memory: inheritance, abduction, marriage alliance and the transfer of power from Calder to Campbell. It is exactly the sort of story that often becomes ghost lore in Scottish castles, where private family trauma is retold as a restless presence in rooms, portraits and grounds.

The details vary in modern retellings. Some accounts call the figure a blue lady; others introduce a handless apparition or blend Cawdor’s story with wider motifs of wronged daughters and violent fathers. That variation is a warning sign as well as a folklore clue. Ghost stories often spread by borrowing familiar images: the lady in blue, the woman at the portrait, the mutilated daughter, the child heiress, the unhappy bride. The most cautious version is the one attached to Nairn’s heritage storytelling: a blue-velvet figure identified in local legend with Muriel Calder, with sightings linked to the grounds and drawing room.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://discoverhighlandsandislands.scot/en/story/cawdor-castle-macbeth-and-a-ghost" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: discoverhighlandsandislands.scot">[discoverhighlandsandislands.scot]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">discoverhighlandsandislands.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on discoverhighlandsandislands.scot.</span></span></span>

Cawdor’s haunted image is strengthened by the building itself. The visitor encounters not a bare ruin but a lived-in, furnished castle with an old tower, vaulted spaces, a preserved tree, portraits and gardens. Historic Environment Scotland’s listing describes the central keep, great hall, iron yett, vaulted ground-floor rooms, blue room and yellow drawing room, while the designed-landscape record traces gardens, policies and woodland across several centuries.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://portal.historicenvironment.scot/apex/f?p=1505%3A300%3A%3A%3A%3A%3AVIEWTYPE%2CVIEWREF%3Adesignation%2CLB1728" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: portal.historicenvironment.scot">[Historic Environment Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">portal.historicenvironment.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on historicenvironment.scot.</span></span></span> These are exactly the kinds of spaces that help ghost stories feel place-specific: a named room, a portrait, a threshold, a cellar, a tree in the vaults.

Tourism then binds the strands together. Cawdor Castle presents itself as a traditional Scottish castle inhabited by the Cawdor family for more than 600 years, with an ancient medieval tower constructed around the legendary holly tree.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cawdorcastle.com/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cawdorcastle.com">[Cawdor Castle]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cawdorcastle.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cawdorcastle.com.</span></span></span> The Macbeth association brings many visitors to the name; the holly tree gives them a concrete wonder to inspect; the ghost stories give the site an after-dark imaginative life even when told in daylight.

The result is a layered Nairnshire legend cluster rather than a single tale. The Macbeth link is historically misleading but culturally productive. The holly tree legend is locally rooted and physically anchored. The ghosts are less firmly evidenced, but they preserve the mood of family conflict and inheritance anxiety around the Calder-Campbell transition. Together, they make Cawdor a useful case study in how folklore is made: not from one old story left untouched, but from repeated retellings that attach themselves to stone, title, landscape and performance.

## What Cawdor adds to Nairnshire folklore

Cawdor’s value within Nairnshire folklore is its mixture of correction and enchantment. It invites the reader in through Macbeth, then immediately asks them to unlearn the easy version. The real castle is too late for the historical Macbeth; the play does not use it as Macbeth’s home; and the title’s theatrical fame has outrun the local record. Yet the place is not made less interesting by that correction. It becomes more interesting because the myth can be seen forming in layers.

For a small historic county, this matters. Nairnshire’s folklore is often documentary and place-bound rather than filled with grand monsters. Auldearn has witch-trial testimony; Rait has violent castle legend; the coast has fishing customs and lost-land memory. Cawdor contributes a different pattern: literary fame altering the reputation of a real estate. It is where a Shakespearean word, a medieval tower and a family ghost tradition meet in the public imagination.

The best way to understand Cawdor is therefore not to ask, “Did Macbeth live here?” and stop when the answer is no. A better question is, “How did Cawdor become Macbeth-haunted even without Macbeth?” The answer lies in the power of a name. Shakespeare made “Cawdor” sound fated. Later visitors arrived with the play in mind. The castle offered its own older wonder in the holly tree. Local heritage supplied Muriel Calder and the blue-velvet ghost. The landscape around Nairn, Cawdor Burn and Strathnairn gave all of it a setting that felt ancient enough to hold the tale.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://portal.historicenvironment.scot/apex/f?p=1505%3A300%3A%3A%3A%3A%3AVIEWTYPE%2CVIEWREF%3Adesignation%2CGDL00099" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: portal.historicenvironment.scot">[Historic Environment Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">portal.historicenvironment.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Historic Environment ScotlandCAWDOR CASTLE (GDL00099)...</span></span></span>

That is why Cawdor’s Macbeth myth should be treated neither as fact nor as nonsense. It is a literary afterlife attached to a real Nairnshire place. Its historical claims need careful handling, but its folklore value is genuine: it shows how a county landmark can be transformed by drama, visitor expectation and the human desire to make old stones speak.
