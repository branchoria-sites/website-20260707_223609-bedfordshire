## Introduction

The Paisley Witch Hunt, often called the Bargarran or [Renfrewshire]({{ 'where-renfrewshires-legends-still/' | relative_url }}) witch case, was the 1696–97 panic that began with Christian Shaw, the eleven-year-old daughter of John Shaw of Bargarran near Erskine, and ended with seven accused people condemned in Paisley on 10 June 1697. It is Renfrewshire’s best-known supernatural tradition because it is not only a story of supposed bewitchment, curses and strange physical torments, but also a documented miscarriage of justice fixed to real places: Bargarran House, Paisley’s tollbooth, Gallow Green and Maxwellton Cross. Glasgow Libraries holds a small Bargarran Witches collection, including John Shaw of Bargarran’s contemporary account, a commission document, medical declarations, proceedings, confessions and a sermon preached before the commissioners.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://libcat.csglasgow.org/web/arena/bargarran-witches" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: libcat.csglasgow.org">[Glasgow Libraries]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">libcat.csglasgow.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Glasgow Libraries Bargarran Witches</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Glasgow Libraries Bargarran Witches</span></span></span>



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/Renfrewshire_6a148e_paisley_witches_5883e1-overview.webp" | relative_url }}" alt="Overview image for Paisley Witches" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

For folklore readers, the power of the story lies in that uncomfortable overlap between record and legend. The accusations were treated as legal and religious evidence in their own time; today, the same events are remembered as a county-defining tale of fear, social pressure, gendered suspicion, local power and ritualised remembrance. The curse attributed to Agnes Naismith, the horseshoe at Maxwellton Cross and the annual memorial work around Gallow Green show how Paisley has turned a witch-trial archive into a living act of public memory.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.paisleysenchantedthreads.co.uk/the-story/the-events/promises-from-beyond-the-grave/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: paisleysenchantedthreads.co.uk">[Paisley&#x27;s Enchanted Threads]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">paisleysenchantedthreads.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Story &#124; The Events &#124; Promises from beyond the grave &#124; Paisley Witch Hunt of 1697...</span></span></span>

## Christian Shaw and the Bargarran accusations

The case began at Bargarran, the Shaw family house near Erskine, in August 1696. Christian Shaw was a child of local gentry, old enough to be examined and talked about as a religious witness, but too young to give evidence under oath. Paisley’s Enchanted Threads, a local history project devoted to the 1697 witch hunt, stresses that her alleged bewitchment was not the whole case, but that without her illness and accusations the wider hunt probably would not have happened.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.paisleysenchantedthreads.co.uk/the-story/the-people/witness-and-judgement/christian-shaw/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: paisleysenchantedthreads.co.uk">[Paisley&#x27;s Enchanted Threads]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">paisleysenchantedthreads.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Story &#124; The People &#124; Witness and Judgement &#124; Christian Shaw &#124; Paisley Witch Hunt of 1697...</span></span></span>

The earliest trigger in the remembered story was domestic and ordinary: Christian accused a female servant in the household of wrongdoing, and the situation escalated into claims that she was being tormented by witches. Glasgow Libraries summarises the archival core cautiously: Christian alleged that she had been bewitched by one of the female servants, accusations of witchcraft were then taken extremely seriously, and a large number of local people were implicated before seven were executed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://libcat.csglasgow.org/web/arena/bargarran-witches" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: libcat.csglasgow.org">[Glasgow Libraries]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">libcat.csglasgow.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Glasgow Libraries Bargarran Witches</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Glasgow Libraries Bargarran Witches</span></span></span>

What made the Bargarran accusations persuasive to contemporaries was not simply that a child named names. Christian’s symptoms were described as fits, torment by invisible forces, and the production of strange matter from her mouth. Modern readers may recognise how vulnerable such evidence was to misinterpretation, staging, illness, panic or adult coaching, but the late seventeenth-century authorities approached it within a world where demonic possession and witchcraft were legal, theological and social realities. The Survey of Scottish Witchcraft’s reading list treats the Paisley or Bargarran case as a major witch-hunt “sparked off by a case of demonic possession” and points to later psychiatric and historical reassessments of Christian Shaw’s bewitchment.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www-staging.witches.hss.ed.ac.uk/further-reading" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: www-staging.witches.hss.ed.ac.uk">[Witches]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">www-staging.witches.hss.ed.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Witches Further Reading &#124; The Survey of Scottish Witchcraft</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Witches Further Reading &#124; The Survey of Scottish Witchcraft</span></span></span>

Christian’s status mattered. She was not a poor outsider accusing her social superiors; she was the daughter of John Shaw, the Laird of Bargarran. Her family had the standing to summon ministers, physicians, neighbours and eventually legal authority. Glasgow Libraries notes that the manuscript account of her trouble probably belonged to John Shaw himself, and that internal evidence connects it to the Shaw family. That matters because the central source is not neutral village gossip: it comes from the household most invested in presenting Christian as a genuine victim of witchcraft.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://libcat.csglasgow.org/web/arena/bargarran-witches" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: libcat.csglasgow.org">[Glasgow Libraries]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">libcat.csglasgow.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Glasgow Libraries Bargarran Witches</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Glasgow Libraries Bargarran Witches</span></span></span>

The accusations also spread beyond the first household quarrel. Enchanted Threads describes Christian as the foundation on which the prosecution case against the seven was built, but also says that the accused already had reputations among local people for witchcraft. In other words, the hunt fed on older suspicions. Once the community accepted that Christian was bewitched, earlier grudges, rumours and reputations could be pulled into a single terrifying explanation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.paisleysenchantedthreads.co.uk/the-story/the-people/witness-and-judgement/christian-shaw/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: paisleysenchantedthreads.co.uk">[Paisley&#x27;s Enchanted Threads]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">paisleysenchantedthreads.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Story &#124; The People &#124; Witness and Judgement &#124; Christian Shaw &#124; Paisley Witch Hunt of 1697...</span></span></span>

## How a household panic became a Renfrewshire prosecution

The Bargarran case did not remain a private family drama. By late 1696 the Presbytery of Paisley had become involved, and the matter was carried to Edinburgh so that the Privy Council could authorise a formal investigation. Enchanted Threads explains that the Privy Council granted the request, but relied heavily on local people to carry out the proceedings. The commission was made up largely of local gentry, including figures with family, religious or regional connections to the case.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.paisleysenchantedthreads.co.uk/the-story/the-people/witness-and-judgement/the-commission/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: paisleysenchantedthreads.co.uk">[Paisley&#x27;s Enchanted Threads]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">paisleysenchantedthreads.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Story &#124; The People &#124; Witness and Judgement &#124; The Commission &#124; Paisley Witch Hunt of 1697...</span></span></span>

That structure is one reason the story still feels so Renfrewshire-specific. It was not an anonymous state machine descending on Paisley from afar. It was a local world investigating itself: lairds, ministers, doctors, witnesses, neighbours and suspects all caught in the same west-of-Scotland web of kinship, reputation and fear. The commission examined witnesses and suspects, oversaw “pricking” tests on suspected witches, and used experiments in which suspects were made to approach or touch Christian to see whether she would fall into a fit.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.paisleysenchantedthreads.co.uk/the-story/the-people/witness-and-judgement/the-commission/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: paisleysenchantedthreads.co.uk">[Paisley&#x27;s Enchanted Threads]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">paisleysenchantedthreads.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Story &#124; The People &#124; Witness and Judgement &#124; The Commission &#124; Paisley Witch Hunt of 1697...</span></span></span>

To a modern reader, those procedures look circular. If people already believed Christian was tormented by witches, then her reaction to a suspect could be taken as proof of guilt. Yet the process was not experienced as lawless by those conducting it. Enchanted Threads notes that protocol was largely followed by the standards of the time: some accused people were released, the list was narrowed, defence counsel was provided, and the accused were given a chance to answer. This is part of what makes the case disturbing. It was not merely a mob story; it was a formal proceeding in which bad assumptions were processed through official channels.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.paisleysenchantedthreads.co.uk/the-story/the-people/witness-and-judgement/the-commission/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: paisleysenchantedthreads.co.uk">[Paisley&#x27;s Enchanted Threads]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">paisleysenchantedthreads.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Story &#124; The People &#124; Witness and Judgement &#124; The Commission &#124; Paisley Witch Hunt of 1697...</span></span></span>

The case’s geography widened as the accusations spread. Although Paisley is the name most attached to the executions, the drama unfolded across Renfrewshire and nearby places. Enchanted Threads identifies Erskine, Kilbarchan, Renfrew, Kilmalcolm and Dumbarton as significant in the wider pattern of accusation, imprisonment and legend, while Paisley became the place where the trial, public procession and execution fixed the story in civic memory.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.paisleysenchantedthreads.co.uk/the-story/the-places/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: paisleysenchantedthreads.co.uk">[Paisley&#x27;s Enchanted Threads]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">paisleysenchantedthreads.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Story &#124; The Places &#124; Paisley Witch Hunt of 1697...</span></span></span>

## Trial, execution and the named victims

The best-attested endpoint is 10 June 1697. Glasgow Libraries states that the Bargarran case resulted in seven executions in Paisley on that date, adding the striking detail that Christian Shaw’s symptoms had vanished in March 1697, before the executions took place.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://libcat.csglasgow.org/web/arena/bargarran-witches" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: libcat.csglasgow.org">[Glasgow Libraries]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">libcat.csglasgow.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Glasgow Libraries Bargarran Witches</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Glasgow Libraries Bargarran Witches</span></span></span>

The seven people remembered as the condemned victims were Katherine Campbell, Margaret Lang, Margaret Fulton, Agnes Naismith, John Lindsay, James Lindsay and John Lindsay of Barloch. Some popular summaries include John Reid among those condemned, but he is usually distinguished as an accused man who died in custody before execution. This is one of the details where retellings can blur, and it is worth keeping the focus on the local memory of seven executed victims at Paisley and the wider circle of people suspected, imprisoned or examined. Paisley’s Enchanted Threads says more than thirty people were suspected or accused across Renfrewshire during the first five months of 1697, before the prosecution narrowed to seven for trial.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.paisleysenchantedthreads.co.uk/the-story/the-people/the-victims/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: paisleysenchantedthreads.co.uk">[Paisley&#x27;s Enchanted Threads]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">paisleysenchantedthreads.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">the victims</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">the victims</span></span></span>

The execution site is often called Gallow Green, but even this landscape has layers. Enchanted Threads places the execution of the guilty at Gallow Green in Paisley on 10 June 1697, with the remains later associated with Maxwellton Cross.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.paisleysenchantedthreads.co.uk/the-story/the-places/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: paisleysenchantedthreads.co.uk">[Paisley&#x27;s Enchanted Threads]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">paisleysenchantedthreads.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Story &#124; The Places &#124; Paisley Witch Hunt of 1697...</span></span></span> Discover Renfrewshire Heritage adds a more topographical argument: recent research suggests the actual execution took place on Gallow Hill, part of Castlehead Hill, while the surviving “Gallow Green” area near Queen Street may have been more likely a gathering place for spectators than the exact place of burning.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://discoverrenfrewshireheritage.com/the-bargarran-witchcraft-case-1696/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: discoverrenfrewshireheritage.com">[discoverrenfrewshireheritage.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">discoverrenfrewshireheritage.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">the bargarran witchcraft case 1696</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">the bargarran witchcraft case 1696</span></span></span>

That distinction matters for folklore because places become symbolic even when the precise ground shifts. Gallow Green, Gallow Hill and Maxwellton Cross have all become part of the remembered route of death: from accusation at Bargarran, to confinement and legal process in Paisley, to public execution, burning and the disposal of remains. The modern visitor is not just looking for a single dot on a map, but for a landscape of accusation and aftermath.

The executions are often described as the last great witch hunt or last mass execution for witchcraft in Scotland, sometimes even in western Europe. Enchanted Threads uses this broader framing but notes that pockets of witch-hunting continued for some years and that old beliefs lasted longer among the wider population. That careful wording is useful: Paisley marks a major endpoint, not the instant disappearance of witch belief.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.paisleysenchantedthreads.co.uk/the-story/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: paisleysenchantedthreads.co.uk">[Paisley&#x27;s Enchanted Threads]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">paisleysenchantedthreads.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Story &#124; Paisley Witch Hunt of 1697...</span></span></span>

## Why the evidence still troubles readers

The Bargarran case survives unusually well because of the documentary trail. Glasgow Libraries’ collection includes John Shaw’s account, the commission document, medical declarations by Dr Matthew Brisbane and Henry Marshall, proceedings, confessions, and a sermon before the commissioners.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://libcat.csglasgow.org/web/arena/bargarran-witches" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: libcat.csglasgow.org">[Glasgow Libraries]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">libcat.csglasgow.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Glasgow Libraries Bargarran Witches</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Glasgow Libraries Bargarran Witches</span></span></span> Those materials make the case far more than a vague legend, but they do not make the accusations true. They show how people in authority recorded, organised and justified belief.

Several features make the evidence troubling rather than merely curious. First, Christian was a child examined in a highly charged religious and legal environment. Enchanted Threads notes that her statement was used as evidence and that she was examined at trial, but because of her age she did not give evidence under oath.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.paisleysenchantedthreads.co.uk/the-story/the-people/witness-and-judgement/christian-shaw/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: paisleysenchantedthreads.co.uk">[Paisley&#x27;s Enchanted Threads]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">paisleysenchantedthreads.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Story &#124; The People &#124; Witness and Judgement &#124; Christian Shaw &#124; Paisley Witch Hunt of 1697...</span></span></span> Second, the investigators used tests that assumed witchcraft could reveal itself physically. A suspect’s touch provoking a fit could be treated as incriminating, even though such a test depended on the behaviour of the alleged victim and the expectations of everyone present.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.paisleysenchantedthreads.co.uk/the-story/the-people/witness-and-judgement/the-commission/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: paisleysenchantedthreads.co.uk">[Paisley&#x27;s Enchanted Threads]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">paisleysenchantedthreads.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Story &#124; The People &#124; Witness and Judgement &#124; The Commission &#124; Paisley Witch Hunt of 1697...</span></span></span>

Third, the social setting was not equal. Christian came from a laird’s household, while several accused people appear in the records as servants, tenants, poorer neighbours or people already burdened by reputation. The case shows how quickly fear could travel down social lines. A curse uttered by a servant, a rumour attached to an older woman, or a reputation for uncanny knowledge could become lethal once a respectable household and local ministers accepted the bewitchment narrative.

Modern interpretation has tended to move away from asking whether Christian was “really bewitched” and towards asking how illness, performance, fear, religious culture and legal belief interacted. The Survey of Scottish Witchcraft points readers to a psychiatric reassessment and to historical work reassessing the famous Paisley case.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www-staging.witches.hss.ed.ac.uk/further-reading" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: www-staging.witches.hss.ed.ac.uk">[Witches]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">www-staging.witches.hss.ed.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Witches Further Reading &#124; The Survey of Scottish Witchcraft</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Witches Further Reading &#124; The Survey of Scottish Witchcraft</span></span></span> That does not mean one modern explanation neatly solves everything. The safer conclusion is that the evidence is strong for what people claimed, feared and did; it is not strong evidence that supernatural harm occurred.

## Agnes Naismith’s curse and the horseshoe at Maxwellton Cross

The most powerful piece of folklore to grow from the executions is the curse attached to Agnes Naismith, often joined in some retellings with Katherine Campbell. According to Enchanted Threads, both women were said to have gone to the scaffold angrily and unrepentantly, calling down divine wrath on those who witnessed the scene. The curse became one of the most enduring stories associated with the Renfrewshire witch hunt.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.paisleysenchantedthreads.co.uk/the-story/the-events/promises-from-beyond-the-grave/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: paisleysenchantedthreads.co.uk">[Paisley&#x27;s Enchanted Threads]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">paisleysenchantedthreads.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Story &#124; The Events &#124; Promises from beyond the grave &#124; Paisley Witch Hunt of 1697...</span></span></span>

This is where the story moves from trial record into folk practice. At Maxwellton Cross, where the remains of the executed were believed to have been disposed of, an iron horseshoe was laid as a protective sign. Enchanted Threads interprets this as evidence that ordinary people took the curse seriously: horseshoes were associated with luck, iron had long-standing protective associations, and the act was unlikely to have been encouraged by the Church, which would have regarded such counter-magic suspiciously.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.paisleysenchantedthreads.co.uk/the-story/the-events/promises-from-beyond-the-grave/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: paisleysenchantedthreads.co.uk">[Paisley&#x27;s Enchanted Threads]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">paisleysenchantedthreads.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Story &#124; The Events &#124; Promises from beyond the grave &#124; Paisley Witch Hunt of 1697...</span></span></span>

Maxwellton Cross itself is central to the legend. Enchanted Threads describes it as lying south-west of the town and locally infamous as the place where the remains of the seven executed witches were disposed of, just yards from the Gallow Green. It also explains the crossroads logic: in popular belief, a crossroads could confuse or contain a dangerous spirit, preventing the dead from returning to trouble the living.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.paisleysenchantedthreads.co.uk/the-story/the-places/maxwellton-cross/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: paisleysenchantedthreads.co.uk">[Paisley&#x27;s Enchanted Threads]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">paisleysenchantedthreads.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Story &#124; The Places &#124; Maxwellton Cross &#124; Paisley Witch Hunt of 1697...</span></span></span>

The horseshoe tradition gave Paisley a physical charm against its own guilty memory. It was said that if the horseshoe were removed, Paisley would suffer decline, accidents or deaths. The original iron horseshoe was eventually lost, and a replacement now marks the place.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.paisleysenchantedthreads.co.uk/the-story/the-events/promises-from-beyond-the-grave/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: paisleysenchantedthreads.co.uk">[Paisley&#x27;s Enchanted Threads]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">paisleysenchantedthreads.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Story &#124; The Events &#124; Promises from beyond the grave &#124; Paisley Witch Hunt of 1697...</span></span></span> In modern memorial language, the point has changed. The old horseshoe tried to hold down danger; the newer memorial asks people to acknowledge pain, suffering and injustice.

## Modern remembrance in Paisley

The Paisley witches are now remembered less as uncanny villains than as victims of law, fear and religious certainty. That change is visible in local memorial activity. A Scottish Parliament motion lodged on 22 June 2022 congratulated Paisley Witch Hunt 1697 for organising a memorial ceremony at Gallow Green on 10 June 2022, noting the injustice done to seven people accused, tried and found guilty of witchcraft. It also recorded that the group campaigns to make Gallow Green a more accessible memorial site and to raise awareness of this part of Paisley’s history.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.parliament.scot/chamber-and-committees/votes-and-motions/votes-and-motions-search/S6M-04958" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: parliament.scot">[Scottish Parliament]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">parliament.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-title">S6M 04958</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">S6M 04958</span></span></span>

The national context has also shifted. In 2022 a proposal for a Scottish Parliament Bill sought to pardon all those convicted under the Witchcraft Act 1563, although the proposal was later withdrawn in April 2023.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.parliament.scot/bills-and-laws/proposals-for-bills/proposed-the-witchcraft-convictions-pardons-scotland-bill" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: parliament.scot">[Scottish Parliament]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">parliament.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on parliament.scot.</span></span></span> The Scottish Government’s own 2026 FOI response confirms that the 2022 apology for the Witchcraft Act was partly connected to public petition PE1855 and to the then First Minister’s statement setting out the rationale for apology.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gov.scot/publications/foi-202600509137/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gov.scot">[Scottish Government]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gov.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-title">foi 202600509137</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">foi 202600509137</span></span></span>

For Renfrewshire, this modern reckoning gives the Bargarran accusations a double identity. They are still local folklore: a girl in torment, a servant’s curse, a procession to the gallows, a crossroads burial, a horseshoe against misfortune. But they are also public history: a documented case of accusation, prosecution and execution now used to talk about injustice, misogyny, class, authority and the danger of communities explaining suffering through scapegoats.

That is why Paisley’s witch hunt still matters. It is not a ghost story floating free of evidence, nor a dry legal episode with the life drained out of it. It is a Renfrewshire memory in which archive, landscape and legend keep correcting one another. Bargarran gives the story its origin, Paisley gives it its civic stage, Gallow Green gives it its horror, and Maxwellton Cross gives it the symbol by which the town has tried, for more than three centuries, to contain and reinterpret what happened.

    section.setAttribute('data-ebay-localized-init', '1');
    var available = availableMarkets(section);
    var marketId = defaultMarket;
    if (window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation && window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation.inferMarket) {
      marketId = window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation.inferMarket(available, {
        storageKey: 'phoenix-ebay-market',
        defaultMarket: defaultMarket
      });
    } else if (available.indexOf(defaultMarket)< 0) {
      marketId = available[0] || defaultMarket;
    }
    var select = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-select]');
    if (select) {
      select.addEventListener('change', function () {
        applyMarket(section, select.value, true);
      });
    }
    var trigger = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-trigger]');
    var menu = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-menu]');
    if (trigger && menu) {
      trigger.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
        event.stopPropagation();
        var expanded = trigger.getAttribute('aria-expanded') === 'true';
        menu.hidden = expanded;
        trigger.setAttribute('aria-expanded', expanded ? 'false' : 'true');
        var picker = trigger.closest('.fr-ebay-market-picker');
        if (picker) picker.classList.toggle('fr-ebay-market-picker--open', !expanded);
      });
      Array.prototype.slice.call(menu.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-option]')).forEach(function (button) {
        button.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
          event.stopPropagation();
          applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-option'), true);
        });
      });
      document.addEventListener('click', function () { closeMenu(section); });
    }
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      button.addEventListener('click', function () {
        applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button'), true);
      });
    });
    applyMarket(section, marketId, false);
  });
