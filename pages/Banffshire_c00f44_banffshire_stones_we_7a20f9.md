## Introduction

[Banffshire]({{ 'what-mysteries-shaped-banffshires/' | relative_url }})’s stones, wells and sacred places preserve some of the clearest links between landscape, archaeology and local memory. Across the historic county, prehistoric monuments, springs and old meeting places were not simply physical features: communities attached stories, rituals and explanations to them over generations. Some stones were remembered as markers of ancestors or important events, while wells were associated with healing, protection and religious devotion. The surviving evidence shows a landscape where older monuments and later beliefs often overlapped, even when the original purpose of a site had been forgotten.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://canmore.org.uk/site/18332/charterstone" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: canmore.org.uk">[Canmore]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">canmore.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Canmore Charterstone &#124; Canmore</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Canmore Charterstone &#124; Canmore</span></span></span>



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/Banffshire_c00f44_banffshire_stones_we_7a20f9-overview.webp" | relative_url }}" alt="Overview image for Sacred Landscapes" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

The challenge is separating what can be demonstrated archaeologically from what belongs to later storytelling. A standing stone may genuinely be thousands of years old, while a tale linking it to giants, saints or local events may have developed centuries later. This mixture of physical survival and changing interpretation is what makes Banffshire’s sacred landscapes important within Scottish folklore.

## Ancient monuments and later legends

### Stones that outlived their makers

Banffshire contains prehistoric monuments that show long histories of human activity before written records. One example is the Doune of Dalmore, a Bronze Age site near the meeting of the Rivers Avon and Livet. Archaeological recording identifies it as a probable Clava-type ring cairn, originally surrounded by standing stones. Although later interpretations of such monuments often focus on mystery and ritual, the physical evidence points to a carefully constructed prehistoric burial and ceremonial landscape.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://canmore.org.uk/site/16029/doune-of-dalmore" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: canmore.org.uk">[Canmore]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">canmore.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Canmore Doune Of Dalmore &#124; Canmore</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Canmore Doune Of Dalmore &#124; Canmore</span></span></span>

Such places became ideal settings for folklore because later communities inherited the stones without knowing exactly why they had been placed there. Across Scotland, standing stones were often explained through stories of giants turned to stone, supernatural builders, ancient battles or forgotten heroes. These explanations did not replace the monument’s history; they were ways of giving meaning to something that appeared ancient and unusual.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.visitscotland.com/things-to-do/attractions/historic/standing-stones" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: visitscotland.com">[VisitScotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">visitscotland.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">7 Standing Stones in Scotland &#124; VisitScotlandAccording to local folklore, they were giants who refused to convert to Christi...</span></span></span>

A smaller but revealing example is the former Conwath Stone, also known as the Charter Stone or Conway Stone. Historic accounts recorded a tall upright stone on the Hill of Balnoon, surrounded by a slight ditch. Although the stone has since disappeared, nineteenth-century descriptions preserve memories of how the place was used. Local tradition connected it with funeral customs, with mourners reportedly stopping there during journeys towards the churchyard.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://canmore.org.uk/site/18332/charterstone" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: canmore.org.uk">[Canmore]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">canmore.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Canmore Charterstone &#124; Canmore</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Canmore Charterstone &#124; Canmore</span></span></span>

The Conwath Stone illustrates an important feature of sacred landscapes: meaning often comes from repeated community use rather than from a single dramatic legend. A stone could become significant because generations of people passed it, gathered near it or included it in ceremonies, even when its prehistoric origin was uncertain.

## Holy wells and healing customs

### Water as a place of belief

Springs and wells were among the most widespread sacred places in Scotland. They were valued because water was essential for life and because isolated springs often seemed to possess unusual qualities. Traditions surrounding holy wells commonly included healing, prayers, offerings and customs intended to bring good fortune. Archaeological and folklore studies note that Scottish holy wells have long histories, although proving exact origins can be difficult because many sites were altered or renamed over time.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://scarf.scot/regional/higharf/early-medieval/8-6-religion-and-ritual-evidence/8-6-6-holy-wells/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: scarf.scot">[Scarf]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">scarf.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-title">8.6.6 Holy Wells &#124; The Scottish Archaeological Research Framework</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">8.6.6 Holy Wells &#124; The Scottish Archaeological Research Framework...</span></span></span>

In Banffshire, memories of sacred water belong to the wider north-east Scottish tradition of wells connected with health and protection. The nineteenth- and early twentieth-century recording of local beliefs shows how widely communities understood wells as places where illness could be treated or where spiritual help might be sought. The Banffshire Field Club preserved material on north-east folk beliefs, including discussion of the importance of holy wells and their healing associations.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://banffshirefieldclub.org.uk/PDFs/BOOK_12/Banffshire_Field_Club_Transactions_1931-1934_-_1932_Folk_Beliefs_in_the_North-east_of_Scotland_WM.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: banffshirefieldclub.org.uk">[Banffshire Field Club]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">banffshirefieldclub.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Banffshire Field ClubBanffshire Field Club Transactions 1931-1934March 11, 2019...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 11, 2019</span></span></span>

These traditions often existed between older landscape beliefs and Christian practice. A spring might become associated with a saint, yet the importance of the water itself could reflect much older attitudes towards special places in nature. Rather than assuming every holy well began as a pre-Christian shrine, historians usually treat each site individually: some may have ancient associations, while others gained importance through medieval or later religious traditions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://scarf.scot/regional/higharf/early-medieval/8-6-religion-and-ritual-evidence/8-6-6-holy-wells/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: scarf.scot">[Scarf]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">scarf.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-title">8.6.6 Holy Wells &#124; The Scottish Archaeological Research Framework</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">8.6.6 Holy Wells &#124; The Scottish Archaeological Research Framework...</span></span></span>

### Healing stories and community memory

The folklore of wells was practical as well as spiritual. People visited them for specific needs: recovery from illness, protection of livestock, fertility concerns or personal hopes. Customs varied from place to place, but the underlying idea was that certain parts of the landscape had a special ability to help humans.

For communities in rural Banffshire, a well could therefore act as a local archive. The story attached to a spring recorded what people feared, hoped for and valued. Even when a well stopped being used, its name or memory could preserve traces of older beliefs.

## Archaeology versus folklore memories

### What the stones can prove

Archaeology is strongest when identifying age, construction and physical changes. Excavation, survey and historic records can show whether a monument is a Bronze Age cairn, a standing stone, a later boundary marker or a reused site. Records such as the national archaeological database entries for Scottish monuments provide evidence of surviving structures and their histories.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://canmore.org.uk/site/18332/charterstone" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: canmore.org.uk">[Canmore]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">canmore.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Canmore Charterstone &#124; Canmore</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Canmore Charterstone &#124; Canmore</span></span></span>

What archaeology usually cannot prove is the exact story people later told about a place. A monument may have been built for burial or ceremony, but later generations might remember it as the work of giants or as a place connected with supernatural events.

### Why the legends still matter

Folklore does not have to be historically accurate to be culturally important. Stories attached to stones and wells reveal how people negotiated their relationship with the land. They explain why certain places deserved respect, why some areas were avoided, and why particular features remained memorable.

Banffshire’s sacred places are therefore best understood as layered landscapes. A prehistoric stone circle, an old spring or a forgotten roadside marker may contain several histories at once: the original builders’ intentions, later religious meanings, local customs and modern interest in heritage.

Today, these places continue to shape how Banffshire is imagined. They connect archaeology with storytelling and show how ordinary features of the countryside became landmarks of memory, belief and identity. The lasting importance of these sites lies not only in their age, but in the generations of people who continued to give them meaning.
