## Introduction

[Sussex]({{ 'why-sussex-still-feels-enchanted/' | relative_url }}) fairy lore is less about glittering storybook fairies than about small, elusive “Pharisees”: hidden people of the Downs, woods, fields and old earthworks. They dance in rings, help with farm work if treated respectfully, punish mockery, and gradually retreat from England as religion, archaeology, modern roads and modern disbelief press in on their old places. The most memorable Sussex version is the tradition that Harrow Hill, near Patching and Angmering, was the last home of the fairies in England; the University of Chichester folklore map records it directly under that claim, while Historic England identifies the hill itself as a protected prehistoric landscape of Neolithic flint mines and later enclosure earthworks.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.chi.ac.uk/app/uploads/2022/07/Folklore-Map-Interactive-compressed.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: chi.ac.uk">[University of Chichester]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">chi.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">University of Chichester</span></span></span>



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/Sussex_0f32ec_pharisees_fairy_hill_253844-overview.webp" | relative_url }}" alt="Overview image for Fairies" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

This matters because Sussex fairies are not floating decoration. They are part of how people remembered the chalk country: ancient shafts, hillforts, green rings in grass, awkward lanes, night work, neighbourly obligation and the feeling that old presences had been driven into smaller and smaller refuges. In Sussex, enchantment does not simply appear; it withdraws.

## Why Sussex fairies were called Pharisees

The oddest thing about Sussex fairies is often the first thing readers notice: the name “Pharisees”. It looks biblical, but in Sussex folklore it normally means fairies. The clearest explanation is dialectal. The form is usually treated as a local or south-eastern development from “fairies”, probably strengthened by double-plural forms such as “fairieses” and then confused in spelling or sound with the New Testament Pharisees. Modern fairy-lore commentary traces related forms across southern and eastern England: Sussex, Surrey and Suffolk have “farisees” or “farisies”, Kent has “fairisies”, and East Anglia has related forms such as “frairies”.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://britishfairies.wordpress.com/tag/farisees/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: britishfairies.wordpress.com">[British Fairies]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">britishfairies.wordpress.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">British Fairiesfarisees &#124; British Fairies</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">British Fairiesfarisees &#124; British Fairies</span></span></span>

That confusion made the word culturally useful. Country people hearing “Pharisees” in church could fold the term back into local belief, while educated collectors could misread it as evidence that rural speakers had muddled Bible stories with fairy stories. The better reading is subtler: the word shows how dialect, religion and oral tradition overlapped. A name that began as “fairies” could sound biblical enough to make the beings feel strangely authorised, yet still remain rooted in Sussex speech rather than in official doctrine.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://britishfairies.wordpress.com/tag/farisees/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: britishfairies.wordpress.com">[British Fairies]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">britishfairies.wordpress.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">British Fairiesfarisees &#124; British Fairies</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">British Fairiesfarisees &#124; British Fairies</span></span></span>

The Sussex stories themselves present the Pharisees as morally sensitive. They are not simply good or wicked. They help when ignored politely, disappear when laughed at, and punish humans who break the delicate rules of courtesy. The University of Chichester map preserves two compact examples: at Washington, Pharisees helped a farmer but stopped after being congratulated; at Selmeston, a carter saw them feeding his horses, mocked their sweating, and afterwards his horses wasted away.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.chi.ac.uk/app/uploads/2022/07/Folklore-Map-Interactive-compressed.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: chi.ac.uk">[University of Chichester]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">chi.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">University of Chichester</span></span></span>

These tales are small, but they reveal a lot. Sussex Pharisees belong to a world where invisible labour has rules. Do not pry. Do not boast. Do not laugh at helpers. Do not assume that a gift is free just because no human hand is visible. That is why they sit comfortably beside other Sussex traditions about dangerous roads, uncanny mounds, witches, [black dogs]({{ 'black-dogs-100e5c/' | relative_url }}) and haunted water: they turn everyday rural behaviour into a moral test.

## Harrow Hill and the last fairies

Harrow Hill is the key Sussex fairy hill because it gathers archaeology, landscape and vanishing enchantment into one place. It lies on the South Downs near Patching and Angmering, in the old western part of Sussex. Historic England lists it as a Scheduled Monument: a Neolithic flint mine and later Martin Down-style enclosure on a chalk hill, with around 245 identified shafts and pits occupying about eight hectares.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://historicengland.org.uk/listing/the-list/list-entry/1015239" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: historicengland.org.uk">[Historic England]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">historicengland.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on historicengland.org.uk.</span></span></span>

The archaeology matters for the folklore. This is not a random “fairy hill” invented for atmosphere. Harrow Hill is full of visible hollows and old workings, the kind of terrain that invites stories about hidden inhabitants under the ground. A Historic England research report describes distinct mining areas, including large shafts on the north and east of the hilltop and smaller pits or open-cast mining to the south; it also suggests an Early Neolithic phase, with a radiocarbon date around 3710 BC for deep-shaft mining.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://historicengland.org.uk/research/results/reports/7643/HARROWHILLWESTSUSSEXAnEarthworkSurveyINDUSTRYANDENCLOSUREINTHENEOLITHIC" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: historicengland.org.uk">[Historic England]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">historicengland.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Historic England</span></span></span>

Sussex is unusually rich in this kind of prehistoric mining landscape. The Novium Museum notes that seven of the ten confirmed Neolithic flint mines in the British Isles are in Sussex, including the Worthing-area group of Cissbury, Church Hill, Harrow Hill and Blackpatch. These mines date to the early centuries of the Neolithic, around the period when farming and new material practices were spreading through southern England.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.thenovium.org/article/31055/Early-Neolithic-Flint-Mines-of-Sussex" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: thenovium.org">[The Novium Museum]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">thenovium.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Novium Museum Early Neolithic Flint Mines of Sussex</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Novium Museum Early Neolithic Flint Mines of Sussex</span></span></span>

The folklore claim attached to Harrow Hill is stark: it was the last home of the fairies in England. The University of Chichester folklore map gives the tradition in that form and links it to Jacqueline Simpson’s Sussex collection.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.chi.ac.uk/app/uploads/2022/07/Folklore-Map-Interactive-compressed.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: chi.ac.uk">[University of Chichester]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">chi.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">University of Chichester</span></span></span> Modern retellings often add that the fairies finally left when archaeologists excavated or disturbed the hill. That detail should be handled carefully: it is a tradition about modernity arriving at an old place, not proof that anyone watched a fairy migration. Its power lies in the contrast. The hill’s hollows once suggested hidden life; excavation turned them into evidence.

Harrow Hill therefore becomes a Sussex parable about explanation. The same pits can be read in two ways. Archaeology sees early mining, labour organisation, flint extraction and long prehistoric use. Folklore sees underground neighbours, last refuges and the sorrow of departure. The two readings do not have to cancel each other. Together, they show why the Downs felt enchanted: their surface is marked by human activity so old that, before modern archaeology, story was one of the main ways people made sense of it.

## Fairy hills, rings and midnight dancing

Harrow Hill is the most famous Sussex fairy refuge, but it belongs to a wider pattern of fairy places on the Downs. Sussex Archaeological Society’s fairy folklore page, though not always easily accessible in full, is indexed as naming Cissbury Ring, Chanctonbury Ring, Harrow Hill and Tarberry Hill as Iron Age hillforts haunted by fairies at midnight on Midsummer Eve.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sussexarch.org.uk/saaf/fairies.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sussexarch.org.uk">[sussexarch.org.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sussexarch.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on sussexarch.org.uk.</span></span></span> The Chichester map also records fairies at Cissbury Ring, where treasure is guarded by snakes and where fairies can be seen dancing on Midsummer Eve.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.chi.ac.uk/app/uploads/2022/07/Folklore-Map-Interactive-compressed.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: chi.ac.uk">[University of Chichester]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">chi.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">University of Chichester</span></span></span>

This pattern makes sense on the ground. The South Downs are full of ancient enclosures, barrows, trackways, flint mines and chalk scars. A ring on a hill is already a dramatic human-made shape; a green ring in a field is a smaller natural echo of the same idea. Sussex fairy lore repeatedly turns circles into thresholds: the dancing ring, the hillfort ring, the ring of hidden people whose world briefly touches ours.

Hilaire Belloc gave this tradition a literary Sussex form in *The Four Men*. In the All-Halloween passage, the “Little People” dance in meadows, undergrowth, reeds, water meadows and heaths, leaving brighter green wheels and circles in the grass. Belloc’s scene is not a neutral collection note; it is a romantic, early twentieth-century literary reworking of country belief. But it shows how easily Sussex fairy lore moved from oral report into county literature, especially when writers wanted the landscape to feel old, intimate and half-lost.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/download/fourmenfarrago0000bell/fourmenfarrago0000bell.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Internet Archive The four men: a farrago</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Internet Archive The four men: a farrago</span></span></span>

The important distinction is that “fairy rings” in Sussex writing can work at several levels at once. They may preserve older folk explanations for unusual grass growth. They may be literary symbols of youth, memory or loss. They may also be tourist-friendly labels for uncanny places on the Downs. A good reading of Sussex fairy lore keeps those layers separate without stripping the stories of their charm.

## Kipling, Belloc and literary Sussex

Rudyard Kipling and Hilaire Belloc are central to how many modern readers meet Sussex enchantment, but they do not play the same role as local informants. They are literary makers who used Sussex traditions, dialect, landscape and older motifs to create a mythic version of the county.

Kipling’s *Puck of Pook’s Hill* is especially important. It opens with children in a Sussex landscape where old trackways, camps, Roman traces, flint marks and marshes are all folded into “Merlin’s Isle of Gramarye”.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/files/557/557-h/557-h.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Project Gutenberg E-text of Puck of Pook&#x27;s Hill, by Rudyard Kipling...</span></span></span> In “Weland’s Sword”, Puck speaks of “the People of the Hills” as beings who do not show themselves unless they choose, and the story turns on an old moral rule very close to Sussex fairy etiquette: if you take help, give thanks.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/files/557/557-h/557-h.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Project Gutenberg E-text of Puck of Pook&#x27;s Hill, by Rudyard Kipling...</span></span></span>

Kipling’s “Dymchurch Flit” is even closer to the Sussex “Pharisees” tradition, although Dymchurch itself lies in Kent’s Romney Marsh rather than in Sussex. In the tale, marsh people say the Pharisees once favoured the Marsh; they dance, show little green lights, lock church doors, and then flee England during the Reformation because the destruction of images and religious ill-will have made the country unbearable to them.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/files/557/557-h/557-h.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Project Gutenberg E-text of Puck of Pook&#x27;s Hill, by Rudyard Kipling...</span></span></span> This is not a simple Sussex field report. It is Kipling’s historical fantasy, but it powerfully expresses the same “vanishing enchantment” motif found at Harrow Hill: old beings retreat when the human world changes.

Belloc’s Sussex is more elegiac. *The Four Men* is a walking book, a county love-letter and a farrago of songs, arguments, jokes and visionary passages. Its fairy material belongs to that mixed form. The Halloween passage turns fairy rings into proof offered to “unbelieving men”, while the later discussion of fairy gold becoming dead leaves makes fairy encounter a metaphor for poetic illusion, youth and loss.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/download/fourmenfarrago0000bell/fourmenfarrago0000bell.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Internet Archive The four men: a farrago</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Internet Archive The four men: a farrago</span></span></span>

Both writers helped preserve a public sense of Sussex as a place where old stories still cling to hills and woods. But their work should not be mistaken for untouched oral tradition. Kipling and Belloc are part of the afterlife of the folklore: they made Sussex fairies memorable to readers who may never have heard a farmer, carter or shepherd speak of Pharisees at all.

## What “vanishing enchantment” really means

The Sussex fairy tradition is full of departures. The Pharisees leave a farm when thanked or mocked too openly. They leave Harrow Hill when excavation exposes their last refuge. In Kipling’s literary version, they leave England because religious conflict and iconoclasm poison the air of Merry England. These are different stories, but they share one emotional structure: enchantment survives only while people behave with tact, goodwill and restraint.

That makes Sussex fairy lore unusually self-aware. Many fairy traditions across Britain say that the fairies have gone, are going, or were last seen by grandparents. Sussex gives that disappearance a local geography. Harrow Hill is not just “a fairy place”; it is the last place. Cissbury, Chanctonbury and Tarberry are not just old forts; they are midnight dancing sites. Selmeston and Washington are not just villages; they preserve small moral dramas about how humans mishandle invisible help.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.chi.ac.uk/app/uploads/2022/07/Folklore-Map-Interactive-compressed.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: chi.ac.uk">[University of Chichester]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">chi.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">University of Chichester</span></span></span>

The South Downs National Park Authority’s report on the Sussex folklore-mapping project makes the broader point well: stories in the South Downs grow around ancient flint mines, barrows and hillforts because “the shape of the land shapes our myths and legends”.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.southdowns.gov.uk/ghosts-and-fairies-in-the-south-downs/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: southdowns.gov.uk">[South Downs National Park]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">southdowns.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">South Downs National Park Ghosts and fairies in the South Downs</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">South Downs National Park Ghosts and fairies in the South Downs</span></span></span> That is exactly what happens with the Pharisees. They are not detachable fantasy creatures. They are a way of reading a worked, walked and remembered landscape.

There is also a modern irony. Archaeology, heritage mapping and folklore tourism are among the forces that keep these fairies visible today. The very activities that the Harrow Hill story imagines as driving the fairies away now help preserve the tale. Historic England protects the hill as archaeology; the University of Chichester map returns the fairy tradition to public view; local writers and walkers retell it as part of Sussex identity.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://historicengland.org.uk/listing/the-list/list-entry/1015239" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: historicengland.org.uk">[Historic England]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">historicengland.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on historicengland.org.uk.</span></span></span>

## Why Sussex fairies still matter

Sussex’s Pharisees matter because they make the county’s old landscape feel morally alive. They turn chalk hollows, hill rings and green circles into places where the past has agency. They also preserve a rural code: be courteous, do not mock what you do not understand, and remember that help may come from beyond the visible social world.

For a modern reader, the best way to approach these stories is neither to flatten them into “just superstition” nor to treat them as literal evidence of hidden beings. They are traditions: shaped by dialect, collected by antiquarians and folklorists, reworked by writers, and now mapped back onto footpaths and heritage sites. Their truth is cultural and imaginative. They tell us how Sussex people and Sussex writers explained the feeling that the Downs were older, stranger and less empty than they looked.

The answer to “Where did Sussex fairies go?” is therefore layered. In the stories, they withdrew into hills, fled from human offence, or left England altogether. In cultural memory, they went into books, maps, place-lore and walking routes. At Harrow Hill especially, the vanished Pharisees still do their old work: they make a prehistoric chalk hill feel like a threshold between evidence and wonder.
