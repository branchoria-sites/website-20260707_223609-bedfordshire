## Introduction

[Northumberland]({{ 'where-northumberlands-strange-stories/' | relative_url }})’s fairy lore is not confined to dainty winged figures in woods. In this county, fairies and fairy-like beings are tied to hills, wells, upland greens, mills, old paths and coal mines. Some are dancers and wish-granters; some are underground workers; some are dangerous beings who punish trespass, disrespect or foolish curiosity. The useful answer to “Where do Northumberland’s fairies actually live?” is therefore: in the places where landscape, work and risk meet.



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/Northumberland_98885a_fairy_hills_wells_mi_be46f7-overview.webp" | relative_url }}" alt="Overview image for Fairy Places" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

The best-attested examples sit in nineteenth- and early twentieth-century folklore collecting: Wooler’s Fairy, Wishing, Maiden or Pin Well; the fairy court of Fawdon Hill; the hill and green traditions listed in local collections; and the mining goblins Bluecap and Cutty Soams. These traditions matter because they show Northumberland fairy belief adapting to very different environments: Cheviot-edge springs, border hills, rural mills and industrial pits. Rather than one simple “fairy people”, Northumberland preserves a mixed population of local spirits whose homes reveal what communities noticed, feared and valued.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/stream/examplesprinted00balfgoog/examplesprinted00balfgoog_djvu.txt" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on archive.org.</span></span></span>

## Why Northumberland’s fairy places are so varied

Northumberland is a county of sharp contrasts: upland fells, fortified border country, coastal settlements, river valleys and a coalfield in the south-east. Historic-county descriptions place it between the Tyne and the Tweed, with a rugged coast, Cheviot uplands, castles, peel towers and coal measures all forming part of the same county identity.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://wikishire.co.uk/wiki/Northumberland" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wikishire.co.uk">[Wikishire]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wikishire.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wikishire.co.uk.</span></span></span> That variety helps explain why Northumberland fairy lore does not settle into one neat type.

In some stories, fairies belong to the old rural and upland imagination: they dance on hills, gather at greens, preside over springs and appear at liminal times such as midnight or May Day. In others, the supernatural enters the working world: pottage-making fairies at mills, mine goblins in collieries, and helpful or troublesome underground beings whose behaviour mirrors the rules and anxieties of miners’ labour. Modern folklore summaries often group these together as “fairy folk”, but the older evidence suggests a broader category of small or hidden beings, not a single species with fixed behaviour.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://betweenworldsdurham.wordpress.com/2018/03/08/the-fairy-folk-of-northumberland/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: betweenworldsdurham.wordpress.com">[Between Worlds]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">betweenworldsdurham.wordpress.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Between Worlds The Fairy Folk of Northumberland &#124; Between Worlds</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Between Worlds The Fairy Folk of Northumberland &#124; Between Worlds</span></span></span>

The result is a distinctively Northumbrian fairy geography. Hills and wells are not decorative backdrops; they are the story’s engine. A spring becomes a place of wishing because people repeatedly visit it, drop offerings into it and tell one another what should happen next. A hill becomes a fairy court because it already feels separate from ordinary ground: raised, old, visible, possibly associated with earthworks or ancient memory. A mine becomes haunted because underground work is dangerous, noisy, dark, hierarchical and full of unexplained knocks, lights and accidents. Fairy belief attaches itself to those conditions.

## Hills, greens and wells in fairy tradition

Northumberland’s fairy hills and wells are best understood as “threshold places”: points where ordinary life seems to brush against a hidden world. They are usually not far from human use. Wooler’s well was visited by young people; Fawdon Hill is part of a named local landscape; other fairy places occur near villages, roads, mills and old gathering sites. The stories are supernatural, but their settings are socially practical: places where people walked, worked, courted, met and remembered.

The clearest well example is at Wooler. M. C. Balfour’s 1904 *Examples of Printed Folk-Lore Concerning Northumberland* records a Wooler well where lovers went at midnight, bent a pin, threw it into the water and wished for a speedy marriage or another love-related outcome. The same collection gives the fuller local description of the Fairy, Wishing, Maiden or Pin Well near Horsdean: on May Day, a procession went from the town to the well, where each person dropped in a crooked pin while making a wish, trusting that the fairy or presiding “genius” of the well would fulfil it before the year was out.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/stream/examplesprinted00balfgoog/examplesprinted00balfgoog_djvu.txt" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on archive.org.</span></span></span>

That detail is important because it shows a custom rather than a single isolated tale. The bent pins at Wooler belong to a wider British pattern of offerings at wells, but the Northumberland version has its own local texture: May Day, youthful courtship, a spring under the lower Cheviot landscape, and a named fairy presence. Modern descriptions still identify the Pin Well, also called the Maiden Well or Cattle Well, as a natural spring and wishing well, with the May Day pin custom remembered as part of Wooler’s local heritage.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://fabulousnorth.com/pin-well-wooler/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: fabulousnorth.com">[Fabulous North]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">fabulousnorth.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on fabulousnorth.com.</span></span></span>

The county’s other well lore reinforces the same point: water was not merely useful but socially charged. Balfour records Northumberland well traditions involving pins, healing, first-foot-like New Year rites and magical protection from running water. At Wark, the first person to take the “Flower of the Well” on New Year’s morning was believed to gain extraordinary powers for the coming year; at Longwitton, holy wells were remembered for their “virtuous” qualities and seasonal gatherings.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/stream/examplesprinted00balfgoog/examplesprinted00balfgoog_djvu.txt" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on archive.org.</span></span></span> These are not all fairy stories in the narrow sense, but they show the belief environment in which a “Fairy Well” made sense.

Hills work differently. They are less about petition and more about hidden society. Northumberland fairy-hill lore imagines fairies not simply as wandering beings but as organised communities with music, dancing, feasting and sometimes royalty. That makes the hill a kind of sealed household or court: visible from outside, but not safely entered by humans.

## Old Fawdon Hill and literary fairy geography

Fawdon Hill is the county’s most striking example of a fairy hill shaped by both tradition and literary retelling. James Service’s *Metrical Legends of Northumberland*, published in 1834, includes “The Fairies of Fawdon Hill” in a volume of Northumberland legendary poetry. The book’s contents explicitly place the Fawdon piece among local legends such as Dunstanburgh, Cuddy Bell and the [Laidly Worm]({{ 'laidly-worm/' | relative_url }}), showing that by the early nineteenth century Fawdon had entered a printed Northumbrian legendary canon.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://books.google.com/books/about/Metrical_Legends_of_Northumberland.html?hl=fr&amp;id=8hm9yAEACAAJ" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: books.google.com">[Google Books]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">books.google.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on google.com.</span></span></span>

The familiar wording that Fawdon Hills are “where the fairies dwell” comes from this literary setting, and later retellings identify Old Fawdon Hill as the residence of Queen Mab and her fairy court. Modern folklore writers have been careful to note the mixed evidence: Service gives a poetic treatment; later collections and retellings expand the idea of a fairy court, midnight music, a banquet inside the hill and a human witness who wisely refuses fairy drink.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://faeryfolklorist.blogspot.com/2009/06/fawdon-hill-fairies.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: faeryfolklorist.blogspot.com">[Faery Folklorist]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">faeryfolklorist.blogspot.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">fawdon hill fairies</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">fawdon hill fairies</span></span></span>

That distinction matters for readers. Fawdon Hill should not be treated as a purely untouched oral tale handed down in a fixed form. It is better understood as a layered place: a local fairy-hill tradition preserved and amplified through nineteenth-century literary antiquarianism, then recycled in twentieth- and twenty-first-century folklore writing. The hill is “traditional” in the sense that it belongs to Northumberland’s older legendary geography, but the polished fairy-court version has clearly passed through the hands of poets, collectors and popular retellers.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/details/metricallegendso00servrich" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on archive.org.</span></span></span>

The geography is also slightly slippery. Modern discussion has sometimes had to distinguish Old Fawdon Hill near Whittingham from other Fawdon-name locations, using clues in the poem such as nearby Dunmoor, Linhope, Hedgley and the Breamish to anchor the likely setting in the north Northumberland landscape.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://faeryfolklorist.blogspot.com/2009/06/fawdon-hill-fairies.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: faeryfolklorist.blogspot.com">[Faery Folklorist]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">faeryfolklorist.blogspot.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">fawdon hill fairies</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">fawdon hill fairies</span></span></span> This is a useful reminder that folklore mapping is not always a matter of dropping a pin on a modern tourist map. Place-names move, memories blur, and literary references can preserve older local associations while also creating new confusion.

What makes Fawdon valuable is not just the fairy queen detail, but the way the story turns a Northumberland hill into a royal fairy centre. It gives the county a version of the widespread “hollow hill” motif: the idea that a mound, hill or old earthwork may contain a lit, musical, perilous world inside. In Northumberland, that motif feels at home because so much of the county’s legend is already attached to raised ground, ancient-looking features and border landscapes where history and strangeness overlap.

## Mine goblins and working-landscape spirits

Northumberland’s mine spirits are among the strongest evidence that local fairy belief was not only rural or pastoral. In the coalfield, supernatural beings entered the industrial workplace. Bluecap and Cutty Soams are usually classed with mine goblins or bogles rather than flower-fairies, but they belong naturally on a page about Northumberland fairy places because they show the same hidden-people logic adapted to underground labour.

A key nineteenth-century source is the *Colliery Guardian* report of 23 May 1863, later reprinted in folklore collections. It says that two goblins were believed to haunt northern mines. Cutty Soams was the spiteful one: his trick was cutting the rope-traces, or “soams”, by which an assistant putter was yoked to a coal tub. Bluecap, by contrast, was a ghostly putter who appeared as a light-blue flame, settled on a full coal tub and moved it as if by powerful unseen effort.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://sacred-texts.com/neu/eng/prwe/prwe196.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sacred-texts.com">[Internet Sacred Text Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sacred-texts.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on sacred-texts.com.</span></span></span>

Bluecap’s most revealing trait is that he expected wages. The same account says miners left his pay in a solitary corner of the mine once a fortnight, rated like an ordinary putter’s wage. If the amount was too little, he would not take it; if it was too much, he left the surplus.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://sacred-texts.com/neu/eng/prwe/prwe196.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sacred-texts.com">[Internet Sacred Text Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sacred-texts.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on sacred-texts.com.</span></span></span> This is a wonderfully practical supernatural rule. Bluecap is not a vague ghost but a worker inside a wage system. He helps, he is paid, and he insists on the correct rate.

That makes the Bluecap story more than a curiosity. It reflects the moral world of the pit: labour should be recognised, underground work is dangerous, and unseen forces may help or hinder the day’s winning. The spirit behaves almost like a supernatural co-worker. Cutty Soams, meanwhile, explains damage to gear, delay, frustration and the vulnerability of those lower down the pit hierarchy. The folklore turns ordinary workplace hazards into named agents.

Shilbottle is often singled out in later accounts as Bluecap’s Northumberland home, with the being also known as Blue Bonnet. Modern heritage material confirms Shilbottle’s long mining identity: coal was being worked in the area by the eighteenth century, a waggonway opened in 1809, and Shilbottle Colliery later became a major twentieth-century pit community.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://bailiffgatecollections.co.uk/about-us/our-area/coal-mining/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: bailiffgatecollections.co.uk">[Bailiffgate Collections]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">bailiffgatecollections.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on bailiffgatecollections.co.uk.</span></span></span> The folklore therefore attaches to a real mining landscape, not an invented gothic setting.

The Northumberland Archives learning material frames mining as a way of life for many North East communities into the mid-late twentieth century.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://northumberlandarchives.com/learn/mining/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: northumberlandarchives.com">[Northumberland Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">northumberlandarchives.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on northumberlandarchives.com.</span></span></span> That context helps explain why mine goblins survived in print and memory: they belonged to a culture where work, danger, skill, solidarity and superstition were closely bound. A flicker of blue flame underground could be read as a warning, a helper, a trick of light, or a story to explain what happened in a place most people above ground would never see.

## What these places tell us about Northumberland fairies

The most important lesson is that Northumberland fairies are localised. They are not just “in nature”; they are in specific working and ritual landscapes.

At Wooler, the fairy presence is tied to a spring, May Day, youth and wishing. At Fawdon, the fairies occupy a hill imagined as a courtly hidden world. In the mines, Bluecap and Cutty Soams belong to labour, wages, equipment and danger. Across these examples, fairies and fairy-like beings answer different human needs:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **They make risky places memorable.** Hills at night, lonely upland paths and underground workings become easier to talk about when given supernatural inhabitants.
* **They regulate behaviour.** Do not intrude too boldly into the fairy hill; make the proper offering at the well; respect the unseen helper in the mine.
* **They explain uncertainty.** A wish may or may not come true; a rope may be mysteriously cut; a coal tub may move; a light may flicker in the dark.
* **They preserve local identity.** Fawdon, Wooler and Shilbottle are not interchangeable fairy backdrops. Their stories belong to recognisable Northumberland places.

</div>

This variety also prevents a common misunderstanding. Northumberland fairy lore is not simply “Celtic” romance imported into England, nor is it only Victorian whimsy. Some of it is certainly shaped by nineteenth-century literary taste, especially the Queen Mab colouring at Fawdon. But other parts are rooted in recorded custom, occupational belief and local topography. The stronger reading is comparative: Northumberland absorbed widespread British motifs — wishing wells, hollow hills, mine goblins — and made them speak in local accents.

## How old and reliable are the traditions?

The evidence is uneven, and that is part of the story. Wooler’s Pin Well custom is relatively strong because it appears in Balfour’s 1904 county folklore collection with details drawn from earlier local journalism and James Hall’s *Guide to Glendale*. The description includes concrete behaviour: midnight visits, bent pins, May Day procession, wishing and continued youthful resort to the well.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/stream/examplesprinted00balfgoog/examplesprinted00balfgoog_djvu.txt" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on archive.org.</span></span></span>

Bluecap and Cutty Soams are also well anchored, thanks to the 1863 *Colliery Guardian* account reprinted in later folklore literature. The source itself already says some beliefs were fading into tradition by that date, while omens retained a stronger hold among some older pitmen.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://sacred-texts.com/neu/eng/prwe/prwe196.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sacred-texts.com">[Internet Sacred Text Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sacred-texts.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on sacred-texts.com.</span></span></span> That does not make the stories worthless; it tells us they were being recorded at a moment when industrial modernity and older occupational superstition overlapped.

Fawdon Hill is more literary. James Service’s 1834 volume proves that the fairy association had entered print by the early nineteenth century, and later writers preserve or elaborate the fairy-court tradition.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://books.google.com/books/about/Metrical_Legends_of_Northumberland.html?hl=fr&amp;id=8hm9yAEACAAJ" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: books.google.com">[Google Books]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">books.google.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on google.com.</span></span></span> But because the most vivid banquet-and-Queen-Mab versions come through literary and later popular retellings, they should be handled as legendary geography rather than as a simple transcript of oral testimony.

Modern blogs, folklore websites and local-history pages are useful for locating how these stories are retold today, especially when they point back to older books and named collections. They are less authoritative than primary or near-primary sources, but they show the living afterlife of the traditions: walkers visiting Fawdon, local heritage pages describing the Pin Well, and popular folklore writers grouping Northumberland’s fairy sites into a county-wide map of strange places.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://betweenworldsdurham.wordpress.com/2018/03/08/the-fairy-folk-of-northumberland/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: betweenworldsdurham.wordpress.com">[wordpress.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">betweenworldsdurham.wordpress.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Between Worlds The Fairy Folk of Northumberland &#124; Between Worlds</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Between Worlds The Fairy Folk of Northumberland &#124; Between Worlds</span></span></span>

## Why the traditions still matter

Northumberland’s fairy hills, wells and mine spirits matter because they show folklore doing local work. The stories do not float above the county; they cling to its geography. A reader can move from the Cheviot-edge wish at Wooler to the fairy court at Fawdon and then down into the coalfield world of Bluecap and Cutty Soams, and see three different Northumberlands: ritual, romantic and industrial.

They also complicate the popular image of fairies. In Northumberland, fairies may grant wishes, dance at night, hold court inside a hill, cook, work, demand wages or sabotage mining gear. They may be beautiful, helpful, mischievous or dangerous. The category is flexible because the county’s landscapes are flexible: upland, village, spring, mill, mine and border road all invite different kinds of hidden inhabitant.

For a modern visitor or reader, the value is not in treating these beings as proven supernatural facts. It is in recognising how communities used story to make sense of place. Wooler’s pins speak to hope and courtship; Fawdon’s court speaks to enchantment and forbidden vision; Bluecap speaks to labour underground and the dignity of correct pay. Together they make Northumberland’s fairy places some of the richest and most varied in northern English folklore.
