## Introduction

[Cornwall]({{ 'where-cornwalls-landscape-turns-into/' | relative_url }})’s mythic landscape is built from places that already feel dramatic before any story is added: sea-bitten cliffs, tidal islands, ruined castles, granite hills and lonely moorland stones. Giants explain why the land looks rough, broken or oversized; Tintagel explains how one Cornish headland became one of Britain’s most famous Arthurian places. The result is not a single ancient “Cornish mythology” with fixed rules, but a layered landscape tradition: oral tales, medieval literature, antiquarian collecting, archaeology, tourism and modern performance all shaping the same places in different ways.



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/Cornwall_4c96d0_giants_tintagel_land_e4da75-overview.webp" | relative_url }}" alt="Overview image for Mythic Places" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

The key point is that Cornish legends are unusually place-bound. St Michael’s Mount is remembered through the giant Cormoran; St Agnes through the giant Bolster; Tintagel through Geoffrey of Monmouth’s twelfth-century Arthurian story, Richard of Cornwall’s thirteenth-century castle and later visitor culture. These are not simply stories about monsters and kings. They are ways of making Cornwall’s wild geography meaningful, giving cliffs, coves, causeways and ruins a role in local memory.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cornwallheritagetrust.org/learn/resources/stories-and-rhymes/giants-of-the-mount/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cornwallheritagetrust.org">[cornwallheritagetrust.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cornwallheritagetrust.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Cornwall Heritage Trust Giants of the Mount</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cornwall Heritage Trust Giants of the Mount</span></span></span>

For this page, Cornwall means the historic county centred on the peninsula west of the Tamar, with the Isles of Scilly forming part of historic Cornwall though long treated separately in local government. That matters because these legends are rooted in older county identity as much as in modern tourist routes or council boundaries.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://wikishire.co.uk/wiki/Cornwall" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wikishire.co.uk">[Wikishire]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wikishire.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wikishire.co.uk.</span></span></span>

## Why Cornwall’s wild places invite giant stories

Cornish giant legends often work like folk explanations for landscape. A strange rock, a tidal island, a heap of boulders or a high beacon can be treated as the trace of enormous hands. The giant is not just a big character; he is a way of reading the land. Stones have been carried in aprons, hurled across bays, dropped in anger, or left as proof that an older, rougher world once stood behind the present one.

This is why Cornwall’s giants cluster so naturally around exposed, memorable places. St Michael’s Mount rises from Mount’s Bay like a ready-made fortress. Chapel Porth, St Agnes Beacon and the cliffs around St Agnes give the Bolster story a stage where a huge body can be imagined striding across the land. West Cornwall’s granite and sea-edge scenery gives the tales scale: the landscape already looks as if it belongs to beings larger than ordinary people.

The stories also make social sense. Cornish giants are often bullies, raiders or dangerous neighbours rather than noble ancestors. Cormoran steals livestock from the mainland; Bolster terrorises the St Agnes district; other giant tales in nineteenth-century collections repeatedly pit human cunning, saintly virtue or local courage against brute force. That pattern turns frightening landscape into community drama. The cliff is not only high; it is where the village outwits the giant. The tidal island is not only remote; it is where a boy’s trick defeats a monster.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cornwallheritagetrust.org/learn/resources/stories-and-rhymes/giants-of-the-mount/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cornwallheritagetrust.org">[cornwallheritagetrust.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cornwallheritagetrust.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Cornwall Heritage Trust Giants of the Mount</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cornwall Heritage Trust Giants of the Mount</span></span></span>

Robert Hunt’s nineteenth-century *Popular Romances of the West of England* is one of the most important printed gateways into this material. Its index alone shows how strongly giants run through Cornish legendary geography, with tales such as “The Giant Bolster”, “The Giant of Morva”, “The Hack and Cast”, “The Giant Wrath, or Ralph” and “Ordulph the Giant”. Hunt was not inventing a tidy mythological system; he was gathering drolls, traditions and local supernatural stories at a time when oral storytelling was being increasingly fixed in print.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/download/popularromanceso00huntuoft/popularromanceso00huntuoft.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on archive.org.</span></span></span>

## Cormoran, St Michael’s Mount and Jack the Giant Killer

St Michael’s Mount is one of Cornwall’s clearest examples of a place where natural drama, folklore and popular literature overlap. In local legend, the Mount was once the home of the giant Cormoran, who used it as a base for raids on nearby communities. Cornwall Heritage Trust tells a version in which the Mount was built by Cormoran and his wife Cormelian, with the giantess doing much of the hard carrying while her lazy husband took advantage of her labour.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cornwallheritagetrust.org/learn/resources/stories-and-rhymes/giants-of-the-mount/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cornwallheritagetrust.org">[Cornwall Heritage Trust]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cornwallheritagetrust.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Cornwall Heritage Trust Giants of the Mount</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cornwall Heritage Trust Giants of the Mount</span></span></span>

The same legend easily becomes a hero tale. St Michael’s Mount’s own educational retelling describes Cormoran as a giant who waded ashore to steal cows, sheep and sometimes children, until a local boy called Jack set a trap for him. In common modern versions, Jack digs a pit, wakes the giant with a horn, and lures him into the hidden hole. This is the Cornish root of the wider “Jack the Giant Killer” tradition, though printed chapbooks, children’s retellings and later fairy-tale collections spread and reshaped the story far beyond Cornwall.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://stmichaelsmount.co.uk/wp-content/uploads/2025/04/Cormoran-StAE-version.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: stmichaelsmount.co.uk">[St Michael&#x27;s Mount]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">stmichaelsmount.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">St Michael&#x27;s Mount Cormoran the Giant</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">St Michael&#x27;s Mount Cormoran the Giant</span></span></span>

The power of the tale lies in its neat reversal. Cormoran has size, strength and a fortified island; Jack has local knowledge, patience and timing. The causeway and tide matter because they make the Mount feel both near and unreachable. To anyone looking across Mount’s Bay, the story gives the island a moral shape: what looks like a giant’s stronghold can be beaten by a boy who understands the ground better than the monster does.

There is also a telling difference between local landscape legend and later literary packaging. “Jack the Giant Killer” became a broader English fairy tale, often set in Arthur’s reign and filled with further giant-slaying adventures. The Cornish material is more specific and more physical. It belongs to the Mount, the causeway, the mainland villages and the fear of losing animals or children to a powerful neighbour. The later fairy tale turns Jack into a national giant-killing hero; the Cornish setting keeps him close to a recognisable shore.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jack_the_Giant_Killer" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Jack the Giant Killer</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Jack the Giant Killer</span></span></span>

## Bolster at St Agnes: a giant kept alive by performance

The giant Bolster belongs to the north Cornish coast around St Agnes, Chapel Porth and St Agnes Beacon. The National Trust’s account presents him as a bad-tempered local giant who bullied his wife, made her carry stones, and then became obsessed with St Agnes herself. Agnes defeats him by asking him to prove his love by filling a hole in the cliff with his blood; because the hole opens to the sea, he bleeds away and dies.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nationaltrust.org.uk/visit/cornwall/chapel-porth/the-st-agnes-bolster-festival-and-legend-of-the-giant-bolster" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nationaltrust.org.uk">[National Trust]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nationaltrust.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nationaltrust.org.uk.</span></span></span>

This is a landscape story with a particularly strong visual logic. Bolster’s size is measured against local landmarks, and the cliff-hole trick makes sense because Chapel Porth gives the tale a real sea-edge setting. The story turns a coastal feature into a narrative device. The dangerous hole is not just scenery; it is the saint’s solution.

Bolster also shows how a legend can move from oral tale to community custom. Every May Day weekend, villagers at St Agnes re-enact the story with a procession through the village and a pageant on the cliffs above Chapel Porth. The National Trust describes the annual event as a re-enactment by villagers through the village and over the clifftops, while the festival’s own material places the pageant at Chapel Porth and invites the public to watch the defeat of the giant.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nationaltrust.org.uk/visit/cornwall/chapel-porth/the-st-agnes-bolster-festival-and-legend-of-the-giant-bolster" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nationaltrust.org.uk">[National Trust]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nationaltrust.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nationaltrust.org.uk.</span></span></span>

That modern performance matters. It means Bolster is not only a preserved tale in a book or a tourist anecdote on a signboard. He is part of local place-memory, renewed by costume, music, procession and cliffside theatre. The pageant does what many living customs do: it makes the old story visible again without pretending that everyone believes in it literally. The giant survives because the community keeps giving him a body.

## Tintagel between history and Arthurian legend

Tintagel’s fame rests on a productive confusion: it is both a real historic site and a legendary Arthurian place, but those two truths do not mean the same thing. Archaeology shows Tintagel was an important post-Roman settlement, with evidence of Mediterranean trade and high-status occupation. English Heritage notes that fifth- and sixth-century Tintagel was part of a long-distance trading network, and research at the site has produced large quantities of imported pottery.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.english-heritage.org.uk/visit/places/tintagel-castle/history-and-legend/history/research/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: english-heritage.org.uk">[English Heritage]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">english-heritage.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on english-heritage.org.uk.</span></span></span>

That archaeological importance does not prove Arthur lived there. The famous Arthurian link comes from Geoffrey of Monmouth’s *History of the Kings of Britain*, written around 1136. In Geoffrey’s story, Tintagel is the place where Uther Pendragon, helped by Merlin’s magic, takes the appearance of Gorlois of Cornwall and conceives Arthur with Ygerna. English Heritage is careful to frame this as literary fame: in the twelfth century, Geoffrey named Tintagel as the site of Arthur’s conception, and that association transformed the place’s later reputation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.english-heritage.org.uk/visit/places/tintagel-castle/history-and-legend/history/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: english-heritage.org.uk">[English Heritage]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">english-heritage.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on english-heritage.org.uk.</span></span></span>

This distinction is essential for readers. Tintagel is not “proved Arthur’s castle”. The visible medieval ruins are largely the result of Richard, Earl of Cornwall, who built a castle there in the thirteenth century, long after the supposed Arthurian period. English Heritage explains that Richard’s castle was shaped by the Arthurian prestige already attached to the headland. In other words, legend helped create architecture: a medieval lord built at Tintagel partly because the story had already made the place powerful.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.english-heritage.org.uk/visit/places/tintagel-castle/history-and-legend/richard-of-cornwall/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: english-heritage.org.uk">[English Heritage]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">english-heritage.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on english-heritage.org.uk.</span></span></span>

Tintagel therefore works in layers:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **Post-Roman power:** a high-status settlement linked to Mediterranean trade.
* **Medieval storytelling:** Geoffrey of Monmouth’s Arthurian conception story.
* **Thirteenth-century politics:** Richard of Cornwall using the legendary site to support prestige.
* **Modern heritage:** visitors encountering ruins, interpretation, Merlin’s Cave, the bridge and Arthurian art.

</div>

The result is more interesting than a simple “true or false” debate. Tintagel matters because a real place with real early medieval significance became a literary and political landmark, then a heritage site where archaeology and myth are still being negotiated.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.english-heritage.org.uk/visit/places/tintagel-castle/history-and-legend/history/research/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: english-heritage.org.uk">[English Heritage]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">english-heritage.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on english-heritage.org.uk.</span></span></span>

## Why Tintagel became the perfect Arthurian place

Geoffrey of Monmouth did not choose a bland setting. Tintagel is a steep, difficult, wave-cut place, split between mainland and headland. In the story of Uther, Ygerna and Gorlois, that geography matters because Tintagel functions as an almost unreachable refuge. English Heritage’s summary of the legend emphasises that Gorlois sends Ygerna to the coastal stronghold because no ordinary attack can succeed there.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.english-heritage.org.uk/visit/places/tintagel-castle/history-and-legend/richard-of-cornwall/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: english-heritage.org.uk">[English Heritage]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">english-heritage.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on english-heritage.org.uk.</span></span></span>

That is why Tintagel feels so convincing as legend even when the historical claims are uncertain. The place performs the story. The cliffs make separation visible. The sea makes danger audible. The ruins make absence feel meaningful. A visitor does not need to believe that Arthur was conceived there to understand why generations of writers, antiquaries, artists and tourists have treated the headland as a mythic threshold.

The site also suits Cornwall’s wider folklore because it turns edge-places into story-places. In Cornish tradition, meaning often gathers where land meets something difficult: sea, mine, moor, cave, well, ruin or parish boundary. Tintagel is all edge. It is coastal, ruined, elevated, historically charged and physically awkward. That makes it ideal for legend because the landscape itself already suggests separation between ordinary life and something larger.

A useful correction is that Arthur is not the main actor at Tintagel in Geoffrey’s original episode. The story is about his conception, not his reign. Later tourism and retelling often make Tintagel feel like Arthur’s own castle, but the medieval literary link is narrower. That does not make the place less important; it makes it a good example of how folklore and heritage grow by expansion. A single episode becomes a whole atmosphere.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://bernarddeacon.com/legend-and-heritage-at-tintagel/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: bernarddeacon.com">[Cornish studies resources]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">bernarddeacon.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on bernarddeacon.com.</span></span></span>

## Stones, cliffs and castles as legendary memory

Cornwall’s mythic landscape is not random. Certain kinds of place repeatedly attract legend because they invite explanation, fear or wonder. Giants suit large stones and steep hills. Saints suit wells, chapels and parish identity. Arthurian legend suits dramatic ruins and royal-looking headlands. Mining spirits suit underground sound and danger. The landscape is not just a backdrop; it guides the kind of story that can plausibly attach to it.

Giant legends especially turn geology into biography. A stone is not merely a stone: it may have been dropped from an apron, thrown in anger, or carried for a giant’s building project. A hill is not merely high: it may have been a stride-marker for a body big enough to cross parishes. A tidal island is not merely an island: it may be the home of a being who can wade where ordinary people must wait for the causeway.

Tintagel does something similar with architecture and absence. The thirteenth-century castle ruins do not date from Arthur’s supposed age, but they make the Arthurian association easier to imagine. The post-Roman archaeology gives the site genuine early medieval weight; the ruins give it visual romance; the literary tradition gives it narrative. Each layer strengthens the others in public memory even when they need to be kept separate in historical explanation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.english-heritage.org.uk/visit/places/tintagel-castle/history-and-legend/history/research/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: english-heritage.org.uk">[english-heritage.org.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">english-heritage.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on english-heritage.org.uk.</span></span></span>

This is one reason Cornwall’s legendary places are so durable. A purely free-floating tale can be forgotten; a tale attached to a visible place can be revisited. The cliff, cave, island or ruin becomes a memory device. People can point to it, walk it, photograph it, perform beside it and argue over its meaning.

## Tourism, festivals and the argument over myth

Modern Cornwall does not simply inherit mythic places; it actively manages, markets and sometimes disputes them. Tintagel is the clearest case. English Heritage now presents the site as both historic and legendary, with the modern footbridge reconnecting the mainland and island sections, the Gallos sculpture on the headland and Merlin’s Cave below the cliffs.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.english-heritage.org.uk/visit/places/tintagel-castle/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: english-heritage.org.uk">[English Heritage]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">english-heritage.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on english-heritage.org.uk.</span></span></span>

That approach has not been universally welcomed. In 2016, Cornish historians criticised English Heritage’s Arthurian additions, arguing that features such as the Merlin carving risked turning Tintagel into a “fairytale theme park” and distracting from the site’s real early medieval and Cornish significance. English Heritage defended the interpretation as a way of telling Tintagel’s rich and varied story, including the Arthurian legends that have shaped its reputation for centuries.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.theguardian.com/uk-news/2016/mar/22/english-heritage-turning-tintagel-into-fairytale-theme-park" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theguardian.com">[The Guardian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theguardian.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Guardian English Heritage turning Tintagel into &#x27;fairytale theme park</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Guardian English Heritage turning Tintagel into &#x27;fairytale theme park</span></span></span>

The dispute is important because it reveals a genuine heritage problem. Myth brings visitors and emotional power, but it can flatten history if handled carelessly. Too much Arthur can make Tintagel feel like a fantasy attraction rather than a complex Cornish site with post-Roman trade, medieval lordship, erosion, archaeology and local identity. Too little Arthur would also be misleading, because the Arthurian association has been part of Tintagel’s history since the twelfth century.

St Agnes shows a gentler model of modern retelling. The Bolster Festival does not need to prove Bolster existed. It uses pageant, procession and cliffside performance to keep a local legend socially alive. Visitors can enjoy the spectacle, but the story still belongs strongly to St Agnes and Chapel Porth rather than becoming a generic fantasy brand.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nationaltrust.org.uk/visit/cornwall/chapel-porth/the-st-agnes-bolster-festival-and-legend-of-the-giant-bolster" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nationaltrust.org.uk">[National Trust]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nationaltrust.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nationaltrust.org.uk.</span></span></span>

## How to read Cornwall’s mythic places today

The best way to understand Cornwall’s giants and Arthurian sites is to ask what kind of tradition is being encountered. Some elements are medieval literature, some are oral legend collected in the nineteenth century, some are community custom, and some are modern heritage interpretation. They overlap, but they are not interchangeable.

At St Michael’s Mount, the Cormoran story is a local giant legend that later fed into the wider Jack the Giant Killer tradition. At St Agnes, Bolster is both a collected legend and a living village performance. At Tintagel, the Arthurian link begins with Geoffrey of Monmouth’s medieval literary story, is reinforced by Richard of Cornwall’s castle-building, and is now interpreted through archaeology, visitor experience and public debate.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cornwallheritagetrust.org/learn/resources/stories-and-rhymes/giants-of-the-mount/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cornwallheritagetrust.org">[cornwallheritagetrust.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cornwallheritagetrust.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Cornwall Heritage Trust Giants of the Mount</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cornwall Heritage Trust Giants of the Mount</span></span></span>

A grounded reading does not spoil the magic. It makes the places richer. Knowing that Tintagel’s archaeology is real but not proof of Arthur helps separate history from legend without dismissing either. Knowing that Bolster is performed every year shows how folklore can be renewed rather than merely preserved. Knowing that Cormoran belongs to a tidal island explains why the story feels so physically right.

Cornwall’s mythic landscape endures because it lets people hold several truths at once. The cliffs are geological facts, dangerous places and story stages. The ruins are medieval remains and Arthurian symbols. The giants are not historical beings, but they are powerful expressions of how communities made sense of scale, danger and place. In Cornwall, wild places become legendary when story gives the landscape a memory that people can still walk into.

    var select = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-select]');
    if (select) {
      select.addEventListener('change', function () {
        applyMarket(section, select.value, true);
      });
    }
    var trigger = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-trigger]');
    var menu = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-menu]');
    if (trigger && menu) {
      trigger.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
        event.stopPropagation();
        var expanded = trigger.getAttribute('aria-expanded') === 'true';
        menu.hidden = expanded;
        trigger.setAttribute('aria-expanded', expanded ? 'false' : 'true');
        var picker = trigger.closest('.fr-ebay-market-picker');
        if (picker) picker.classList.toggle('fr-ebay-market-picker--open', !expanded);
      });
      Array.prototype.slice.call(menu.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-option]')).forEach(function (button) {
        button.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
          event.stopPropagation();
          applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-option'), true);
        });
      });
      document.addEventListener('click', function () { closeMenu(section); });
    }
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      button.addEventListener('click', function () {
        applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button'), true);
      });
    });
    applyMarket(section, marketId, false);
  });
