## Introduction

The Trefeglwys changeling tale is one of [Montgomeryshire]({{ 'where-montgomeryshires-old-stories-still/' | relative_url }})’s sharpest fairy stories because it turns a familiar folk motif into a local drama of childcare, suspicion and fear. The setting is a lonely shepherd’s cottage near Trefeglwys, close to Llanidloes, where a mother briefly leaves her twin babies and later fears that the Fair Folk have replaced them. The tale was printed in the early nineteenth century as “The Egg-Shell Pottage”, then reused by later folklore [collectors]({{ 'collectors/' | relative_url }}), including Wirt Sikes and Elias Owen, which makes it better attested than many short local legends. Its power lies not in proving that fairies were believed in literally by everyone, but in showing how rural communities could explain infant sickness, slow development and family anxiety through the dangerous idea of the changeling.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/stream/cu31924029911520/cu31924029911520_djvu.txt" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on archive.org.</span></span></span>



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/Montgomeryshire_1814c4_trefeglwys_changelin_b975d5-overview.webp" | relative_url }}" alt="Overview image for Changelings" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

Trefeglwys matters because the story is not merely “a Welsh changeling tale” with a vague setting. It names a parish, a cottage remembered as the “Place of Strife”, nearby Llyn Ebyr, the road world around Llanidloes, and the fear that even a brief absence from the cradle could invite supernatural interference. In county folklore terms, it is a small tale with a large shadow: a Montgomeryshire story about fairies, but also about isolation, parenthood, illness and the risky power of local wise people.

## The Place of Strife near Llanidloes

The tale places the action in the parish of Trefeglwys, near Llanidloes, in the historic county of Montgomeryshire. Older gazetteer material describes Trefeglwys as a parish in the upper division of the hundred of Llanidloes, while the modern community council describes Trefeglwys as a rural community about eight miles long by three miles wide, including Llawryglyn and Staylittle, with parts of the Clywedog Reservoir within its bounds and Llanidloes, Caersws and Newtown within easy reach.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.genuki.org.uk/big/wal/MGY/Trefeglwys" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: genuki.org.uk">[GenUKI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">genuki.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on genuki.org.uk.</span></span></span>

That geography matters. This is not a fairy palace, a remote mythic island or a courtly romance. It is a cottage landscape: upland, scattered, parish-based, and close enough to neighbours for ordinary errands, yet isolated enough for a mother to worry about leaving babies alone “even for a minute”. The printed tale says there were already “many tales” of goblins or the Fair Folk haunting the neighbourhood, so the mother’s fear belongs to a remembered local atmosphere rather than a one-off supernatural shock.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/stream/cu31924029911520/cu31924029911520_djvu.txt" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on archive.org.</span></span></span>

The cottage itself is called Twt y Cwmrws, translated in the source as the “place of strife”. In the story, the name is explained by the quarrel that grows between husband and wife after the twins seem not to thrive. The father insists that the children are not really theirs; the mother resists the idea, but becomes increasingly distressed. This is the emotional centre of the tale. The fairy fear does not simply frighten the family from outside. It enters the household, turns illness or difference into accusation, and makes the cradle a site of conflict.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/stream/cu31924029911520/cu31924029911520_djvu.txt" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on archive.org.</span></span></span>

The place-name may not be easily recoverable on the ground for a casual visitor today, and the tale should not be treated as a map to a proven event. Its folkloric value is different: it shows how a community could attach a moral and emotional memory to a named cottage. In Montgomeryshire’s wider folklore, that kind of local anchoring is important. Stories survive because they are fastened to fields, lakes, farms, roads, chapels and remembered houses.

## What happens in the changeling tale

The plot is simple, but every stage adds pressure. A married couple living at the shepherd’s cottage have twin babies. The mother cares for them tenderly, but one day she is called away to a neighbour. She dislikes leaving the children alone because the house is solitary and the neighbourhood has fairy stories attached to it. On her return, she sees fairy-like beings, described in one version as “old elves of the blue petticoat”, even though it is midday. She rushes home and finds the babies apparently still in their cradle.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/stream/cu31924029911520/cu31924029911520_djvu.txt" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on archive.org.</span></span></span>

Only later does the dread deepen. The twins do not grow as expected. They remain small, and neighbours begin to wonder. The father argues that they cannot be his children; the mother insists they must be. The strife gives the place its remembered name. Eventually, in despair, the mother consults a local wise man, a figure who appears in many British and Welsh folk traditions as a person able to diagnose hidden causes and prescribe tests or charms.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/stream/cu31924029911520/cu31924029911520_djvu.txt" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on archive.org.</span></span></span>

His advice is the famous test. When preparing food for the harvest reapers, she is to empty a hen’s egg, boil pottage in the shell, and carry it out as though this tiny meal is meant for the workers. If the babies speak with knowledge beyond the understanding of children, they are to be treated as changelings. When the mother performs the test, one child remarks, in effect, that he has known acorns before oak trees and eggs before hens, but has never seen an eggshell meal prepared for harvest men. The impossible age of the speaker exposes the fairy substitution.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/stream/cu31924029911520/cu31924029911520_djvu.txt" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on archive.org.</span></span></span>

The ending is bleak to modern ears. The mother takes the two children and throws them into Llyn Ebyr. The fairies, or goblins, save their own “dwarfs”, and the mother receives her true children back again. Later retellings for children tend to soften the delivery but retain the key shape: the egg-shell test, the lake, the rescue by the Fair Folk, and the return of the human babies. Mythstories’ modern Montgomeryshire Folktales project, for example, retells the tale as “The Changelings”, placing it at Twt y Cwmrws and Llyn Ebyr and presenting it in both English and Welsh as part of a local storytelling resource.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://mythstories.com/the-changelings-y-plant-cyfnewid/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: mythstories.com">[Mythstories]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">mythstories.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Changelings / Y Plant Cyfnewid – Mythstories</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Changelings / Y Plant Cyfnewid – Mythstories</span></span></span>

## How changeling tests worked in Welsh tradition

The Trefeglwys story belongs to a wider changeling pattern: a child is believed to have been stolen by fairies and replaced by a strange substitute. Wirt Sikes, in *British Goblins*, describes the Welsh changeling as a child that may at first look like the stolen infant but soon becomes ugly, shrivelled, bad-tempered, wailing, uncanny or alarmingly old in knowledge. Sikes is not a neutral modern clinician; he is a nineteenth-century collector with his own assumptions. Yet his chapter is useful because it places the Trefeglwys tale inside a group of Welsh stories about infant exchange, detection and forced restoration.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://sacred-texts.com/neu/celt/wfl/wfl05.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sacred-texts.com">[Internet Sacred Text Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sacred-texts.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on sacred-texts.com.</span></span></span>

The egg-shell test is one of the most recognisable methods. Its logic is theatrical rather than scientific. The mother does something absurd: she pretends that a tiny eggshell can feed a group of working harvesters. The changeling, unable to resist commenting on the impossibility, reveals itself by speaking like an ancient being trapped in an infant’s body. In the Trefeglwys version, the rhyme about acorn, oak, egg and hen gives the moment a memorable poetic snap.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/stream/cu31924029911520/cu31924029911520_djvu.txt" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on archive.org.</span></span></span>

Sikes notes that similar stories were told in other parts of Wales and compares the egg-shell device with Breton material, while later compilers such as Joseph Jacobs reshaped the same material for a broader fairy-tale readership. Jacobs’ version, often known as “Brewery of Eggshells”, keeps the Trefeglwys cottage, the wise man, the harvesters and the lake, but makes the story read more like a polished fairy tale than a local memorandum of belief.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://sacred-texts.com/neu/celt/wfl/wfl05.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sacred-texts.com">[sacred-texts.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sacred-texts.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on sacred-texts.com.</span></span></span>

This difference matters for readers. The older antiquarian versions preserve the rougher fear: a mother’s panic, a quarrel with her husband, and dangerous advice. The literary retellings preserve the story pattern: clever test, fairy confession, restored children. Both are part of the tale’s history, but they do different work. One shows how belief might have operated under stress; the other shows how a disturbing local tradition became safe enough for the fairy-tale shelf.

## Infant sickness, fear and upland isolation

The most uncomfortable part of the Trefeglwys changeling story is also the most important. Changeling belief often attached itself to babies and children who were ill, disabled, slow to develop, distressed, or simply not thriving in ways a family could understand. Elias Owen, writing in his late nineteenth-century collection of Welsh folklore, explicitly links changeling belief with “sickly” or “idiotic” babies and says that the methods used to recover the supposedly lost child could be extremely dangerous.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/stream/cu31924029911520/cu31924029911520_djvu.txt" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on archive.org.</span></span></span>

That is why this tale should be read with care. It is not just a charming fairy anecdote. It records a belief system that could put vulnerable children at risk. Owen discusses exposure beneath a fairy oak, the idea that fairies would rescue their own child if it were endangered, and then introduces the Trefeglwys tale as another example of that same logic. The frightening assumption is that harming or threatening the suspected changeling might force the fairies to return the human child.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/stream/cu31924029911520/cu31924029911520_djvu.txt" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on archive.org.</span></span></span>

The Trefeglwys setting intensifies that fear. The mother does not leave the twins for long, and she has a practical reason for going out, but the tale treats even a brief absence as dangerous. The solitary cottage, the neighbouring errand, the midday sighting of fairy figures and the later failure of the babies to grow all combine to make ordinary rural life feel exposed. For a modern reader, the story captures the pressure of parenting in a landscape where help, medicine and reassurance were not always close at hand.

The “wise man” is equally double-edged. Within the story, he solves the problem. From a critical perspective, he represents the authority of local magical expertise, which could offer comfort but also sanction danger. The instruction “do them no injury” if nothing unusual happens shows that the tale recognises a line between test and harm; yet the successful test leads directly to the lake. This is exactly why the story is valuable as folklore: it preserves both the hope that hidden order can be found and the risk of acting on fear.

## Why the lake ending matters

Llyn Ebyr is not a decorative setting. It is the place where the suspected changelings are returned to the fairy world and the human children are restored. In the story’s own terms, water functions as a boundary: a crossing point between the human household and the beings who have interfered with it. Edwin Sidney Hartland, in *The Science of Fairy Tales*, discusses the Trefeglwys-type episode as part of a wider pattern in which water is hostile or powerful against witches, fairies and other uncanny beings.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://sacred-texts.com/neu/celt/sft/sft06.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sacred-texts.com">[Internet Sacred Text Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sacred-texts.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on sacred-texts.com.</span></span></span>

The lake ending also shows the story’s harsh bargain. The mother does not defeat the fairies by strength. She forces an exchange by threatening what belongs to them. The goblins rescue their own children, and only then are the human twins returned. This reflects an important feature of fairy belief across Wales and beyond: the Fair Folk may be beautiful, musical or gift-giving in some tales, but they are not harmless. They operate by their own rules, and human beings survive by caution, ritual knowledge and sometimes desperate cunning.

In Montgomeryshire terms, Llyn Ebyr gives the tale a landscape memory. Mythstories’ modern version includes a photograph of Llyn Ebyr and identifies it as the place where the changelings were returned, showing that the lake still helps modern audiences locate the tale imaginatively, even when it is presented through schools, storytelling workshops and online heritage resources.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://mythstories.com/the-changelings-y-plant-cyfnewid/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: mythstories.com">[Mythstories]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">mythstories.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Changelings / Y Plant Cyfnewid – Mythstories</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Changelings / Y Plant Cyfnewid – Mythstories</span></span></span>

## How well attested is the Trefeglwys story?

The Trefeglwys changeling tale is unusually traceable for a local fairy legend. Elias Owen says the story appears in *The Cambrian Quarterly Magazine and Celtic Repertory*, volume 2, pages 86–87, under the title “The Egg Shell Pottage”. D. L. Ashliman’s changeling collection also identifies the source as *The Cambrian Quarterly Magazine and Celtic Repertory*, volume 2, number 5, dated 1 January 1830.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/stream/cu31924029911520/cu31924029911520_djvu.txt" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on archive.org.</span></span></span>

That does not mean the events happened as described. It means the story was in print by 1830 and was later important enough to be copied, quoted and adapted by major folklore collectors. Sikes gives it in *British Goblins* as a version related in Radnorshire, while locating the scene in Trefeglwys, Montgomeryshire. Owen quotes the tale in his Welsh folklore collection and says the earlier writer described it as translated almost literally from Welsh as told by local peasantry.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://sacred-texts.com/neu/celt/wfl/wfl05.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sacred-texts.com">[Internet Sacred Text Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sacred-texts.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on sacred-texts.com.</span></span></span>

There are also signs of later reshaping. Sikes comments that stories of this type often acquire named localities in non-primitive or later forms, meaning that a widespread motif may become attached to a specific place as it circulates. Joseph Jacobs’ later “Brewery of Eggshells” turns the material into a compact literary folktale, while modern projects present it as a Montgomeryshire story suitable for school and community storytelling.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://sacred-texts.com/neu/celt/wfl/wfl05.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sacred-texts.com">[sacred-texts.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sacred-texts.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on sacred-texts.com.</span></span></span>

The best way to judge the evidence is therefore balanced. The Trefeglwys tale is not a documented historical incident. It is, however, a well-attested Montgomeryshire version of a wider Welsh and Celtic changeling motif, with an early printed source, named local features, nineteenth-century folklore transmission and active modern retelling.

## Why this tale belongs to Montgomeryshire

Montgomeryshire’s folklore is often strongest where landscape, parish memory and older belief meet. Trefeglwys fits that pattern precisely. The tale is attached to a rural parish near Llanidloes, a named cottage of strife, a lake, harvest work, neighbours, and a wise man. It does not need a castle, saint or spectacular monster. Its drama comes from the fear that the uncanny could enter a small household through the most ordinary gap: a mother stepping away from the cradle.

The historic-county frame is also useful. Modern Trefeglwys is in Powys, but the older sources place the tale in the county of Montgomery. DataMapWales notes that the thirteen historic counties of Wales remained in use until the Local Government Act 1972 was implemented in 1974, after which new administrative counties, including Powys, replaced them; Wikimedia Commons’ historic-counties map identifies Montgomeryshire as one of those thirteen historic Welsh counties.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://datamap.gov.wales/layers/geonode%3Ahistoric_counties_bng_rcahmw_ply" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: datamap.gov.wales">[datamap.gov.wales]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">datamap.gov.wales</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Historic County Boundaries of Wales</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Historic County Boundaries of Wales</span></span></span>

For folklore, that distinction is not pedantry. The tale was collected, printed and remembered as Montgomeryshire material. Its local world is the old parish and county landscape of Llanidloes, Trefeglwys, Newtown road country and the surrounding uplands. Modern readers may find it through Powys heritage, but its older identity is Montgomeryshire.

## Retelling the fair folk without romanticising the fear

Modern fairy culture often turns fairies into tiny decorative beings or symbols of enchantment. The Trefeglwys story resists that. Its Fair Folk are not cute. They are suspected child-stealers whose presence turns a family against itself. Even the “happy” ending depends on an act that would be horrifying outside the logic of the tale.

That is why the story is best retold with two truths held together. First, it is a vivid fairy legend with a memorable test, a local lake and a strong place-name tradition. Secondly, it preserves a dangerous way of explaining infant illness and developmental difference. Owen’s warning about the risks of changeling belief, and Sikes’ description of the “cruel creed” surrounding changelings, should remain part of any responsible account.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/stream/cu31924029911520/cu31924029911520_djvu.txt" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on archive.org.</span></span></span>

The modern Montgomeryshire Folktales project shows one way the tale continues to live: as a local story retold by young people, linked to place, language and community memory rather than presented as literal instruction. Mythstories explains that its online resource grew from a 1947 Montgomeryshire History Teachers Committee book of site-specific tales, later reinterpreted through a Heritage Lottery Funded project with storytellers, artists, schools and volunteers.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://mythstories.com/the-changelings-y-plant-cyfnewid/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: mythstories.com">[Mythstories]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">mythstories.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Changelings / Y Plant Cyfnewid – Mythstories</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Changelings / Y Plant Cyfnewid – Mythstories</span></span></span>

Read this way, the Trefeglwys changeling tale remains one of Montgomeryshire’s most revealing fairy traditions. It keeps the old fear visible, but it also lets modern readers ask better questions: how communities made sense of sickness, why lonely places gathered supernatural stories, how local wise people gained authority, and how a frightening belief can become heritage without losing its edge.

  sections.forEach(function (section) {
    if (section.getAttribute('data-ebay-localized-init') === '1') return;
    section.setAttribute('data-ebay-localized-init', '1');
    var available = availableMarkets(section);
    var marketId = defaultMarket;
    if (window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation && window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation.inferMarket) {
      marketId = window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation.inferMarket(available, {
        storageKey: 'phoenix-ebay-market',
        defaultMarket: defaultMarket
      });
    } else if (available.indexOf(defaultMarket)< 0) {
      marketId = available[0] || defaultMarket;
    }
    var select = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-select]');
    if (select) {
      select.addEventListener('change', function () {
        applyMarket(section, select.value, true);
      });
    }
    var trigger = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-trigger]');
    var menu = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-menu]');
    if (trigger && menu) {
      trigger.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
        event.stopPropagation();
        var expanded = trigger.getAttribute('aria-expanded') === 'true';
        menu.hidden = expanded;
        trigger.setAttribute('aria-expanded', expanded ? 'false' : 'true');
        var picker = trigger.closest('.fr-ebay-market-picker');
        if (picker) picker.classList.toggle('fr-ebay-market-picker--open', !expanded);
      });
      Array.prototype.slice.call(menu.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-option]')).forEach(function (button) {
        button.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
          event.stopPropagation();
          applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-option'), true);
        });
      });
      document.addEventListener('click', function () { closeMenu(section); });
    }
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      button.addEventListener('click', function () {
        applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button'), true);
      });
    });
    applyMarket(section, marketId, false);
  });
})();
