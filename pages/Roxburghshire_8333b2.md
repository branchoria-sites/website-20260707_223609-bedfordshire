## Why Roxburghshire folklore feels so Border-shaped

Roxburghshire sits where hills, river valleys and national frontier history overlap. The county’s great story-world is not isolated “Celtic myth” or generic Scottish supernaturalism, but the culture of the Middle Shires: marches, reiving, ruined strongholds, old roads, abbey lands and communities that kept memory through song, procession and place-name. The rivers matter. The Teviot runs through the county towards the Tweed at Kelso, while Liddesdale opens towards the English border and carries some of the county’s fiercest reiver and castle traditions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://gazetteer.org.uk/place/Roxburghshire" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gazetteer.org.uk">[Gazetteer]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gazetteer.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on gazetteer.org.uk.</span></span></span>



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/Roxburghshire_8333b2-overview.webp" | relative_url }}" alt="Overview image for Where Roxburghshire&#x27;s Legends Meet the..." loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

That frontier setting explains why Roxburghshire folklore often feels half-historical. Its heroes are not only saints or fairies, but riders, outlaws, seers, border wardens and local champions. A tale may begin with a supernatural creature, but it usually ends in a place you can point to: a church doorway, a hill hollow, a stone circle, a tower ruin, a march route or a memorial ride. The result is a county tradition where the uncanny does not float above the landscape; it is pinned to it.

The historic-county frame also needs care. Modern “Scottish Borders” tourism naturally groups Roxburghshire with Berwickshire, Selkirkshire, Peeblesshire and other neighbouring areas. Folklore does the same. [Thomas]({{ 'thomas/' | relative_url }}) the Rhymer, for example, belongs to the wider Border tradition and to Ercildoune/Earlston in neighbouring Berwickshire, but the Eildon Hills and Melrose landscape are central to how his fairy legend is imagined and visited today. Historic-county pages should keep Roxburghshire at the centre while recognising that ballads, riding customs, reiver families and fairy roads rarely obey modern lines on a map.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_the_Rhymer" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[wikipedia.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Thomas the Rhymer</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Thomas the Rhymer</span></span></span>

## Thomas the Rhymer and the fairy landscape of the Eildons

The best-known fairy tradition connected with Roxburghshire’s landscape is the legend of Thomas the Rhymer and the Queen of Elfland. In the ballad, “True Thomas” lies on Huntlie Bank and sees a bright lady riding down by the Eildon Tree. She is not the Queen of Heaven, as he first imagines, but the Queen of Elfland. Thomas follows her into the otherworld, returns after a long absence, and gains the gift of prophecy and truth-speaking. The Scottish Poetry Library’s text preserves the famous opening at Huntlie Bank and the Eildon Tree, while ballad scholarship identifies the tale as Child Ballad 37 and Roud 219.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.scottishpoetrylibrary.org.uk/poem/thomas-the-rhymer/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: scottishpoetrylibrary.org.uk">[Scottish Poetry Library]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">scottishpoetrylibrary.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on scottishpoetrylibrary.org.uk.</span></span></span>

The county relevance lies in the Eildon landscape. The Eildon Hills rise just south of Melrose, one of Roxburghshire’s most recognisable landmarks. The exact site of the Eildon Tree and Huntly Bank has been debated, but the tradition consistently places the encounter around the Eildon Hills and the Melrose/Earlston borderland. That uncertainty is part of the folklore rather than a flaw in it: the story has survived because it attaches to a walkable, visible landscape without needing a modern plaque to settle every detail.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://walterscott.eu/education/ballads/supernatural-ballads/thomas-the-rhymer/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: walterscott.eu">[Walter Scott]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">walterscott.eu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">thomas the rhymer</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">thomas the rhymer</span></span></span>

Thomas is unusually important because he stands between person and legend. The historical Thomas of Ercildoune appears to have been a 13th-century Border figure associated with poetry and prophecy; later tradition turned him into “True Thomas”, the man who could not lie after his encounter with the fairy queen. The legend was transmitted through medieval romance, oral ballad, antiquarian collection and later literary retelling. Walter Scott’s *Minstrelsy of the Scottish Border*, first published in 1802, helped fix the Border ballads in print, but it also reminds readers that printed versions often sit at the end of a long, shifting oral life.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_the_Rhymer" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[wikipedia.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Thomas the Rhymer</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Thomas the Rhymer</span></span></span>

For readers of fairy folklore, Thomas matters because the story is not a pretty Victorian fairy tale. Elfland is alluring, dangerous and morally ambiguous. The fairy queen gives Thomas knowledge, but she also removes him from ordinary time. The roads she shows him — towards heaven, hell and her own green world — make the Eildons feel like a threshold landscape. That is why this tradition sits naturally beside other UK fairy-road and fairy-hill material, including neighbouring Selkirkshire’s Tam Lin tradition at Carterhaugh, while remaining rooted in the Roxburghshire-facing Eildon and Melrose terrain.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_the_Rhymer" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Thomas the Rhymer</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Thomas the Rhymer</span></span></span>

## The Linton Worm: Roxburghshire’s dragon at the church door

Roxburghshire’s clearest dragon legend is the Worm of Linton, attached to Linton near Morebattle. The story tells of a serpent or dragon-like “worm” that lived in a hollow on Linton Hill, sometimes called the Worm’s Den, and ravaged the surrounding country. A local champion, usually named as a Somerville of Lariston, defeats it by fastening burning peat or a fiery device to a lance and thrusting it into the creature’s mouth. The tale belongs to the wider British “worm” family of dragon legends, comparable in broad shape to the Lambton Worm and other northern serpent-slaying traditions, but Linton gives it a very specific Roxburghshire home.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Worm_of_Linton" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Worm of Linton</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Worm of Linton</span></span></span>

The most valuable evidence is not simply the story but the stone. Linton Church is a listed building, and Historic Environment Scotland describes the carved Norman tympanum above the doorway as showing a bearded knight on horseback thrusting a lance into the jaws of one of two animals. Its official record notes that the panel is said locally to depict William de Somerville slaying the dragon or worm, and calls the sculpture extremely significant as one of the few Scottish examples in that position.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://portal.historicenvironment.scot/apex/f?p=1505%3A300%3A%3A%3A%3A%3AVIEWTYPE%2CVIEWREF%3Adesignation%2CLB15254" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: portal.historicenvironment.scot">[portal.historicenvironment.scot]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">portal.historicenvironment.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on historicenvironment.scot.</span></span></span>

That does not prove that a dragon terrorised Linton. It shows something more useful for folklore: by the medieval and later local imagination, a church carving, a hill hollow, a family tradition and a parish story had fused into one memorable legend. Historic Environment Scotland also notes that the first records of a church at Linton date to about 1160, although later rebuilding means little of the earliest structure remains. The worm story therefore sits at the meeting point of Romanesque church sculpture, family prestige and local storytelling.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://portal.historicenvironment.scot/apex/f?p=1505%3A300%3A%3A%3A%3A%3AVIEWTYPE%2CVIEWREF%3Adesignation%2CLB15254" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: portal.historicenvironment.scot">[portal.historicenvironment.scot]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">portal.historicenvironment.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on historicenvironment.scot.</span></span></span>

The [Linton Worm]({{ 'linton-worm/' | relative_url }}) is a good example of how Roxburghshire legend turns topography into memory. Some versions say the worm’s death throes marked the hill, while modern visitor accounts point readers from the church towards Linton Hill and the surrounding Cheviot-edge landscape. Whether read as a dragon legend, a saint-and-monster echo, a family origin tale or a local explanation for landforms, it is one of the county’s strongest place-based traditions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Worm_of_Linton" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Worm of Linton</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Worm of Linton</span></span></span>

## Hermitage Castle, Robin Redcap and the dark folklore of Liddesdale

If Linton gives Roxburghshire its dragon, Hermitage Castle gives it its most sinister goblin. The castle stands in Liddesdale, in the historic county of Roxburghshire, and is strongly associated with tales of William de Soulis, tyranny, witchcraft and a redcap familiar called Robin Redcap. Redcaps are a Border type of malicious goblin said to haunt ruined towers and castles, especially places linked with bloodshed. In the Hermitage tradition, Robin Redcap is the familiar spirit or demonic helper of “Bad Lord Soulis”.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Redcap" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wikipedia.org.</span></span></span>

The legend says Soulis could not be harmed by ordinary weapons, so the people bound him in lead and boiled him at Ninestane Rig, a stone circle near the castle. The historical record is less theatrical: William de Soulis was implicated in a conspiracy against Robert Bruce and died as a prisoner at Dumbarton Castle. That gap between record and legend is important. It shows how Border memory transformed a politically suspect noble into a wizard-tyrant, then placed his punishment in the local ritual landscape.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Redcap" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wikipedia.org.</span></span></span>

Hermitage also shows the role of literary antiquarians in intensifying folklore. Walter Scott and his circle, including John Leyden, were fascinated by the Border past. Later commentary has noted that some of Hermitage’s “grisly” atmosphere owes much to ballad and antiquarian elaboration, not only to medieval fact. That does not make the folklore worthless; it helps explain why visitors experience Hermitage as both monument and story-machine.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://blog.nls.uk/walter-scott-and-the-minstrelsy-of-the-scottish-border/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: blog.nls.uk">[blog.nls.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">blog.nls.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">walter scott and the minstrelsy of the scottish border</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">walter scott and the minstrelsy of the scottish border</span></span></span>

For a county folklore page, Robin Redcap is especially useful because he links Roxburghshire to a wider Anglo-Scottish Border creature-type. Redcaps are not gentle household brownies. They belong to ruined, violent places; they are a supernatural expression of the fear attached to towers, raids, imprisonment and lawless lordship. In Roxburghshire, that makes Hermitage Castle less a “haunted castle” cliché than a concentrated version of Liddesdale’s hard historical memory.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Redcap" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wikipedia.org.</span></span></span>

## Reiver ballads: when history becomes oral legend

Roxburghshire’s folklore cannot be separated from the Border ballads. These songs preserve the imaginative world of reivers, raids, rescues, feuds and loyalty under pressure. Walter Scott’s *Minstrelsy of the Scottish Border* drew heavily on southern Scottish tradition and was shaped by fieldwork, manuscripts, collaborators and editorial intervention. The National Library of Scotland describes the publication as one of Scott’s earliest major works, first appearing in 1802, while later accounts stress both its preservation value and its complicated editorial nature.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://blog.nls.uk/walter-scott-and-the-minstrelsy-of-the-scottish-border/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: blog.nls.uk">[blog.nls.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">blog.nls.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">walter scott and the minstrelsy of the scottish border</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">walter scott and the minstrelsy of the scottish border</span></span></span>

Liddesdale is especially important. Ballads such as “Jock o’ the Side” and “Hobbie Noble” belong to the reiver world of rescue, betrayal and cross-border outlawry. “Jock o’ the Side” is a jailbreak ballad in which a captured reiver is rescued, and it is classed as Child Ballad 187 and Roud 82. “Hobbie Noble” is closely linked to it, with Scott’s educational material treating the two as connected reiver ballads.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://walterscott.eu/education/ballads/reiver-ballads/jock-o-the-side/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: walterscott.eu">[walterscott.eu]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">walterscott.eu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on walterscott.eu.</span></span></span>

These ballads matter because they show a different kind of folklore from fairy tales and dragon stories. Their marvel is social rather than magical: impossible rides, daring rescues, kinship loyalty, trickery, endurance and the rough honour-code of the Marches. They preserve how communities wanted to remember reivers — not merely as criminals, and not simply as heroes, but as dangerous figures whose actions could be condemned, admired and sung about at the same time.

Scott’s role needs to be handled honestly. He preserved and popularised a body of Border tradition that might otherwise be far less visible, but he also selected, shaped and sometimes polished what he printed. For Roxburghshire, that means the ballads should not be treated as transparent transcripts of medieval events. They are traditional narratives, filtered through singers, collectors, manuscripts and antiquarian taste. Their value is in showing what the Border past became in memory.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://blog.nls.uk/walter-scott-and-the-minstrelsy-of-the-scottish-border/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: blog.nls.uk">[blog.nls.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">blog.nls.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">walter scott and the minstrelsy of the scottish border</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">walter scott and the minstrelsy of the scottish border</span></span></span>

## Witches, cunning folk and the danger of making thin evidence too neat

Roxburghshire certainly belongs within the wider Scottish witchcraft landscape, but the evidence is different from the county’s fairy, dragon and ballad traditions. For witchcraft, the strongest starting point is not local ghost lore but the Survey of Scottish Witchcraft, the University of Edinburgh database covering people known to have been accused of witchcraft in Scotland between 1563 and 1736. The project was designed to examine accused people, belief patterns, local and national legal procedures, and the geography and chronology of accusation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://witches.hca.ed.ac.uk/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: witches.hca.ed.ac.uk">[witches.hca.ed.ac.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">witches.hca.ed.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on ed.ac.uk.</span></span></span>

Scotland’s witch-hunt history was severe, and modern research has repeatedly shown that accusations were embedded in community conflict, religious anxiety, healing practices, neighbour disputes and legal procedure. A 2024 study using the Survey data identified folk-healers and midwives among accused people and found that their alleged practices were often reframed as harmful magic rather than helpful care. This is directly relevant to folklore because many charms, cures and protective rituals survive only because they were recorded by hostile courts or church authorities.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0260691723003209" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on sciencedirect.com.</span></span></span>

For Roxburghshire readers, the key caution is not to turn every “witch” place-name or execution tradition into a verified case. Border towns, including Jedburgh and nearby districts, are often mentioned in modern witch-trail storytelling, but the most responsible approach is to separate three layers: documented accusations and trials; later local memory of execution places such as gallows hills; and modern commemorative or tourism retellings. The Survey and its later mapping projects are the best route for checking names and places before attaching a specific story to a parish.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.heritagefund.org.uk/news/mapping-scotlands-accused-witches-through-open-data" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: heritagefund.org.uk">[heritagefund.org.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">heritagefund.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on heritagefund.org.uk.</span></span></span>

That caution does not diminish the subject. It makes it more humane. Witchcraft folklore in Roxburghshire is not just about spooky hills or “dark tourism”; it is about how ordinary misfortune, healing knowledge, suspicion and authority could become deadly. In a county already rich in tales of borders and belonging, witchcraft material asks a different question: who was pushed outside the protection of the community, and how was that exclusion remembered?

## Holy wells, saints and sacred water in the Roxburghshire landscape

Roxburghshire’s sacred landscape is quieter than its dragons and redcaps, but wells and saints are an important part of the county’s belief culture. The wider Scottish Borders contains many named holy wells, and lists of Borders wells include several in and around Roxburghshire’s historic parishes and neighbouring Border districts: St Dunstan’s Well at Melrose, St Helen’s and St John’s wells around Darnick, St Mary’s Well at Dingleton, St Cuthbert associations around Eildon and Maxton, Lady Wells, and other saintly or ecclesiastical water sites.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://britishholywells.co.uk/scotland/scottishborders/scottishborderslist.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: britishholywells.co.uk">[British Holy Wells]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">britishholywells.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on britishholywells.co.uk.</span></span></span>

These wells should not all be treated as active folklore sites in the same way. Some are extant, some capped, some destroyed, some lost or uncertain. That variation matters. A “holy well” may preserve medieval devotion, post-Reformation memory, local healing belief, a field-name, a Victorian antiquarian note or a modern heritage revival. In Roxburghshire, the most meaningful pattern is the association between water, abbey landscapes and saints, especially around Melrose and the Tweed corridor.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://britishholywells.co.uk/scotland/scottishborders/scottishborderslist.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: britishholywells.co.uk">[British Holy Wells]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">britishholywells.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on britishholywells.co.uk.</span></span></span>

St Cuthbert is especially important for the Borders. St Cuthbert’s Way now runs from Melrose to Lindisfarne, turning an early medieval saint’s life and northern Christian memory into a modern long-distance walking route. That is not “folklore” in the narrow sense of a supernatural tale, but it is part of how sacred landscape is retold today: pilgrims, walkers and visitors experience old ecclesiastical memory through paths, springs, abbey ruins and place-names.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.stcuthbertsway.info/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: stcuthbertsway.info">[St Cuthbert&#x27;s Way]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">stcuthbertsway.info</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on stcuthbertsway.info.</span></span></span>

The useful distinction for readers is this: Roxburghshire’s wells are not usually dramatic miracle legends in the way some holy wells elsewhere are. Their strength is cumulative. They show how Christian devotion, local naming and water sources shaped the county’s mental map, especially around abbeys, old parishes and routes through the Tweed and Teviot valleys.

## Common ridings: living custom, not museum folklore

Roxburghshire’s most visible living folklore is not a ghost story but a mounted civic tradition. Hawick Common Riding is widely presented as the first of the Border Common Ridings. It commemorates both the ancient practice of riding the marches, or boundaries of common land, and the capture of an English flag by youths of Hawick at Hornshole in 1514. The Hawick Common Riding organisation describes the election of the Cornet, rideouts to surrounding farms and villages, the Mosspaul ride, the visit to the Hornshole memorial, and the ritual importance of returning the flag “unsullied”.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://hawickcommonriding.co.uk/the-common-riding/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: hawickcommonriding.co.uk">[Hawick Common Riding]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">hawickcommonriding.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on hawickcommonriding.co.uk.</span></span></span>

This matters because it keeps an old border idea alive in public ritual: land is known by riding it. What began as boundary maintenance and defence becomes ceremony, music, memory and identity. Hawick’s festival is not merely a pageant about the past; it is a way the town annually tells itself who it is. The songs, colours, Cornet, flag, rideouts and communal gatherings make history bodily and local.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://hawickcommonriding.co.uk/the-common-riding/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: hawickcommonriding.co.uk">[Hawick Common Riding]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">hawickcommonriding.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on hawickcommonriding.co.uk.</span></span></span>

Jedburgh’s Jethart Callant’s Festival offers a related but distinct example. It dates as a festival to the mid-20th century, with formal origins in 1947, but its rideouts commemorate older events and relationships, including Jedburgh’s alliance with Kelso in reiver times and rides to historically charged places. Modern summaries note that the festival lasts around two weeks and culminates in Festival Day, with the Callant as the central figure.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://scotlandstartshere.com/event/jedburgh-callants-festival/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: scotlandstartshere.com">[scotlandstartshere.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">scotlandstartshere.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on scotlandstartshere.com.</span></span></span>

Kelso Civic Week is younger in formal origin, emerging in the 1930s in the wider Borders festival movement, but it similarly sustains civic tradition, local pride and equestrian ceremony. Together, Hawick, Jedburgh and Kelso show how Roxburghshire folklore is not confined to old books. It is still performed in streets, on horseback, at memorials and along remembered routes.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.kelsocivicweek.co.uk/about" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: kelsocivicweek.co.uk">[Kelso Civic Week]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">kelsocivicweek.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on kelsocivicweek.co.uk.</span></span></span>

## What is old oral tradition, and what is later retelling?

A good Roxburghshire folklore page should not flatten every tradition into the same category. The county’s stories survive through different channels, and those channels affect how confidently they can be read.

**Old oral and ballad tradition** includes Thomas the Rhymer, reiver ballads and fragments of fairy and Border supernatural lore. These often reached print through collectors such as Walter Scott and Francis James Child, meaning they are old in substance but mediated in surviving form.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_the_Rhymer" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Thomas the Rhymer</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Thomas the Rhymer</span></span></span>

**Materially anchored local legend** includes the Linton Worm. The story may be fabulous, but it is tied to a real medieval church carving, a named parish and a local hill. The stone does not authenticate the monster; it authenticates the long local attachment of the story to the site.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://portal.historicenvironment.scot/apex/f?p=1505%3A300%3A%3A%3A%3A%3AVIEWTYPE%2CVIEWREF%3Adesignation%2CLB15254" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: portal.historicenvironment.scot">[portal.historicenvironment.scot]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">portal.historicenvironment.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on historicenvironment.scot.</span></span></span>

**Antiquarian and literary intensification** is especially visible at Hermitage Castle. The real fortress, real violence and real Border politics created fertile ground, while ballad-makers and antiquarians heightened the redcap and Lord Soulis material into one of Scotland’s darkest castle legends.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hermitage_Castle" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Hermitage Castle</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Hermitage Castle</span></span></span>

**Living custom** includes Hawick Common Riding, Jedburgh’s Callant’s Festival and Kelso Civic Week. These are modern public events with historic claims and older roots, but they are not frozen survivals. They change, adapt and continue because communities still find meaning in them.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://hawickcommonriding.co.uk/the-common-riding/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: hawickcommonriding.co.uk">[hawickcommonriding.co.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">hawickcommonriding.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on hawickcommonriding.co.uk.</span></span></span>

That distinction is what keeps the county’s folklore interesting without making it credulous. Roxburghshire’s traditions are not all equally ancient, equally oral or equally documented. Their power lies in how they have been preserved: in song, stone, print, procession, place-name and repeated local telling.

## Why Roxburghshire matters in the wider UK folklore map

Roxburghshire is a key county for understanding Border folklore because it brings together several major UK motifs in one landscape. The Eildon tradition connects to fairy abduction and otherworld journeys. Linton connects to the northern worm or dragon-slayer cycle. Hermitage connects to redcaps, haunted towers and the demonisation of violent lordship. Liddesdale ballads connect to reiver memory and oral heroic narrative. Common Ridings connect to seasonal civic custom, boundary ritual and public performance.

The county also shows why historic geography matters. A modern Scottish Borders article can sweep all of this into one regional story, but a Roxburghshire frame brings out the Teviotdale, Liddesdale, Melrose, Hawick, Jedburgh, Kelso and Linton anchors that make the traditions feel local rather than generic. At the same time, the county cannot be sealed off from its neighbours. Thomas the Rhymer crosses towards Berwickshire; Tam Lin belongs strongly to neighbouring Selkirkshire; reiver ballads move between Roxburghshire, Northumberland, Cumberland and beyond; and Common Ridings form a wider Border custom.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://map.realcounties.com/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: map.realcounties.com">[realcounties.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">map.realcounties.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on realcounties.com.</span></span></span>

For visitors and readers, the most memorable way to understand Roxburghshire folklore is to follow the places: the Eildon Hills for fairy prophecy, Linton Kirk for the worm, Hermitage for redcap and Soulis traditions, Hawick for living march-riding custom, Jedburgh and Kelso for civic memory, and Liddesdale for the ballad world of raids and rescues. The county’s folklore is not a separate layer laid over history; it is one of the ways Roxburghshire has remembered being a Border county.

    section.setAttribute('data-ebay-selected-market', marketId);

  sections.forEach(function (section) {

        storageKey: 'phoenix-ebay-market',
        defaultMarket: defaultMarket
      });

      marketId = available[0] || defaultMarket;
    }
    var select = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-select]');
    if (select) {
      select.addEventListener('change', function () {
        applyMarket(section, select.value, true);
      });
    }
    var trigger = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-trigger]');
    var menu = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-menu]');
    if (trigger && menu) {
      trigger.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
        event.stopPropagation();
        var expanded = trigger.getAttribute('aria-expanded') === 'true';
        menu.hidden = expanded;
        trigger.setAttribute('aria-expanded', expanded ? 'false' : 'true');
        var picker = trigger.closest('.fr-ebay-market-picker');
        if (picker) picker.classList.toggle('fr-ebay-market-picker--open', !expanded);
      });
      Array.prototype.slice.call(menu.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-option]')).forEach(function (button) {
        button.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
          event.stopPropagation();
          applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-option'), true);
        });
      });
      document.addEventListener('click', function () { closeMenu(section); });
    }
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      button.addEventListener('click', function () {
        applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button'), true);
      });
    });
    applyMarket(section, marketId, false);
