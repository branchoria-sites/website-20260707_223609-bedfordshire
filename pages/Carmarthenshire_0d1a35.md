## Introduction

For this project, Carmarthenshire is treated as a historic county as well as a modern Welsh county. That matters because folklore follows older parishes, lordships, roads, rivers and uplands more readily than today’s administrative lines. Carmarthenshire was one of the Welsh shires named in the late medieval county system associated with the Statute of Rhuddlan, and modern local government has changed more than once, especially through Dyfed in 1974 and the re-established Carmarthenshire unitary authority in 1996.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://datamap.gov.wales/layers/geonode%3Ahistoric_counties_bng_rcahmw_ply/metadata_detail" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: datamap.gov.wales">[gov.wales]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">datamap.gov.wales</span><span class="citation-popover-title">metadata detail</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">metadata detail</span></span></span>

## Why Carmarthenshire feels so legendary

Carmarthenshire’s folklore is not simply “Welsh mythology in one county”. It is rooted in a particular landscape: Carmarthen, one of Wales’s oldest towns; the Tywi valley around Llandeilo and Dinefwr; the Black Mountain edge near Myddfai and Llanddeusant; the Cothi valley around Cwrt-y-Cadno; and the industrial south around Llanelli, where older rural remedies and beliefs survived alongside modern town life. The result is a county where medieval learning, oral tradition and local place-memory overlap.

The county’s identity also depends on a tension that makes its folklore interesting. Some traditions are old oral tales later written down by antiquarians; some are medieval manuscript traditions with legendary framing; some are tourist retellings shaped for visitors; and some are documented folk practices from the nineteenth and twentieth centuries. The Lady of Llyn y Fan Fach, for example, is both a fairy-wife tale, a lake legend, a Myddfai origin story and a modern walking-tour landmark. The Harries family of Cwrt-y-Cadno, by contrast, are not just “wizards” from legend: their manuscripts, patient records, astrological papers and books survive in the National Library of Wales.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK558273/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[nih.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">NCBIThe Nature of the Corpus</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NCBIThe Nature of the Corpus</span></span></span>

## Merlin’s Carmarthen: prophecy, oak and town identity

Carmarthen is the county’s great legendary anchor. The town’s Welsh name, often explained through Myrddin, helped attach it to Merlin in popular imagination, and tourism bodies still describe Merlin as strongly associated with Carmarthen. That does not mean every Merlin story in Carmarthen is ancient in its present form. It means the town has become one of the places where medieval literary Merlin, Welsh Myrddin traditions and local civic legend meet.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.visitwales.com/info/history-heritage-and-traditions/welsh-myths-and-legends" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: visitwales.com">[Visit Wales]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">visitwales.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on visitwales.com.</span></span></span>

The most memorable Carmarthen tradition is Merlin’s Oak. A well-known local saying warned that when Merlin’s Oak fell, Carmarthen town would fall too. The old tree once stood near Oak Lane and Priory Street; a branch is preserved at Carmarthenshire County Museum, and local legend linked the final removal of the remains in 1978 with severe flooding in Carmarthen. This is best read as folklore rather than proof of supernatural cause and effect: the power of the story lies in how a town landmark became a prophecy, and how later events were folded back into the legend.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.landoflegends.wales/location/carmarthenshire-county-museum" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: landoflegends.wales">[Land of Legends]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">landoflegends.wales</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Land of Legends Carmarthenshire County Museum</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Land of Legends Carmarthenshire County Museum</span></span></span>

Carmarthen also matters because of manuscript culture. The Black Book of Carmarthen, a thirteenth-century Welsh manuscript, includes early poems connected with Arthurian and prophetic material, and modern heritage interpretation links it to Arthur and Merlin as a clairvoyant figure. The point for readers is not that Carmarthen “owns” Merlin in a simple way, but that the town has a unusually strong claim as one of the places where Welsh literary memory, civic pride and later Arthurian tourism reinforce each other.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.landoflegends.wales/location/carmarthenshire-county-museum" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: landoflegends.wales">[Land of Legends]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">landoflegends.wales</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Land of Legends Carmarthenshire County Museum</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Land of Legends Carmarthenshire County Museum</span></span></span>

## Llyn y Fan Fach and the fairy bride of the Black Mountain

The Lady of Llyn y Fan Fach is Carmarthenshire’s most famous fairy legend. The lake lies on the western side of Bannau Brycheiniog, on the Black Mountain edge of north-east Carmarthenshire, and Visit Wales presents it as the home of the Lady of the Lake tradition associated with Myddfai. In the familiar version, a young local farmer sees a beautiful woman from the lake, wins her as his wife, and lives with her under a condition: if he strikes her three times, she must return to the water. After the three blows, often framed as misunderstandings rather than cruelty in later retellings, she leaves and takes her otherworldly cattle with her. Their sons become the famous Physicians of Myddfai.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.visitwales.com/info/history-heritage-and-traditions/welsh-myths-and-legends" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: visitwales.com">[visitwales.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">visitwales.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on visitwales.com.</span></span></span>

This tale matters because it joins two kinds of Welsh tradition. On one level it is a fairy-wife story, part of a wider pattern in which a supernatural bride marries a human man under a taboo or condition. On another level it is a local origin story for a real medical tradition associated with Myddfai. The story explains not just why the lake feels enchanted, but why the physicians’ knowledge was imagined as partly otherworldly, inherited from a lake woman who returned to instruct her sons.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK558273/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[NCBI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">NCBIThe Nature of the Corpus</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NCBIThe Nature of the Corpus</span></span></span>

The landscape gives the legend much of its force. Llyn y Fan Fach is a glacial upland lake under the Carmarthenshire Fans, a setting that naturally lends itself to threshold stories: water as a doorway, mountain as boundary, cattle as wealth, and the lonely farm as the meeting-place between ordinary life and the otherworld. Modern walking guides and tourism pages keep the legend alive, but the story is more than a visitor-board anecdote. It is one of the clearest examples in Wales of a fairy tradition tied to a named family, a named lake and a manuscript medical legacy.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.visitwales.com/things-do/nature-and-landscapes/wonderful-welsh-rivers-lakes-and-waterways" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: visitwales.com">[visitwales.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">visitwales.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on visitwales.com.</span></span></span>

## The Physicians of Myddfai: where legend meets medical memory

The Physicians of Myddfai are central to Carmarthenshire folklore because they blur the line between family history, medieval medicine and wonder-tale. The tradition places the first physicians at Myddfai, near Llandovery, and connects them with Rhiwallon and his sons, who are said to have served Rhys Gryg of Dinefwr. Modern scholarship on medieval Welsh medical texts notes that a famous preface attributes a medical collection to Rhiwallon and his sons, connecting them with Rhys Gryg of Dinefwr Castle in Llandeilo.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK558273/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[NCBI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">NCBIThe Nature of the Corpus</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NCBIThe Nature of the Corpus</span></span></span>

The manuscript record is important. The Red Book of Hergest, compiled between the late fourteenth and early fifteenth centuries, contains a medicinal tract attributed to the Physicians of Myddfai. National Museum Wales also notes that the Physicians’ healing knowledge appears in the Red Book of Hergest and that Welsh oral testimony preserved many plant-based remedies across Wales. This gives the Myddfai tradition a stronger evidential base than many local legends: the lake woman is folklore, but the medical corpus and its later reputation are historically traceable.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.library.wales/about-nlw/press-and-news/press-releases/2014-press-releases/final-three-weeks-for-the-four-books" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: library.wales">[library.wales]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">library.wales</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on library.wales.</span></span></span>

For Carmarthenshire’s identity, the Physicians are especially valuable because they show “magic” as care, skill and practical knowledge. The story is not about sorcery for its own sake. It is about healing herbs, inherited expertise, local prestige and the belief that true knowledge might come from a liminal source: a lake, a fairy mother, a mountain-edge encounter. That makes Myddfai one of the county’s strongest internal links between fairy lore, folk medicine and manuscript culture.

## Cunning folk in the Cothi valley

Carmarthenshire’s best-documented magical practitioners are the Harries family of Cwrt-y-Cadno, especially John Harries, also known as Shon Harri Shon. The Dictionary of Welsh Biography describes him as an astrologer and medical practitioner, probably born at Pantycoy in Cwrt-y-Cadno, Carmarthenshire, and baptised at Caeo in 1785. The National Library of Wales holds his Book of Incantations, a manuscript volume from his library containing spirit lists and seals from seventeenth-century grimoires.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://biography.wales/article/s14-HARR-JOH-1839.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: biography.wales">[biography.wales]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">biography.wales</span><span class="citation-popover-title">s14 HARR JOH 1839</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">s14 HARR JOH 1839</span></span></span>

This is one of the places where Carmarthenshire folklore becomes unusually concrete. The Pant-coy manuscripts include patient prescriptions, payment records, horoscopes, correspondence, medical notes and astrological material. Archives describe John Harries and his son Henry as among Wales’s most famous cunning men: people associated with healing, charming, astrology, prophecy and conjuring.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archifau.llyfrgell.cymru/index.php/pant-coy-mss" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archifau.llyfrgell.cymru">[Archifau Cymru]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archifau.llyfrgell.cymru</span><span class="citation-popover-title">pant coy mss</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">pant coy mss</span></span></span>

The Harries tradition also helps readers avoid a common misunderstanding. In local culture, “cunning folk” were not simply witches in the horror-story sense. They occupied a more ambiguous role: healers, diviners, charmers and sometimes feared experts whose services people sought when ordinary explanations failed. Historian Owen Davies’s work on cunning folk in England and Wales places such practitioners within a wider eighteenth- and nineteenth-century world of medicine, astrology, reputation and local need.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://uhra.herts.ac.uk/id/eprint/3510/1/906673.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: uhra.herts.ac.uk">[Hertfordshire Research Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">uhra.herts.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on herts.ac.uk.</span></span></span>

## Holy wells, saints and healing water

Holy wells are another important Carmarthenshire tradition, though they are less famous to casual visitors than Merlin or Llyn y Fan Fach. Wales has a long well tradition in which springs were associated with saints, healing, pilgrimage, offerings and local ritual. Francis Jones’s The Holy Wells of Wales remains a key collection, and the University of Wales Press describes it as drawing on published, manuscript and oral sources while tracing how well traditions were Christianised, condemned, explained and yet survived.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.uwp.co.uk/book/holy-wells-of-wales/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: uwp.co.uk">[UWP]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">uwp.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on uwp.co.uk.</span></span></span>

In Carmarthenshire, holy wells belong to a broader west-Wales pattern of sacred water and local saints. A Cadw-funded Dyfed Archaeological Trust project set out to identify medieval and early post-medieval holy wells across Pembrokeshire, Carmarthenshire and Ceredigion, beginning with Historic Environment Record material and narrowing nearly 200 possible sites to 188 for desktop appraisal and 96 for field visits. This kind of work matters because many wells are modest, vulnerable and easy to miss: folklore can survive in a spring-name, a parish memory or a damaged structure rather than a grand monument.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://heneb.org.uk/project/medieval-and-early-post-medieval-holy-wells-in-west-wales/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: heneb.org.uk">[Heneb]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">heneb.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on heneb.org.uk.</span></span></span>

Saintly place-lore is also visible at Llandybie. The National Churches Trust records the legend that St Tybie and St Lluan were daughters of Brychan Brycheiniog who lived devotionally in the area in the fifth century before being killed by marauding Irishmen; Llandybie is explained as the church enclosure of Tybie. Dyfed Archaeological Trust notes evidence suggesting pre-conquest religious use at St Tybie’s, including the Celtic dedication and formerly subcircular churchyard.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nationalchurchestrust.org/church/st-tybie-llandybie" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nationalchurchestrust.org">[National Churches Trust]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nationalchurchestrust.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nationalchurchestrust.org.</span></span></span>

## Stones, place-names and the memory hidden in fields

Not all Carmarthenshire folklore survives as a polished tale. Some of it is embedded in place-names, field-names and archaeological memory. The Royal Commission on the Ancient and Historical Monuments of Wales has described work on historic place-names that included old Carmarthenshire, noting that field names could preserve memories of vanished cairns, standing stones and chapels. In some cases, the name was the only surviving clue to a site that had disappeared by the time it was recorded.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://rcahmw.gov.uk/place-names-project-2017/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: rcahmw.gov.uk">[RCAHMW]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">rcahmw.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">place names project 2017</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">place names project 2017</span></span></span>

This is crucial for understanding county folklore. A fairy hill, a giant’s stone, a saint’s well or a “church field” may not come with a long narrative attached. Its folklore value may lie in the fact that people once explained the landscape through supernatural agency, saintly presence or heroic memory. The Royal Commission gives examples from its place-name work of sites with local traditions involving supernatural agency preventing a church from being built in the intended place, and other locations remembered as the burial site of a mischievous spirit. Such details show how old inventories can preserve the last trace of stories that might otherwise vanish.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://rcahmw.gov.uk/place-names-project-2017/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: rcahmw.gov.uk">[RCAHMW]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">rcahmw.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">place names project 2017</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">place names project 2017</span></span></span>

For readers exploring Carmarthenshire, this means the folklore map is not limited to named attractions. It also includes churchyards, farms, vanished chapels, ridgelines, wells, fields and old roads. The county’s legendary texture often sits just below the surface of ordinary geography.

## Black dogs, fairy roads and wider Welsh motifs

Carmarthenshire does not have one universally famous black-dog legend on the scale of East Anglia’s Black Shuck, but it sits within the Welsh tradition of spectral hounds and ominous dogs. The Welsh gwyllgi, often described as a terrifying black dog or “dog of darkness”, belongs to a wider British and Irish black-dog pattern associated with roads, dusk, death omens and lonely places. Most well-known Welsh examples are attached to north-east Wales, so Carmarthenshire should not be over-claimed as a main black-dog county.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gwyllgi" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[wikipedia.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wikipedia.org.</span></span></span>

What Carmarthenshire does have in abundance is the kind of landscape where such motifs make sense: drovers’ routes, mountain passes, wooded valleys, old parish roads and liminal water sites. The same applies to fairy roads and fairy hills. The county’s best-evidenced fairy material is the Llyn y Fan Fach cycle, but its wider belief culture belongs to a Welsh world in which fairies, otherworld cattle, lake women, warning apparitions and enchanted places overlap.

The useful distinction is between motif and local attestation. A motif is a recognisable story-pattern shared across regions; a local attestation is a specific story attached to a named place and source. Llyn y Fan Fach is strongly localised. The Welsh black dog is a wider national motif that may help interpret Carmarthenshire’s roads and fear-lore, but it should not be forced into the county without careful place-specific evidence.

## Customs, remedies and everyday belief

Carmarthenshire folklore is not only about dramatic legends. It also includes everyday practices: charms, plant remedies, protective habits and family cures. National Museum Wales’s account of traditional medicines gives examples from Welsh oral testimony, including a Carmarthenshire example from Llanfallteg near Carmarthen, where garlic was put in the bottom of socks overnight to ward off coughs and colds. The same museum account places such remedies beside the Myddfai medical tradition and other Welsh plant-based cures.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://museum.wales/blog/2160/Traditional-Medicines-/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: museum.wales">[Museum Wales]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">museum.wales</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Traditional Medicines</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Traditional Medicines</span></span></span>

These small practices matter because they show how belief worked in daily life. A family remedy did not need to be called “magic” to belong to folk culture. It could be passed down because it seemed useful, because an elder recommended it, because it fitted older ideas about smell, heat, plants and protection, or because it linked the household to a wider Welsh healing tradition.

This everyday layer also helps explain why figures such as the Myddfai physicians and the Harries cunning men remained memorable. They stood at the point where learned medicine, folk remedy, astrology, manuscript culture and popular trust met. In Carmarthenshire, healing is one of the main bridges between folklore and history.

## What is old, what is literary, and what is modern retelling?

Carmarthenshire’s folklore is strongest when the different evidence layers are kept distinct. Some stories are preserved through medieval manuscripts; some through nineteenth- and twentieth-century antiquarian or scholarly collecting; some through archival records; and some through modern tourism, walking culture and popular retelling. These layers do not cancel each other out, but they do change how a story should be read.

The Lady of Llyn y Fan Fach is a good example. A modern visitor may meet it through Visit Wales, a walking blog or a local attraction page. A folklore reader may trace it through John Rhys’s Celtic Folklore: Welsh and Manx, W. Jenkyn Thomas’s Welsh Fairy Book, or later scholarship on the Lady of the Lake cycle. A historian of medicine may approach it through the Myddfai medical texts and the Red Book of Hergest. Each route tells a slightly different story: place legend, fairy tale, manuscript tradition, medical heritage.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/details/celticfolklorewe01rhys" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[archive.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on archive.org.</span></span></span>

Merlin’s Oak works differently. Its power is civic and prophetic: a town tree, a warning rhyme, a preserved branch, and later flood-memory. The Harries family works differently again: their reputation as wizards is folkloric, but the archive is material and detailed. Carmarthenshire’s best folklore writing should therefore resist flattening everything into “myth”. The county’s traditions are more interesting when they are allowed to be mixed: oral, literary, archival, devotional, medical and touristic.

## Why Carmarthenshire matters in UK folklore

Carmarthenshire deserves a major place in a UK folklore map because it offers one of the clearest county-level examples of folklore attached to landscape and practical life. It has a nationally recognised Merlin centre at Carmarthen; a famous fairy lake at Llyn y Fan Fach; a medically significant legend at Myddfai; a documented cunning-folk archive at Cwrt-y-Cadno; and a quieter network of holy wells, saints, place-names and remedies.

It also shows how folklore crosses boundaries without losing its local centre. The Lady of Llyn y Fan Fach belongs to a wider Welsh and Celtic fairy-wife pattern, but its emotional geography is Carmarthenshire. Merlin belongs to international Arthurian culture, but Carmarthen gives him a civic home. Holy wells are found across Wales and Britain, but Carmarthenshire’s wells, saints and parishes root the pattern in local ground. Cunning folk were known across England and Wales, but the Harries manuscripts make the Cothi valley one of the most vivid Welsh case studies.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.visitwales.com/info/history-heritage-and-traditions/welsh-myths-and-legends" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: visitwales.com">[visitwales.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">visitwales.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on visitwales.com.</span></span></span>

The county’s folklore is therefore best understood not as a list of spooky stories, but as a living map of how people explained water, illness, luck, danger, memory and place. Carmarthenshire’s legends endure because they are attached to things people can still see: a lake under the Black Mountain, a town shaped by Merlin, a museum-held oak branch, a churchyard dedication, a manuscript page, a valley where “wizard” could mean healer as much as magician.
