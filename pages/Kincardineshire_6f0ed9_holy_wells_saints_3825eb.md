## Introduction

Holy wells in the Mearns were not just picturesque springs. They were places where water, saints, healing, fairs and routeways met: St Kieran at Stonehaven and Fetteresso, St Palladius at Fordoun, St Ninian around [Dunnottar]({{ 'dunnottar/' | relative_url }}), and the fainter St Mungo or Kentigern tradition at Kinneff. Together they show how [Kincardineshire]({{ 'where-kincardineshire-turns-history-into/' | relative_url }})’s sacred landscape worked at parish level. A well might supply water, mark a saint’s presence, draw pilgrims to relics, give a fair its name, or preserve a memory after the chapel beside it had fallen into ruin.

The evidence is uneven, which is part of the story. Fordoun is strongly attested as a medieval pilgrimage centre, with a surviving chapel, crypt and well tradition. St Kieran’s Well at Stonehaven is visible as a nineteenth-century fountain, but the older spring itself was reportedly lost when the water supply was altered. St Ninian’s Well near Dunnottar is known through place-name, antiquarian and Historic Environment Record evidence, including a May Day pin-offering custom. Kinneff’s St Mungo’s Well is much thinner: recorded as a lost or untraced medieval well, but important because it shows how far the saintly map of the Mearns once extended.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://portal.historicenvironment.scot/apex/f?p=1505%3A300%3A%3A%3A%3A%3AVIEWTYPE%2CVIEWREF%3Adesignation%2CSM9723" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: portal.historicenvironment.scot">[historicenvironment.scot]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">portal.historicenvironment.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Historic Environment ScotlandSt. Palladius&#x27; Chapel (SM9723)...</span></span></span>

## Why wells mattered in the Mearns

The Mearns, or historic Kincardineshire, is a county of old parishes, coastal tracks, burn valleys and upland crossings. Its holy wells belong to that pattern. They are rarely isolated “mystical” curiosities. More often they sit beside churches, chapels, ruined ecclesiastical sites, manse grounds, den paths, old roads or settlement edges. That makes them useful landmarks for understanding how local people once joined everyday water use to religious memory.

A holy well could serve several roles at once. It might be a practical spring; a devotional stop; a healing place; a memory of a saint; or a local name that survived long after formal Catholic practice was suppressed after the Reformation. In Fordoun, the well, chapel, relics and annual fair created a whole sacred cluster. In Fetteresso and Stonehaven, the St Kieran tradition links a saintly dedication to a mineral spring reputed for health-giving properties. At Dunnottar, St Ninian’s Well and Den sit in the shadow of one of the county’s great legendary landscapes, showing that saints’ traditions attached not only to quiet inland kirkyards but also to the dramatic cliff-edge world around Stonehaven.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.aberdeenshire.gov.uk/leisure-sport-and-culture/archaeology/projects/historic-kirkyards/st-palladiuss-chapel-fordoun-parish-kirkyard-auchenblae/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: aberdeenshire.gov.uk">[aberdeenshire.gov.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">aberdeenshire.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Aberdeenshire Council St Palladius&#x27;s Chapel</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Fordoun Parish Kirkyard, Auchenblae - Aberdeenshire Council...</span></span></span>

This is why the wells matter for Kincardineshire folklore. They show belief becoming local geography. Instead of a single tale with one hero and one plot, the tradition survives as a network of names: a well, a chapel, a den, a fair, a wood, a churchyard, a lost spring. The story is carried by the map as much as by narrative.

## St Kieran, St Ninian and Kentigern wells

The best-known healing-well example in the Stonehaven area is St Kieran’s Well in Mineralwell Park. Trove, drawing on earlier Ordnance Survey and RCAHMS records, describes it as a disused drinking fountain beneath the Glenury railway viaduct. The granite fountain bears the inscription “St Kieran’s Well. Erected by Public Subscription AD. 1860”. The older spring was described as chalybeate, meaning iron-bearing, and was “for many years famous for its health-giving properties”; according to Morris and Morris, the fountain was built on the site of the old holy well, but the spring dried up when its supply was cut and could not be found again.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.trove.scot/place/36895" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: trove.scot">[Trove.scot]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">trove.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Stonehaven, St Kieran&#x27;s Well &#124; Place &#124; trove.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Stonehaven, St Kieran&#x27;s Well &#124; Place &#124; trove.scot</span></span></span>

That makes St Kieran’s Well a useful warning against romanticising holy wells as unchanged survivals. What a visitor sees now is not a medieval spring in continuous devotional use, but a Victorian public monument to an older spring tradition. Even the saintly attribution has caution attached. Trove notes that the only reason given for linking the fountain with St Ciaran or St Kieran was the presence of St Ciaran’s Church about a mile away. In folklore terms, that does not make the tradition worthless; it shows how local memory, nearby church dedication, mineral-water reputation and nineteenth-century civic improvement became layered together.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.trove.scot/place/36895" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: trove.scot">[Trove.scot]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">trove.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Stonehaven, St Kieran&#x27;s Well &#124; Place &#124; trove.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Stonehaven, St Kieran&#x27;s Well &#124; Place &#124; trove.scot</span></span></span>

Fetteresso gives the St Kieran tradition a stronger ecclesiastical setting. The Saints in Scottish Place-Names project records Fetteresso parish in Kincardineshire as containing St Caran’s Church, St Kiaran’s Well, Our Lady’s Well and St Mary’s Church at Cowie, with nearby saintly sites including St Bridget’s Church at Dunnottar and St Ninian’s Well and Den.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://saintsplaces.gla.ac.uk/place.php?id=860" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: saintsplaces.gla.ac.uk">[Saints in Scottish Place-Names]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">saintsplaces.gla.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on gla.ac.uk.</span></span></span> This cluster matters because it suggests that the Stonehaven-Fetteresso area was not defined by one well alone, but by a small sacred geography in which different dedications sat within walking distance of one another.

St Ninian’s Well near Dunnottar is more fragmentary but more folkloric. Aberdeenshire Council’s Historic Environment Record records the chapel, well and den as associated sites, while also noting disagreement among earlier writers about the chapel’s exact position. The important local detail is the custom: pins were dropped into St Ninian’s Well on May Day.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://her.aberdeenshire.gov.uk/Monument/MAB62866/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: her.aberdeenshire.gov.uk">[her.aberdeenshire.gov.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">her.aberdeenshire.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on aberdeenshire.gov.uk.</span></span></span> That is a classic well-offering pattern, modest rather than spectacular. A pin is a tiny object, but as a votive gift it turns a water source into a place of exchange: the visitor leaves something behind in hope, gratitude or ritual observance.

Kentigern, better known in Scotland as St Mungo, appears in the Mearns at Kinneff in a much fainter form. The Aberdeenshire Historic Environment Record lists St Mungo’s Well as a medieval well “said to be within the bounds of Kinneff Parish”, but adds that there is “no trace and no further information”.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://her.aberdeenshire.gov.uk/Monument/MAB62738/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: her.aberdeenshire.gov.uk">[her.aberdeenshire.gov.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">her.aberdeenshire.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on aberdeenshire.gov.uk.</span></span></span> A nineteenth-century folklore study of Scottish lochs and springs gives a little more colour, saying that one of the wells sacred to Kentigern north of the Tweed was in Kincardineshire at Kinneff, locally known as “Kenty’s Well”.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/files/56034/56034-h/56034-h.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Project Gutenberg Folklore of Scottish Lochs and Springs</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Project Gutenberg Folklore of Scottish Lochs and Springs</span></span></span> The result is not a full legend, but a valuable survival of naming: a lost well still holding the outline of a saintly association.

## Fordoun and the Palladius tradition

Fordoun is the strongest case in the Mearns because its holy-well tradition is tied to a documented pilgrimage centre. St Palladius’s Chapel at Auchenblae, in Fordoun parish, is a scheduled monument. Historic Environment Scotland describes it as the remains of a late medieval church dedicated to St Palladius, “an early christian martyr in the Mearns”, and says it appears to have been a centre of pilgrimage. Its unusual features include a crypt with descending stairs and a tomb recess in the east gable, both possibly connected with pilgrimage use.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://portal.historicenvironment.scot/apex/f?p=1505%3A300%3A%3A%3A%3A%3AVIEWTYPE%2CVIEWREF%3Adesignation%2CSM9723" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: portal.historicenvironment.scot">[Historic Environment Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">portal.historicenvironment.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Historic Environment ScotlandSt. Palladius&#x27; Chapel (SM9723)...</span></span></span>

Aberdeenshire Council’s Historic Kirkyards material presents the local tradition in fuller form. Palladius is described as the patron saint of the parish and as an early missionary to northern Scotland, believed to have been ordained by Pope Celestine in 430 and to have come to the Mearns after preaching in Ireland. His chapel, standing above the Luther Water, is called the Mother Church of the Mearns. The present ruined chapel probably stands on the site of a fifth-century cell, while the oldest part of the visible structure is associated with a dedication by the Bishop of St Andrews in 1244.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.aberdeenshire.gov.uk/leisure-sport-and-culture/archaeology/projects/historic-kirkyards/st-palladiuss-chapel-fordoun-parish-kirkyard-auchenblae/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: aberdeenshire.gov.uk">[Aberdeenshire Council]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">aberdeenshire.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Aberdeenshire Council St Palladius&#x27;s Chapel</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Fordoun Parish Kirkyard, Auchenblae - Aberdeenshire Council...</span></span></span>

The pilgrimage element is not a modern tourist flourish. The council account says the crypt may have preserved Palladius’s relics, that pilgrims came “from far and wide” to venerate them, and that Archbishop Shevez of St Andrews came on pilgrimage in the early sixteenth century, restoring the chapel and donating a new silver shrine for the bones.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.aberdeenshire.gov.uk/leisure-sport-and-culture/archaeology/projects/historic-kirkyards/st-palladiuss-chapel-fordoun-parish-kirkyard-auchenblae/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: aberdeenshire.gov.uk">[Aberdeenshire Council]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">aberdeenshire.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Aberdeenshire Council St Palladius&#x27;s Chapel</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Fordoun Parish Kirkyard, Auchenblae - Aberdeenshire Council...</span></span></span> Historic Environment Scotland is more cautious in tone, but reaches the same general conclusion: the chapel’s design is unusual, the crypt may relate to pilgrimage, and the site is important for studying the veneration of saints and medieval belief.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://portal.historicenvironment.scot/apex/f?p=1505%3A300%3A%3A%3A%3A%3AVIEWTYPE%2CVIEWREF%3Adesignation%2CSM9723" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: portal.historicenvironment.scot">[Historic Environment Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">portal.historicenvironment.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Historic Environment ScotlandSt. Palladius&#x27; Chapel (SM9723)...</span></span></span>

The well belongs to that wider sacred complex. Saints in Scottish Place-Names records Fordoun parish as containing St Palladius’s Chapel, St Palladius’s Well, Paldy Fair and Paldyfair Wood, all in Kincardineshire and within the medieval diocese of St Andrews.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://saintsplaces.gla.ac.uk/place.php?id=861" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: saintsplaces.gla.ac.uk">[Saints in Scottish Place-Names]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">saintsplaces.gla.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on gla.ac.uk.</span></span></span> The old fair is especially important. It shows how saintly devotion could spill into social and economic life. A well and chapel might begin as sacred places, but a fair turns the saint’s name into a date, a gathering and a local institution.

A nineteenth-century study of Scottish wells preserves the same link in plain terms: a spring in Fordoun parish was locally known as Paldy’s Well, and an annual market was called Paldy’s or Paddy’s Fair. The same passage connects the chapel with Palladius’s relics and with Terrananus, whose name is preserved in Banchory-Ternan and who is associated with other north-east dedications.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/files/56034/56034-h/56034-h.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Project Gutenberg Folklore of Scottish Lochs and Springs</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Project Gutenberg Folklore of Scottish Lochs and Springs</span></span></span> This does not mean every detail of the saint’s life can be treated as secure history. It means Fordoun kept a unusually dense bundle of saint-name, relic tradition, well, chapel and fair.

## Fetteresso, healing and the Stonehaven edge

Fetteresso and Stonehaven show a different mechanism from Fordoun. Here the tradition is less about a major relic shrine and more about the way a saint’s name, a church dedication and a healing spring could reinforce one another at the edge of a growing county town.

St Ciaran’s or St Kieran’s Old Church at Fetteresso was the religious anchor. Aberdeenshire Council notes that when Stonehaven became the county town of Kincardineshire in 1600, population growth eventually made the old kirk inconvenient for the congregation, leading to the opening of the New Kirk on Bath Street in 1813. St Ciaran’s then fell derelict, though it was preserved by the Duffs of Fetteresso as their burial ground, and objects including a font and carved panel were later recorded or recovered.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.aberdeenshire.gov.uk/leisure-sport-and-culture/archaeology/projects/historic-kirkyards/st-ciarans-kirkyard-fetteresso/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: aberdeenshire.gov.uk">[Aberdeenshire Council]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">aberdeenshire.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">st ciarans kirkyard fetteresso</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">st ciarans kirkyard fetteresso</span></span></span>

The well in Mineralwell Park belongs to this same zone of memory. Its nineteenth-century fountain made the old spring visible in a new civic form. The name Mineralwell itself points to a period when water was valued not only as holy but as health-giving in a more medical or spa-like sense. The recorded chalybeate character of the spring helps explain why the place could retain a reputation even when the older devotional framework had weakened.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.trove.scot/place/36895" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: trove.scot">[Trove.scot]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">trove.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Stonehaven, St Kieran&#x27;s Well &#124; Place &#124; trove.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Stonehaven, St Kieran&#x27;s Well &#124; Place &#124; trove.scot</span></span></span>

This is one of the most interesting shifts in Mearns well-lore. The same water source could be read in several ways across time: as a holy well linked to a saint, as a healing spring, as a public fountain, and finally as a heritage feature whose water no longer runs. The tradition did not simply disappear after the Reformation or with modernisation. It changed form, moving from ritual use to local memory, inscription and preservation.

Fetteresso parish also contained Our Lady’s Well and lay close to St Mary’s Church at Cowie, according to Saints in Scottish Place-Names.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://saintsplaces.gla.ac.uk/place.php?id=860" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: saintsplaces.gla.ac.uk">[Saints in Scottish Place-Names]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">saintsplaces.gla.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on gla.ac.uk.</span></span></span> That Marian layer matters because holy wells in Scotland were not only attached to missionary saints. Some were tied to the Virgin Mary, to parish churches, or to small ecclesiastical sites whose stories have thinned over time. Around Stonehaven, the result is a compact sacred landscape: Fetteresso, Cowie, Dunnottar and Mineralwell Park all preserve pieces of a once fuller devotional map.

## Routes, fairs and walking memory

The “saintly routes” of the Mearns should not be imagined as one clearly signposted medieval pilgrimage trail surviving unchanged. The evidence points instead to a mesh of local movement: parish paths to wells, routes to chapels, journeys to relics, fair gatherings, and older roads linking Stonehaven, Dunnottar, Cowie and inland settlements.

Fordoun gives the clearest pilgrimage route in miniature. People travelled to St Palladius’s relics; the chapel’s crypt may have been designed for such devotion; and Paldy Fair extended the saint’s name into an annual gathering.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://portal.historicenvironment.scot/apex/f?p=1505%3A300%3A%3A%3A%3A%3AVIEWTYPE%2CVIEWREF%3Adesignation%2CSM9723" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: portal.historicenvironment.scot">[historicenvironment.scot]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">portal.historicenvironment.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Historic Environment ScotlandSt. Palladius&#x27; Chapel (SM9723)...</span></span></span> This kind of pilgrimage was not always a long-distance journey in the modern sense. For many people it may have been a parish or regional movement: walking from nearby farms, villages or churches to a recognised holy place on a feast day or fair day.

The Stonehaven coast adds the roadscape. The old Causey Mounth route is recorded as a medieval route connecting Stonehaven’s coastal area with the Dee crossing near Aberdeen, associated with Cowie Castle and Dunnottar Castle as controlling points for movement.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://wikishire.co.uk/wiki/Cowie_Castle" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wikishire.co.uk">[Wikishire]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wikishire.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">wie Castle</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">wie Castle</span></span></span> Although this was primarily a practical and strategic route rather than a dedicated pilgrimage trail, it helps explain why saintly sites near Stonehaven mattered. Wells and chapels beside routes are remembered because people passed them, used them, named them and retold them.

Dunnottar’s St Ninian sites fit this moving landscape especially well. The recorded Den runs down towards the promontory on which Dunnottar Castle stands, and the well, chapel and den are discussed together in the Historic Environment Record.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://saintsplaces.gla.ac.uk/place.php?id=1319556615" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: saintsplaces.gla.ac.uk">[Saints in Scottish Place-Names]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">saintsplaces.gla.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Saints in Scottish Place-Names St Ninian&#x27;s Den, Dunnottar</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Saints in Scottish Place-Names St Ninian&#x27;s Den, Dunnottar</span></span></span> A den is a route as well as a place: a cleft, descent or passage in the land. When it carries a saint’s name, it turns movement through the landscape into saintly memory.

May Day pin-dropping at St Ninian’s Well also links the sacred route to the calendar. The visitor did not merely know about the well; they went there at a particular time and performed a small act.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://her.aberdeenshire.gov.uk/Monument/MAB62866/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: her.aberdeenshire.gov.uk">[her.aberdeenshire.gov.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">her.aberdeenshire.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on aberdeenshire.gov.uk.</span></span></span> In folklore terms, that is crucial. A holy well survives most strongly when it is not only named, but visited.

## How strong is the evidence?

The Mearns holy wells are best understood as a graded tradition, not a single body of equally secure facts.

**Strongest evidence: Fordoun.** St Palladius’s Chapel is protected as a scheduled monument, and official heritage records identify it as a likely centre of pilgrimage. The physical fabric, crypt, tomb recess, chapel dedication, relic tradition, well-name and fair-name all point in the same direction. Some details of Palladius’s biography remain uncertain, but the medieval importance of the site is well supported.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://portal.historicenvironment.scot/apex/f?p=1505%3A300%3A%3A%3A%3A%3AVIEWTYPE%2CVIEWREF%3Adesignation%2CSM9723" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: portal.historicenvironment.scot">[Historic Environment Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">portal.historicenvironment.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Historic Environment ScotlandSt. Palladius&#x27; Chapel (SM9723)...</span></span></span>

**Moderate evidence: St Kieran at Stonehaven and Fetteresso.** The Stonehaven fountain is physically recorded and dated to 1860, and the healing reputation of the chalybeate spring is preserved in heritage records. The older holy well is reported as lost, and the specific saintly attribution is treated cautiously by Trove, which notes that the link to St Ciaran or St Kieran appears to rest on the nearby church dedication.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.trove.scot/place/36895" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: trove.scot">[Trove.scot]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">trove.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Stonehaven, St Kieran&#x27;s Well &#124; Place &#124; trove.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Stonehaven, St Kieran&#x27;s Well &#124; Place &#124; trove.scot</span></span></span>

**Folklorically valuable but fragmentary: St Ninian at Dunnottar.** The well, chapel and den are recorded in the Historic Environment Record, with earlier antiquarian sources cited and a May Day pin-offering custom preserved. There is, however, uncertainty over the exact siting of some associated features, and the surviving evidence is more place-name and record based than architectural.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://her.aberdeenshire.gov.uk/Monument/MAB62866/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: her.aberdeenshire.gov.uk">[her.aberdeenshire.gov.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">her.aberdeenshire.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on aberdeenshire.gov.uk.</span></span></span>

**Thinnest evidence: St Mungo or Kentigern at Kinneff.** The well is recorded as medieval and within Kinneff parish, but no trace or further information is known in the Aberdeenshire record. The older folklore source naming “Kenty’s Well” adds interest, but the site is best treated as a lost well tradition rather than a visitable or richly documented holy place.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://her.aberdeenshire.gov.uk/Monument/MAB62738/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: her.aberdeenshire.gov.uk">[her.aberdeenshire.gov.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">her.aberdeenshire.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on aberdeenshire.gov.uk.</span></span></span>

This unevenness should not be seen as failure. It is how local folklore often survives. Some traditions leave ruins and scheduled monuments; others leave a fountain inscription; others leave a line in an antiquarian book or an old Ordnance Survey record. The reader’s task is to notice the difference rather than flatten everything into either “true history” or “mere legend”.

## Why the wells still shape Kincardineshire identity

Holy wells give Kincardineshire a quieter sacred geography than the dramatic cliff stories of Dunnottar or the fiery spectacle of Stonehaven’s winter customs. They are smaller, but they reach deeper into parish life. They connect the Mearns to early Christianity, medieval pilgrimage, healing belief, fairs, old roads and the naming habits of ordinary people.

They also show why historic county framing still matters. Modern visitors may encounter these places under Aberdeenshire tourism, council or heritage records, but the older sources repeatedly speak in terms of Kincardineshire, the Mearns, Fordoun, Fetteresso, Dunnottar and Kinneff. Saints in Scottish Place-Names explicitly places Fordoun and Fetteresso within Kincardineshire and the medieval diocese of St Andrews, preserving the older geography that shaped the traditions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://saintsplaces.gla.ac.uk/place.php?id=861" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: saintsplaces.gla.ac.uk">[Saints in Scottish Place-Names]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">saintsplaces.gla.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on gla.ac.uk.</span></span></span>

For folklore, the most memorable point is that these wells are mechanisms of belonging. St Palladius makes Fordoun feel like a mother church and pilgrimage centre. St Kieran makes a Stonehaven spring into a healing place and civic monument. St Ninian turns a Dunnottar well into a May Day offering site. St Mungo at Kinneff survives as a ghost of a dedication: faint, lost, but still part of the county’s saintly map.

The Mearns holy wells therefore ask to be read as a family of places rather than as isolated curiosities. Some are visible, some lost; some well documented, some barely traceable; some devotional, some medicinal, some folded into fairs and routeways. Together they show how water carried memory through Kincardineshire: not as proof of miracles, but as a durable local language for holiness, healing and place.
