## Where Huntingdonshire Folklore Belongs

For this project, Huntingdonshire is best understood as the historic county, not simply as the modern district name. Wikishire describes the County of Huntingdon as a small shire between the Midlands and East Anglia, with Huntingdon as county town, St Neots as its biggest town, and the Great Ouse running through St Neots, Huntingdon and St Ives before leaving the county at Earith.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://wikishire.co.uk/wiki/Huntingdonshire" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wikishire.co.uk">[Wikishire]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wikishire.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Wikishire Huntingdonshire</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wikishire Huntingdonshire</span></span></span>



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/Huntingdonshire_d54801-overview.webp" | relative_url }}" alt="Overview image for Where Huntingdonshire Keeps Its Strange..." loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

Modern local government can confuse the picture. Modern Cambridgeshire was formed in 1974 by bringing together Cambridgeshire and the Isle of Ely with Huntingdon and Peterborough; the latter covered historic Huntingdonshire and the Soke of Peterborough, historically part of Northamptonshire.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambridgeshirelieutenancy.org.uk/the-county-of-cambridgeshire/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridgeshirelieutenancy.org.uk">[Cambridgeshire Lieutenancy]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridgeshirelieutenancy.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cambridgeshirelieutenancy.org.uk.</span></span></span> Folklore, however, rarely follows council lines neatly. Black-dog stories, fen lights, witchcraft accusations, river customs and parish memories cross into Cambridgeshire, Northamptonshire, Bedfordshire, Norfolk and Suffolk. The centre of gravity here remains Huntingdonshire, but the motifs often belong to wider East Anglian and fenland storytelling.

This geography helps explain the county’s folklore texture. Huntingdonshire has low-lying fen country to the north, the Great Ouse running through its main towns, old routes such as the Great North Road and Ermine Street, and a historic mixture of market towns, abbey lands, farming villages and river crossings. Those are exactly the kinds of places where English local folklore tends to gather: bridges, marshes, lonely roads, parish boundaries, wells, churchyards and old legal memory.

## The Witches of Warboys: Huntingdonshire’s Most Famous Legend

The best-known Huntingdonshire supernatural tradition is the Warboys witch case of 1589–1593. It began when the daughters of Robert Throckmorton of Warboys suffered fits and accused Alice Samuel, an elderly local woman, of bewitching them. The case widened to include Alice’s husband John and daughter Agnes, and after the death of Lady Cromwell, wife of Sir Henry Cromwell, the Samuels were tried at Huntingdon in 1593 and hanged. Cambridge University Press’s account notes that the family were accused of bewitching the Throckmorton daughters and causing Lady Cromwell’s death, with “familiars” appearing in the story as named demon agents.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cambridgeblog.org/2015/10/a-warning-to-witches-this-halloween/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridgeblog.org">[Fifteen Eighty Four]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridgeblog.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Fifteen Eighty Four A Warning to Witches</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Fifteen Eighty Four A Warning to Witches</span></span></span>

This is not just a ghost story. It is a documented early modern witch trial, preserved through a sensational 1593 pamphlet, later scholarship and local memory. The importance of the case lies partly in its social reach: the accusation touched the gentry Throckmortons, the Cromwell family, Huntingdon assizes, and the wider religious culture of the county. After the executions, Sir Henry Cromwell used confiscated property to fund an annual sermon at All Saints’ Church in Huntingdon warning against witchcraft; the sermons continued into the early nineteenth century, long after educated opinion had shifted towards scepticism.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cambridgeblog.org/2015/10/a-warning-to-witches-this-halloween/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridgeblog.org">[Fifteen Eighty Four]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridgeblog.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Fifteen Eighty Four A Warning to Witches</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Fifteen Eighty Four A Warning to Witches</span></span></span>

For folklore readers, Warboys is a powerful example of how a local belief becomes many things at once: a legal case, a religious warning, a printed horror story, a county legend and a modern retelling. It should not be presented as proof that witchcraft happened, but as evidence that people in late Elizabethan Huntingdonshire could interpret illness, misfortune and conflict through witchcraft belief. Its afterlife is just as important as the trial itself. Warboys became one of the stories by which Huntingdonshire remembered fear, authority and village suspicion.

## Ann Izzard and the Survival of Witch Belief

Warboys was not the end of Huntingdonshire witchcraft belief. In 1808, more than two centuries later, Ann Izzard of Great Paxton was attacked by neighbours who believed she was a witch. A scholarly account of the case describes two indictments from Huntingdonshire charging residents of Great Paxton with attacking a man and his wife on two successive nights; the same study notes that the case survived in local tradition and can be read as a late flowering of vigorous local witchcraft belief.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.academia.edu/1963856/Witchcraft_Persecutions_in_the_Post_Craze_Era_The_Case_of_Ann_Izzard_of_Great_Paxton_1808" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: academia.edu">[Academia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">academia.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on academia.edu.</span></span></span>

The Ann Izzard case matters because it shows the difference between law and belief. By 1808, witchcraft was no longer being prosecuted in the old early modern way, and the authorities acted against the attackers rather than against the supposed witch. Yet the pattern of belief was recognisable: illness or misfortune needed an explanation, local suspicion settled on a vulnerable person, and charms or informal tests were used to identify the imagined source of harm.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.academia.edu/1963856/Witchcraft_Persecutions_in_the_Post_Craze_Era_The_Case_of_Ann_Izzard_of_Great_Paxton_1808" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: academia.edu">[Academia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">academia.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on academia.edu.</span></span></span>

This makes Great Paxton one of the most useful Huntingdonshire cases for modern readers. It warns against treating witchcraft traditions as quaint village colour. They could produce real violence, especially against socially exposed people. At the same time, the case belongs to folklore because it shows how old explanatory habits persisted in oral culture after the legal and intellectual climate had changed.

A related caution applies to Mary Hicks, the alleged Huntingdon witch of 1716. A pamphlet claimed that Mary Hicks and her nine-year-old daughter Elizabeth were tried and executed at Huntingdon for witchcraft, but modern historians have generally treated the story as a journalistic invention or spurious pamphlet tale.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://engole.info/whole-trial-and-examination-of-mrs-mary-hicks-and-her-daughter-elizabeth/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: engole.info">[Engole]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">engole.info</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Whole trial and examination of Mrs. Mary Hicks and her daughter Elizabeth</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Whole trial and examination of Mrs. Mary Hicks and her daughter Elizabeth</span></span></span> This is exactly the kind of distinction a good Huntingdonshire folklore page needs to make: Warboys is a historically attested witch trial; Ann Izzard is a documented assault rooted in witch belief; Mary Hicks is better handled as doubtful print folklore rather than solid county history.

## Black Shuck, Fen Lights and the Haunted Lowlands

Huntingdonshire also shares in the wider East Anglian world of ghostly black dogs and fenland lights. The Huntingdonshire History Festival summarises Black Shuck as a monstrous black dog whose sight may bring death or ill luck, and notes that such stories are common across East Anglia, including Huntingdonshire. It also records the dual character of the figure: sometimes a death omen, sometimes a strange helper that leads lost travellers back from the fens.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://huntshistoryfest.wordpress.com/2019/10/27/fantastical-folk-tales/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: huntshistoryfest.wordpress.com">[Huntingdonshire History Festival]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">huntshistoryfest.wordpress.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wordpress.com.</span></span></span>

The most famous Black Shuck story belongs to Bungay and Blythburgh in Suffolk, not Huntingdonshire, but the motif travels easily across the flat, watery eastern counties. That matters because Huntingdonshire’s north-eastern fen edge is part of the same imaginative landscape: dark drove roads, marsh lights, isolated churches, sudden storms and the danger of losing one’s way.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://huntshistoryfest.wordpress.com/2019/10/27/fantastical-folk-tales/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: huntshistoryfest.wordpress.com">[Huntingdonshire History Festival]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">huntshistoryfest.wordpress.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wordpress.com.</span></span></span>

Will-o’-the-wisp traditions fit the county even more naturally. The History Festival contrasts Huntingdonshire wisps as passive false beacons with Norfolk’s more active “Lantern Man”, a figure said to chase or punish night travellers.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://huntshistoryfest.wordpress.com/2019/10/27/fantastical-folk-tales/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: huntshistoryfest.wordpress.com">[Huntingdonshire History Festival]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">huntshistoryfest.wordpress.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wordpress.com.</span></span></span> In plain terms, these are stories about dangerous light in wet country. Whether explained today through gases, reflections, lamps, memory or storytelling, the folklore speaks to a real landscape anxiety: in fenland and river country, a light in the dark is not always a safe guide.

## Holy Wells and Healing Water at Hail Weston

Huntingdonshire has fewer famous fairy hills or dragon sites than some counties, but its well lore is unusually strong at Hail Weston, near St Neots. The village’s mineral springs were credited with healing properties over several centuries. A holy-well survey cites Thomas Fuller’s seventeenth-century account of two nearby “fountainlets”: one sweet and thought good for dimness of sight, the other salty and valued against scab and leprosy. The same source notes that in 1770 a mineral spring at Hail Weston was still described as useful for eye disorders and skin eruptions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://insearchofholywellsandhealingsprings.wordpress.com/category/huntingdonshire/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: insearchofholywellsandhealingsprings.wordpress.com">[holyandhealingwells]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">insearchofholywellsandhealingsprings.wordpress.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">holyandhealingwells Huntingdonshire &#124; holyandhealingwells</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">holyandhealingwells Huntingdonshire &#124; holyandhealingwells</span></span></span>

This is classic medicinal-well folklore: not necessarily a saint’s miracle cult, but a blend of observed mineral water, local reputation, printed description and repeated visitation. The Hail Weston springs were also linked by later accounts to Roman remains nearby and to writers including Holinshed, Michael Drayton, Fuller and Camden.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://insearchofholywellsandhealingsprings.wordpress.com/category/huntingdonshire/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: insearchofholywellsandhealingsprings.wordpress.com">[holyandhealingwells]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">insearchofholywellsandhealingsprings.wordpress.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">holyandhealingwells Huntingdonshire &#124; holyandhealingwells</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">holyandhealingwells Huntingdonshire &#124; holyandhealingwells</span></span></span>

For readers, the point is not that the water should be treated as medically reliable today. The value is cultural. Hail Weston shows how a Huntingdonshire village could become known through healing reputation, landscape memory and old natural philosophy. Its folklore sits at the boundary between medicine, wonder and local pride.

## May Garlands, Bread Doles and Boundary Memory

Huntingdonshire’s folklore is not only supernatural. Some of its richest material is customary: May celebrations, charity doles and civic rituals that turned the calendar and the parish boundary into public performance.

A nineteenth-century account of Glatton May Day describes the election of a Queen of the May and the making of a large pyramidal garland decorated with flowers, greenery, ribbons, fabric and a doll representing Flora. Another account records Mayers singing at Denton and Caldecote in 1865, while Warboys had a May Day custom allowing some poorer parishioners to gather bundles of sticks from Warboys Wood, possibly connected with older May-morning visits to woods and dew.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://traditionalcustomsandceremonies.wordpress.com/category/huntingdonshire/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: traditionalcustomsandceremonies.wordpress.com">[Traditions &amp; Ceremonies]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">traditionalcustomsandceremonies.wordpress.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wordpress.com.</span></span></span>

St Ives has a different kind of custom in Langley Bread. Robert Langley’s will of 1656 charged lands in St Ives with annual payments for poor widows, fatherless children, churchwardens and bellringers; the modern tradition continued as an Epiphany-linked distribution of food to poor widows and widowers.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://traditionalcustomsandceremonies.wordpress.com/category/huntingdonshire/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: traditionalcustomsandceremonies.wordpress.com">[Traditions &amp; Ceremonies]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">traditionalcustomsandceremonies.wordpress.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wordpress.com.</span></span></span> This is not a legend of ghosts or fairies, but it is absolutely part of folk culture: a remembered obligation, enacted yearly, linking charity, local identity and ritual timing.

One of the strangest Huntingdon customs was a civic boundary ceremony involving the freemen of the borough, an ox skull carried on poles, boundary holes, boys placed in the holes and struck with a spade, and refreshments at Blackstone Leys. The account interprets it as a confused form of beating the bounds, with the blows and gifts helping children remember the boundary.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://traditionalcustomsandceremonies.wordpress.com/category/huntingdonshire/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: traditionalcustomsandceremonies.wordpress.com">[Traditions &amp; Ceremonies]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">traditionalcustomsandceremonies.wordpress.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wordpress.com.</span></span></span> It is a vivid reminder that local custom can look bizarre once separated from its original legal and ritual purpose.

## Old Roads, Barrows and the Mythic Landscape

Huntingdonshire’s mythic landscape is quieter than counties famous for stone circles or giant-built hills, but it has important ancient routes and burial mounds. Historic England records Roman barrows near Ermine Street at The Stukeleys, including a substantial earthwork east of St Bartholomew’s Church. Roman barrows are rare nationally, with fewer than 150 recorded examples, mostly in lowland England and concentrated in East Anglia; the Stukeleys example stands beside the Roman road and survives as an earthwork with a ditch.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://historicengland.org.uk/listing/the-list/list-entry/1018973" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: historicengland.org.uk">[Historic England]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">historicengland.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on historicengland.org.uk.</span></span></span>

There is a useful folklore lesson here. Not every ancient mound has a famous fairy or giant legend attached to it, and forcing one onto the landscape would be misleading. But barrows, Roman roads and old church sites create the conditions in which legend often grows. They are visible puzzles in the landscape. People ask who made them, who lies beneath them, why they stand where they do, and whether the old road is safe after dark.

The Victoria County History series for Huntingdonshire remains one of the key foundations for understanding this deeper local landscape, covering religious houses such as Ramsey Abbey, St Neots Priory, St Ives Priory, Hinchingbrooke and Sawtry, as well as parish histories and the Middle Level of the Fens.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.british-history.ac.uk/series/victoria-county-history-huntingdonshire" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: british-history.ac.uk">[British History Online]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">british-history.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on british-history.ac.uk.</span></span></span> For folklore, such works matter because abbeys, priories, old parish boundaries and fen drainage often form the historical skeleton beneath later legend.

## Cromwell’s County and Modern Retelling

Huntingdonshire is often remembered as Cromwell’s county, and that identity shapes how its folklore is retold. Oliver Cromwell was born in Huntingdon in 1599, and the Cromwell Museum presents itself as holding the best public collection relating to his life and times.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.huntingdontown.gov.uk/cromwell-museum/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: huntingdontown.gov.uk">[Huntingdon Town Council]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">huntingdontown.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on huntingdontown.gov.uk.</span></span></span> The Cromwell connection also intersects with folklore through Warboys: Lady Cromwell’s death became central to the accusations against the Samuels, and Sir Henry Cromwell’s sermon endowment helped turn the case into a long-lasting public warning.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cambridgeblog.org/2015/10/a-warning-to-witches-this-halloween/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridgeblog.org">[Fifteen Eighty Four]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridgeblog.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Fifteen Eighty Four A Warning to Witches</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Fifteen Eighty Four A Warning to Witches</span></span></span>

Modern Huntingdonshire retellings often come through local museums, history festivals, blogs, guided walks, county societies and reprints of antiquarian works. W. H. Bernard Saunders’s 1888 *Legends and Traditions of Huntingdonshire* remains a key antiquarian collection, but it needs careful reading: antiquarian writers preserved valuable material, yet they also worked with the assumptions, moral tone and source limits of their time.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.abebooks.com/first-edition/Legends-Traditions-Huntingdonshire-Saunders-W.H-Bernard/32436037989/bd" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: abebooks.com">[AbeBooks]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">abebooks.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Abe Books Legends and Traditions of Huntingdonshire (Hardback</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Abe Books Legends and Traditions of Huntingdonshire (Hardback</span></span></span>

This is why Huntingdonshire folklore is best presented as layered. Some material is early printed narrative, such as the Warboys pamphlet. Some is documented social history, such as Ann Izzard. Some is regional motif, such as Black Shuck and fen lights. Some is custom, such as May garlands and Langley Bread. Some is doubtful or literary, such as Mary Hicks. The county’s folklore becomes more interesting, not less, when these layers are kept distinct.

## What Huntingdonshire Folklore Is Really About

Huntingdonshire’s folklore is about fear, memory and belonging in a small historic county with a large imaginative reach. Its witch stories show how suspicion could attach to women, illness and village tension. Its fenland stories turn light, darkness, dogs and water into warnings about travel and danger. Its wells show how healing reputation can cling to a place for centuries. Its May customs and charity doles show how communities used ritual to mark season, poverty, obligation and boundary.

The county may not have one single monster as famous as a national legend, but it has something more revealing: a set of traditions rooted in real places. Warboys, Great Paxton, Hail Weston, St Ives, Glatton, Denton, Caldecote, Warboys Wood, Huntingdon and the Stukeleys each show a different way folklore enters local life. The result is a county folklore shaped less by fantasy kingdoms than by roads, rivers, sermons, wells, parish customs and the long afterlife of stories people once took very seriously.
