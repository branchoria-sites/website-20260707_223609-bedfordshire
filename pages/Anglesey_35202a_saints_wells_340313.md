## Introduction

Holy wells on [Anglesey]({{ 'why-anglesey-feels-so-full-of-legends/' | relative_url }}) turn the island’s saint stories into something a person can still walk, touch and imagine. At Llanddwyn, St Dwynwen’s well made love and courtship part of sacred geography; at Penmon, St Seiriol’s well links healing water with an early monastic site; and at the Clorach wells near Llannerch-y-medd, the friendship of Cybi and Seiriol gives Anglesey one of its most memorable pilgrimage routes. These traditions matter because they are practical folklore: not only tales told in books, but beliefs attached to paths, springs, ruined churches, offerings, cures, signs and annual devotion.



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/Anglesey_35202a_saints_wells_340313-overview.webp" | relative_url }}" alt="Overview image for Saints Wells" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

The evidence is mixed in the best folklore sense. Some details belong to medieval cults and pilgrimage, some to later antiquarian record, and some to modern heritage walking. The result is not a single “ancient route” preserved unchanged, but a living Anglesey pattern: saints are remembered through movement across the island, and water becomes the point where prayer, healing, friendship and local identity meet.

## St Dwynwen and love at Llanddwyn

Llanddwyn Island, off the south-west coast of Anglesey near Newborough, is the clearest example of how a holy well can make a saint’s legend feel intimate. Dwynwen is remembered as the Welsh patron saint of lovers, and her story explains why people came to her not for victory in battle or protection at sea, but for help with the troubles of love. Cadw’s modern retelling describes her as fleeing to Llanddwyn after being forbidden to marry the man she loved, becoming a nun, and spending the rest of her life there; the site remains a popular pilgrimage destination associated with St Dwynwen’s Day on 25 January.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cadw.gov.wales/about-us/news/fall-love-waless-most-romantic-relics-celebration-st-dwynwens-day" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cadw.gov.wales">[Cadw]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cadw.gov.wales</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on gov.wales.</span></span></span>

The folklore of the well is wonderfully specific. Pilgrims were said to seek signs about love through the movement of sacred eels in the water. Cadw records the belief that a person would lay a handkerchief on the surface and a local wise woman would interpret the eels’ reaction. That small ritual is important because it shows the well functioning almost like a local oracle: not merely a pretty sacred spring, but a place where a private emotional question could be taken into the landscape and answered through signs.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cadw.gov.wales/about-us/news/fall-love-waless-most-romantic-relics-celebration-st-dwynwens-day" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cadw.gov.wales">[Cadw]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cadw.gov.wales</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on gov.wales.</span></span></span>

The island’s religious history is not just modern romance branding. Archaeological reporting on Llanddwyn describes a religious past of more than 1,000 years, with evidence for a small medieval Benedictine community probably established in the 12th century and centred on the lore of Dwynwen. The same research places the church dedicated to her about 60 metres north of the monastic settlement, probably in the 13th century, and notes that it became an important place of pilgrimage after her death.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archaeology.co.uk/articles/features/uncovering-a-lost-landscape.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archaeology.co.uk">[Current Archaeology]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archaeology.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Current Archaeology Uncovering a lost landscape</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Current Archaeology Uncovering a lost landscape</span></span></span>

That matters for folklore because it keeps the story grounded. Llanddwyn is not only “the romantic island”; it is a place where medieval devotion, later ruin, coastal walking and popular love-custom have layered over one another. The ruined church, the rough crossing from Newborough, the sea views and the well tradition all work together. A lover’s question becomes part of an older sacred landscape, while the landscape gives the story its staying power.

There is also a useful caution here. Dwynwen’s legend varies in detail, and modern versions often smooth it into a gentler Welsh Valentine story. The archaeological and heritage evidence supports Llanddwyn’s importance as a medieval pilgrimage place, but the exact age and continuity of every love-divination custom is harder to pin down. That uncertainty does not weaken the folklore; it helps explain how the island has carried different kinds of meaning at different moments: medieval shrine, ruined sacred site, romantic destination, and modern walking goal.

## Cybi and Seiriol across the island

If Llanddwyn is the island’s great love pilgrimage, Cybi and Seiriol give Anglesey its friendship pilgrimage. The modern Anglesey Saints Way, also called Llwybr Cybi a Seiriol, is presented by the British Pilgrimage Trust as a 92 km, 57-mile route that links St Cybi’s church at Holyhead in the west with St Seiriol’s foundation at Penmon in the east. The route is explicitly shaped around the story that the two sixth-century saints travelled from opposite ends of Anglesey to meet at the holy wells of Clorach.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.britishpilgrimage.org/portfolio/anglesey-saints-way-seiriol-cybi" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: britishpilgrimage.org">[British Pilgrimage Trust]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">britishpilgrimage.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on britishpilgrimage.org.</span></span></span>

The charm of the story lies in its geography. Cybi, walking from Holyhead, faced the rising sun in the morning and the setting sun in the evening, becoming “the Tanned”; Seiriol, walking from Penmon in the opposite direction, had the weaker sun and became “the Fair”. The British Pilgrimage Trust treats the tale as the symbolic centre of the route: two holy friends, two ends of the island, one meeting place in the middle.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.britishpilgrimage.org/portfolio/anglesey-saints-way-seiriol-cybi" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: britishpilgrimage.org">[British Pilgrimage Trust]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">britishpilgrimage.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on britishpilgrimage.org.</span></span></span>

The likely Clorach site is not just a vague “somewhere in the middle”. The Cult of Saints in Wales database identifies Ffynnon Gybi, south-west of Llannerch-y-medd, as probably the location of the “wells of Clorach” where local tradition says Cybi met Seiriol. It gives the site an Ordnance Survey grid reference and notes that it appears on early six-inch maps from the 1901 publication onwards, with a possible lost chapel nearby.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://seintiadur.saints.wales/en/chwilio.php?func=safle&amp;siteid=1983" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: seintiadur.saints.wales">[The Cult of Saints in Wales]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">seintiadur.saints.wales</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Ffynnon Gybi, Llannerch-y-medd, Anglesey: The Cult of Saints in Wales...</span></span></span>

At either end of the route, the saints are attached to major historic sites. St Cybi’s Church at Holyhead stands inside the walls of the Roman fort of Caer Gybi. Coflein records the church as a medieval and post-medieval site, noting that it was referred to as a Welsh monastic community in 1282 and later became a college before dissolution in 1548. It also records that the church stands within the walls of a Roman stronghold, with a smaller church, Eglwys-y-Bedd, in the southern part of the enclosure.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://coflein.gov.uk/en/sites/27500" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: coflein.gov.uk">[Coflein]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">coflein.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Coflein English – Coflein</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Coflein English – Coflein</span></span></span> Cadw separately describes the Roman fort walls as extremely well preserved, standing up to 13 feet, or 4 metres, high in places.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cadw.gov.wales/visit/places-to-visit/caer-gybi-roman-fort" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cadw.gov.wales">[Cadw]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cadw.gov.wales</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Cadw Caer Gybi Roman Fort &#124; Cadw</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cadw Caer Gybi Roman Fort &#124; Cadw</span></span></span>

Penmon, at the eastern end, has a different atmosphere: monastic, coastal and enclosed around spring water. Cadw states that Penmon’s history stretches back to the sixth century, when St Seiriol established a monastery there. The holy well bearing his name is thought to be associated with that early period, although the stone “cell” that houses it is much later; the surviving priory remains mostly date from the 13th century, when the site became Augustinian.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cadw.gov.wales/visit/places-to-visit/penmon-priory-cross-and-st-seiriols-well" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cadw.gov.wales">[Cadw]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cadw.gov.wales</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Cadw Penmon Priory, Cross and St Seiriol’s Well &#124; Cadw</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cadw Penmon Priory, Cross and St Seiriol’s Well &#124; Cadw</span></span></span>

This is why the Cybi and Seiriol tradition works so well as Anglesey folklore. It does not depend on a single dramatic miracle. Instead, it maps character onto place: Cybi belongs to Holyhead, Roman walls and the west; Seiriol belongs to Penmon, the priory and the east; their friendship is placed at a well near the island’s centre. The story makes Anglesey readable as a walked landscape.

## Healing water, signs and saintly power

Holy wells in Anglesey tradition were not ornamental. They were places where people expected water to do something: heal, reveal, bless, answer, protect or sometimes unsettle. St Seiriol’s Well at Penmon is the simplest example. Cadw describes it as a holy well with reputed healing properties, associated with Seiriol’s early monastery even though the present structure around it is later.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cadw.gov.wales/visit/places-to-visit/penmon-priory-cross-and-st-seiriols-well" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cadw.gov.wales">[Cadw]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cadw.gov.wales</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Cadw Penmon Priory, Cross and St Seiriol’s Well &#124; Cadw</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cadw Penmon Priory, Cross and St Seiriol’s Well &#124; Cadw</span></span></span>

That mixture of early association and later fabric is common in holy-well folklore. The saint’s authority gives the water meaning; the stone chamber, path, church and local memory keep the meaning visible. A visitor today may see a historic site rather than a functioning healing shrine, but the old reputation still shapes how the place is interpreted. The well is not just “near” the priory; it helps explain why Penmon feels like a sacred compound rather than a ruin standing by itself.

Dwynwen’s well shows another use of sacred water: divination. The eels or fish in the well did not cure a broken heart in a medical sense; they gave signs about love. That made the spring a meeting point between Christian saintly devotion and older, wider folk habits of reading nature for answers. The important point is not whether eels literally predicted romance, but that generations of visitors treated the well as a place where emotional uncertainty could be ritualised.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cadw.gov.wales/about-us/news/fall-love-waless-most-romantic-relics-celebration-st-dwynwens-day" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cadw.gov.wales">[Cadw]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cadw.gov.wales</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on gov.wales.</span></span></span>

The Clorach tradition adds a third function: fellowship. The water there is not mainly remembered for cures or courtship, but as the meeting point of two saints. This gives Anglesey an unusually gentle saint legend. Many holy places are organised around martyrdom, penance or fear; Cybi and Seiriol are remembered through friendship, regular walking and conversation. The modern pilgrimage route has revived that emphasis by inviting walkers to understand the island through companionship as well as devotion.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.britishpilgrimage.org/portfolio/anglesey-saints-way-seiriol-cybi" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: britishpilgrimage.org">[British Pilgrimage Trust]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">britishpilgrimage.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on britishpilgrimage.org.</span></span></span>

Other Anglesey wells show how broad this water culture could become. Ffynnon Eilian on the north coast, for example, was remembered as a healing well before acquiring darker cursing associations in later tradition. A recent Aberystwyth-linked educational resource on Anglesey’s holy wells notes that people in Wales continued visiting saints’ wells after the Reformation and describes Ffynnon Eilian as having exceptional healing powers in local belief.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pure.aber.ac.uk/ws/portalfiles/portal/68145590/CursesAndBlessingsAtTheHolyWellsOfAngleseyBendithAMelltithWrthFfynhonnauSanctaiddMon.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pure.aber.ac.uk">[Aberystwyth University]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pure.aber.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Aberystwyth University Curses and Blessings at the Holy Wells of Anglesey</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Aberystwyth University Curses and Blessings at the Holy Wells of Anglesey</span></span></span> That strand sits slightly to the side of the Cybi-Seiriol and Dwynwen routes, but it confirms the wider island pattern: holy water could be hopeful, dangerous, useful and morally complicated.

## Modern walking keeps the folklore alive

Modern pilgrimage on Anglesey is not simply nostalgia. It changes how old stories are encountered. A reader can learn Dwynwen’s legend in a paragraph, but walking to Llanddwyn across sand, dunes and tide-sensitive ground makes the story bodily. The Wales Coast Path describes the Newborough and Llanddwyn walk as an easy 4-mile, 6 km route to one of Wales’s most romantic historic sites, with the island accessible at all but the highest tides and the well remembered for eels that predict romantic fortune.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.walescoastpath.gov.uk/plan-your-visit/itineraries/culture-and-heritage-walks/ynys-llanddwyn-and-newborough-forest-isle-of-anglesey/?lang=en" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: walescoastpath.gov.uk">[Wales Coast Path]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">walescoastpath.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on walescoastpath.gov.uk.</span></span></span>

The Anglesey Saints Way does something similar at county scale. Its five-day structure turns the Cybi and Seiriol story into a sequence: Holyhead, Valley, Llanbabo, Llannerch-y-medd, the Clorach wells, Mynydd Bodafon, the east coast and Penmon. The British Pilgrimage Trust’s route description explicitly places ancient churches, holy wells and sacred places along the way, so the walk functions as a curated folklore map as much as a religious journey.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.britishpilgrimage.org/portfolio/anglesey-saints-way-seiriol-cybi" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: britishpilgrimage.org">[British Pilgrimage Trust]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">britishpilgrimage.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on britishpilgrimage.org.</span></span></span>

This modern revival also creates a healthy distinction between old tradition and present use. Nobody needs to claim that today’s marked routes reproduce every step of a sixth-century saint. What matters is that modern walkers are using credible historic anchors — St Cybi’s church, Penmon, Llanddwyn, Ffynnon Gybi and the Clorach tradition — to make an old saintly landscape legible again. The route is contemporary, but the places it joins are not invented out of nothing.

There is a public-facing reason this works so well on Anglesey. The island’s holy wells are not hidden abstractions. They are attached to memorable landscapes: a tidal island, a Roman-walled churchyard, a priory and well chamber, a central meeting spring, a coastal path. For folklore readers, this makes Anglesey unusually rewarding. Belief is not floating free; it is fixed to places where water, walking and story still meet.

## What these wells reveal about Anglesey folklore

Anglesey’s saint-and-well traditions show a different side of the island from its [giant stones]({{ 'giant-stones/' | relative_url }}), witch stories or ghostly legends. They are quieter, but they explain how folklore can be practical. A person came to a well because they wanted help: a cure, a sign, a blessing, a friend, a way through grief or uncertainty. The supernatural element sits close to ordinary human need.

St Dwynwen’s well makes love a matter of pilgrimage. St Seiriol’s well keeps healing attached to a monastic landscape. Ffynnon Gybi and the Clorach wells turn friendship into geography. St Cybi’s Holyhead setting shows how Christian memory could inhabit an older Roman structure, while Penmon shows how a saint’s spring could remain meaningful beside a later medieval priory. Together they make Anglesey feel less like a collection of isolated legends and more like a network of sacred routes.

The strongest way to understand these traditions is not to ask whether every detail happened exactly as told. It is to ask why these stories stayed attached to these places. On Anglesey, the answer is unusually clear: the island lends itself to crossings, meeting points and thresholds. Its saints walk from west to east, its lovers cross sand and tide to Llanddwyn, its wells sit where the human need for help meets the older power of water.
