## Introduction

Fairy Water near Omagh is a named [Tyrone]({{ 'where-tyrone-turns-landscape-into-legend/' | relative_url }}) stream with a dark changeling story attached to it: a crying, troublesome baby is suspected of being a fairy substitute, placed on a shovel and held over the fire, and then vanishes. The tale survives in the National Folklore Collection’s Schools’ Collection, where it is presented as an explanation for how the stream got its name. It is not a comfortable fairy story. Its value lies in the way it preserves a local memory of fear around infant illness, family pressure and dangerous folk remedies, all fixed to a real watercourse west of Omagh. The best evidence is brief rather than abundant, but it is unusually specific: it names the Fairy Water, places it about two miles from Omagh and connects it with the River Strule.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.duchas.ie/en/cbes/5008971/4972293/5114249" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: duchas.ie">[Dúchas]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">duchas.ie</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Dúchas Story</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Dúchas Story</span></span></span>

## The stream behind the story

The Fairy Water is not just a poetic phrase. It is a real river in County Tyrone, rising in the hills above Drumquin and joining the Strule near Omagh. Modern angling descriptions call it a medium-sized river, around 5–20 metres wide, with slower stretches near its confluence with the Strule outside Omagh.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.loughs-agency.org/what-we-do/angling-support/where-to-fish/the-foyle-catchment/river/the-fairy-water" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: loughs-agency.org">[Loughs Agency]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">loughs-agency.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Loughs Agency Fairy Water</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Loughs Agency Fairy Water</span></span></span>

That geography matters because the folklore account is also precise. The Dúchas text begins by placing “a little stream called the Fairy-water” about two miles from Omagh, flowing into the River Strule. It then says there is a story told to explain how the stream received its name. In other words, the tale is not simply a floating changeling legend that happens to mention Tyrone. It is a place-name story: the frightening event explains why this particular watercourse is remembered as “Fairy Water”.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.duchas.ie/en/cbes/5008971/4972293/5114249" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: duchas.ie">[Dúchas]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">duchas.ie</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Dúchas Story</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Dúchas Story</span></span></span>

The wider valley adds another layer of local texture. North of Drumquin, Fairy Water Bogs are officially recognised as a Special Area of Conservation; DAERA describes them as lying in the former floodplains of the Fairy Water valley and as the most important concentration of lowland raised bogs in Northern Ireland. That does not prove any folklore in itself, but it helps explain why the name belongs to a landscape of wet ground, floodplain, river edges and old rural routes rather than to an isolated storybook setting.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.daera-ni.gov.uk/protected-areas/fairy-water-bogs-sac" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: daera-ni.gov.uk">[DAERA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">daera-ni.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on daera-ni.gov.uk.</span></span></span>

## The changeling story recorded for Dúchas

The surviving account appears in the Schools’ Collection under the title “Story - The Fairy Water”. It was collected by Josephine Thornton from Mrs P. Thornton, aged 75, of Kells, County Meath, and is preserved in a Drumbaragh school volume. This is one reason the evidence needs careful handling: the story is about Tyrone, but the recorded informant’s address is in Meath, so the archive gives us a transmitted version of a Tyrone place-story rather than a neat local interview taken on the riverbank itself.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.duchas.ie/en/cbes/5008971/4972293/5114249" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: duchas.ie">[Dúchas]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">duchas.ie</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Dúchas Story</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Dúchas Story</span></span></span>

The plot is stark. A woman and her husband live on the banks of the Fairy Water with a baby who cries constantly and causes trouble in the house. A neighbour suggests that the baby “must be a fairy” and advises placing it on a shovel over the fire. When the husband returns, the parents agree to try it. They put the baby on the shovel, hold it over the fire, and it suddenly disappears. The next morning, the text says, they hear fairy music outside, before the digitised page breaks off and continues on the next page.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.duchas.ie/en/cbes/5008971/4972293/5114249" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: duchas.ie">[Dúchas]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">duchas.ie</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Dúchas Story</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Dúchas Story</span></span></span>

For a modern reader, the important point is not whether the event “happened” as told. Folklorically, the tale is a warning-shaped memory. It links three things that often sit together in changeling tradition: a distressed or unwell child, neighbourly diagnosis, and a ritual act intended to force the fairies to reveal or reverse the exchange. The Omagh version is especially bleak because the child’s disappearance is not softened into an obvious rescue scene in the visible part of the archival transcription. What remains is the shock of the act and the eerie sound of fairy music by the stream.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.duchas.ie/en/cbes/5008971/4972293/5114249" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: duchas.ie">[Dúchas]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">duchas.ie</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Dúchas Story</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Dúchas Story</span></span></span>

## Why the shovel and fire matter

The shovel-over-the-fire detail is not random. It belongs to a wider British and Irish changeling pattern in which fire, iron and the hearth are treated as tests or weapons against a suspected fairy substitute. E. Sidney Hartland’s nineteenth-century study of fairy tales describes several harsh variants: a shovel made red-hot, a child seated on it, a child threatened with the oven, or a changeling expected to flee up the chimney. Hartland also notes that such stories could slide from symbolic threat into real cruelty.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Science_of_Fairy_Tales/Chapter_5" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: en.wikisource.org">[Wikisource]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">en.wikisource.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Science of Fairy Tales/Chapter 5</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Science of Fairy Tales/Chapter 5</span></span></span>

That wider pattern helps interpret the Fairy Water tale without over-expanding it. The neighbour’s advice in the Omagh story uses the same logic: if the baby is truly a fairy, fire will expose it or drive it away. The home’s central hearth becomes a testing place, while the shovel provides the iron surface between human hands and the suspected supernatural child. In folklore terms, the household is trying to force a hidden truth into the open. In human terms, the story records the terrifying pressure that could gather around a child who cried, failed to thrive or behaved in a way adults could not explain.

The most unsettling part is the neighbour’s role. The parents do not invent the test alone; they are prompted by a local adviser. That detail feels socially important. Changeling belief was not only a private fear inside a family. It could be reinforced by the community, especially where illness, exhaustion and uncertainty made ordinary care feel powerless.

## Illness, fear and local memory

Changeling tales often circle around babies or children who are sick, restless, disabled, undernourished or simply different from what adults expected. Modern readers should be wary of turning every changeling story into one medical diagnosis. The Fairy Water account does not name a disease. It says only that the baby was “always crying and giving trouble in the house”. That small phrase is enough to show how the story makes distress legible through fairy belief: crying becomes evidence, trouble becomes suspicion, and suspicion becomes ritual danger.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.duchas.ie/en/cbes/5008971/4972293/5114249" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: duchas.ie">[Dúchas]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">duchas.ie</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Dúchas Story</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Dúchas Story</span></span></span>

The tale also shows why changeling folklore can feel darker than many fairy traditions attached to trees, forts or music. In a fairy-bush story, the warning is usually “do not interfere with that place”. In a changeling story, the danger enters the home and attaches to a vulnerable person. The suspected fairy is also the child in the cradle. That ambiguity is what makes the motif so disturbing: the story depends on the belief that the child might not really be the child.

For Tyrone folklore, Fairy Water is therefore less a picturesque fairy site than a place where illness, fear and explanation meet. The stream name becomes a container for a hard memory: a family under strain, a neighbour’s supernatural interpretation, and a ritual that modern readers will recognise as cruel even while understanding its place in older belief.

## How strong is the evidence?

The evidence for the Fairy Water changeling story is valuable but narrow. Its strongest source is the Dúchas Schools’ Collection item, and the Schools’ Collection itself is a major folklore archive: UCD describes it as a 1937–39 project supervised by the Irish Folklore Commission in which senior primary school children recorded more than 750,000 pages of local history and oral tradition.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ucd.ie/irishfolklore/en/collections/schoolscollectionduchas/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ucd.ie">[University College Dublin]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ucd.ie</span><span class="citation-popover-title">University College Dublin Schools&#x27; Collection (Dúchas</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">University College Dublin Schools&#x27; Collection (Dúchas</span></span></span>

At the same time, this specific Fairy Water item should not be overstated. It is a short tale, not a cluster of many independent Omagh testimonies. The collector and informant details suggest the story was recorded in a Meath school context from an older woman whose address was Kells, while the story’s setting is explicitly Tyrone. That does not make it worthless; migration, family memory and repeated storytelling often carried local legends well beyond their original townlands. But it does mean the page should be read as a preserved tradition about Fairy Water, not as proof of a documented local incident.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.duchas.ie/en/cbes/5008971/4972293/5114249" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: duchas.ie">[Dúchas]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">duchas.ie</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Dúchas Story</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Dúchas Story</span></span></span>

Modern retellings sometimes smooth the tale into a cleaner origin legend, where fairy music confirms that the stream’s supernatural name has been explained. That is useful for public folklore, but the archival version is more revealing because it leaves the rough edges visible: the crying child, the neighbour’s advice, the shovel, the disappearance and the unfinished continuation on the next page. The fragmentary nature of the evidence is part of the story’s character.

## Why Fairy Water belongs on Tyrone’s folklore map

Fairy Water matters because it gives Tyrone’s fairy tradition a specific and uncomfortable local anchor. Many county fairy stories protect outdoor places: hills, bushes, raths, stones, wells and paths. This one attaches fairy belief to a stream near Omagh, but the real drama happens inside a household. The landscape remembers the story afterwards.

It also fits Tyrone’s wider pattern of folklore surviving in scattered, place-bound pieces rather than in one grand county myth. A stream name, a school manuscript, an elderly informant, a troubling household tale: together these are enough to show how local legend worked. It did not need castles, heroes or elaborate mythology. It could cling to an ordinary watercourse and explain why people spoke of it as fairy-haunted.

The Fairy Water story is best retold today with care. It should not be romanticised as a charming fairy anecdote, and it should not be treated as supernatural fact. Its power lies in the tension between belief and vulnerability. Near Omagh, the name Fairy Water preserves not only a memory of fairies by a stream, but a memory of how fear of illness could become a story about substitution, disappearance and music heard from the other side.
