## Introduction

[Banffshire]({{ 'what-mysteries-shaped-banffshires/' | relative_url }})’s fairy traditions belong to a wider Scottish belief world in which hills, woods, springs and lonely roads could be places of hidden activity. In this historic north-eastern county, fairy stories were not simply tales of charming little beings but ways of explaining unusual landscapes, dangerous encounters, illness, luck and the boundaries between the human world and the unknown. The traditions attached meaning to places that might otherwise appear ordinary: a grassy mound, an old track, a remote glen or a stretch of woodland could become a location where stories warned people to show respect.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://blog.historicenvironment.scot/2025/06/scary-fairies-scotlands-gaelic-fairy-folklore/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: blog.historicenvironment.scot">[Historic Environment Scotland Blog]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">blog.historicenvironment.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Historic Environment Scotland Blog Scary fairies!</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Scotland&#x27;s Gaelic fairy folkloreJune 24, 2025 — 24 Jun 2025 — In this blog Catriona Murray explores Scotland&#x27;s Gaelic fairy folklore and...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 24, 2025</span></span></span>

Directly documented Banffshire fairy tales are less numerous than the county’s broader collections of songs, legends and supernatural traditions, but the evidence shows that Banffshire sat within a rich north-eastern Scottish storytelling landscape. Collectors of local tradition, including figures associated with the north-east such as Gavin Greig, helped preserve the oral culture of the region, while wider Scottish folklore collections record the kinds of fairy beliefs that shaped how communities understood remote places.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.abdn.ac.uk/elphinstone/resources/northern-folk/part-iii/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: abdn.ac.uk">[The University of Aberdeen]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">abdn.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Folk-Song in Buchan and Folk-Song of the North-East, ed. The importance of Banffshire’s fairy lore lies less in finding a single famous “fairy hill” and more in understanding a pattern: the county’s varied landscape of coast, river valleys, forests and uplands provided the setting in which hidden-world stories could survive. Fairy traditions became a way of remembering the character of the land itself.

## Fairy beliefs across Banffshire landscapes

Scottish fairy belief was traditionally closely connected with geography. Fairy dwellings were often imagined beneath hills or within mounds, while fairy paths were associated with routes crossing significant natural features. These ideas created a landscape where certain places carried special rules: some should not be disturbed, some should be avoided after dark, and some were treated with caution because they were thought to belong partly to another world.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://strathardheritage.org/archive/some-fairy-sites-in-the-national-park/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: strathardheritage.org">[strathardheritage.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">strathardheritage.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Some Fairy Sites in the National ParkThe sìthein are often small conical hills, and in Celtic mythology they were reputed to have hollow...</span></span></span>

In Banffshire, this way of thinking fitted naturally with the county’s mixture of farmland, woodland and upland country. The inland areas towards the Cairngorms and upper river valleys shared many traditions found elsewhere in north-eastern Scotland, where remote hills and forests were remembered as places of fairy activity. Accounts from the wider Cairngorm region describe fairy communities beneath grassy hills, night-time dancing and encounters with hidden beings, showing the type of storytelling environment that also influenced neighbouring historic counties such as Banffshire.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://faeryfolklorist.blogspot.com/2018/04/the-fairy-folk-of-cairngorms.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: faeryfolklorist.blogspot.com">[faeryfolklorist.blogspot.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">faeryfolklorist.blogspot.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">the fairy folk of cairngorms</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Fairy Folk of the Cairngorms28 Apr 2018 — Folklore from the Cairngorms, including: Fairy Sweethearts, Green Fairy Dogs, Kelpies, Brow...</span></span></span>

Fairies in this tradition were not simply the friendly figures of later children’s stories. They could be helpful, but they could also be unpredictable. A traveller might receive assistance, while another person might disappear, become confused or suffer bad luck after entering the wrong place or breaking an old custom. Scottish fairy belief often reflected a practical relationship with uncertainty: wild landscapes could be beautiful and valuable, but they could also be dangerous.

### Why hills became fairy places

Hills and mounds were among the strongest locations for fairy associations because they appeared to offer a boundary between visible and hidden worlds. A small rounded hill could be imagined as a dwelling beneath the surface, with music, gatherings and movement continuing unseen below. Researchers of Scottish cultural landscapes have noted the importance of fairy hills as places where communities attached supernatural meaning to physical features of the land.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.alastairmcintosh.com/articles/1997_faerie_hills.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: alastairmcintosh.com">[alastairmcintosh.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">alastairmcintosh.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on alastairmcintosh.com.</span></span></span>

For Banffshire communities living among ancient routes, farm boundaries and older settlements, these beliefs helped preserve memories of places whose origins were uncertain. A mound, unusual rock formation or isolated hill might be explained through stories of hidden inhabitants rather than through archaeology or geology.

This does not mean every landscape feature had a fairy story attached to it. Instead, fairy tradition provided a shared language for describing places that felt old, strange or separate from everyday life.

## Hills, woods and hidden places in tradition

Banffshire’s fairy landscape was shaped by the relationship between people and the natural environment. Woods, burns, springs and high ground were not empty spaces in traditional imagination; they were places where encounters could happen.

### Woods and lonely routes

Woodlands often carried fairy associations because they represented places beyond normal settlement. Travellers entering a forest or passing through a remote track entered a setting where ordinary rules were less certain. Similar Scottish traditions include solitary woodland spirits and fairy figures connected with protecting or inhabiting wild places.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ghillie_Dhu" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Ghillie Dhu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Ghillie Dhu</span></span></span>

In a county like Banffshire, where older routes linked coastal settlements with inland areas, stories about encounters on the margins of habitation would have been particularly meaningful. A fairy encounter story often worked as both entertainment and advice: do not wander carelessly, respect unusual places and remember that the countryside has its own dangers.

### Rivers, wells and the edge of the unknown

Water was another important part of Scottish supernatural geography. Streams and wells were often associated with spirits, healing traditions and encounters with otherworldly beings. Fairy lore sometimes overlapped with beliefs about beings connected to water, including traditions of supernatural women encountered beside lonely streams.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bean-nighe" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wikipedia.org.</span></span></span>

For Banffshire, with its rivers flowing from upland areas towards the Moray coast, water landscapes provided natural settings for stories. A quiet pool or isolated ford could become a place where folklore attached warnings about what might happen when humans crossed into unfamiliar spaces.

### Fairy lore and older memories of place

One reason fairy traditions survived is that they connected ordinary people with the deeper history of their surroundings. A story about hidden inhabitants beneath a hill could preserve the feeling that a place was ancient, even when its original meaning had been forgotten.

This process can be seen across Scotland, where prehistoric sites, ancient mounds and natural landmarks were frequently absorbed into later storytelling. ## From oral tradition to recorded folklore

Much of Banffshire’s fairy lore belongs to the world of oral memory rather than written medieval records. This creates an important distinction: some stories are well-attested examples of regional belief, while others survive mainly through later retellings, local tradition or comparisons with neighbouring areas.

The north-east of Scotland was an important region for collectors of traditional song and folklore. The work of collectors such as Gavin Greig helped preserve material from communities whose storytelling cultures were changing rapidly during the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries. The University of Aberdeen’s Elphinstone Institute continues to maintain resources connected with northern Scottish folk traditions and collections.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.abdn.ac.uk/elphinstone/resources/northern-folk/part-iii/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: abdn.ac.uk">[The University of Aberdeen]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">abdn.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Folk-Song in Buchan and Folk-Song of the North-East, ed. Kenneth S. Goldstein and Arthur Argo (Hatboro, 1963). Jackson, Bruce. Fieldwork...</span></span></span>

Fairy stories were often recorded alongside ballads, customs and accounts of supernatural experiences. This reflects how people actually understood them: fairy belief was not a separate “mythology” but part of everyday explanations for fortune, misfortune, landscape and community memory.

## Modern retellings of Banffshire fairy lore

Today, Banffshire’s fairy traditions are usually encountered through heritage interpretation, walking routes, folklore writing and wider interest in Scotland’s mysterious landscapes. Modern retellings often emphasise beauty and enchantment, but the older traditions were more complicated. The fairy world could inspire wonder while also reminding people that nature and unfamiliar places deserved respect.

The popularity of Scottish fairy landscapes in tourism has also changed the tone of these stories. Places associated with fairy traditions across Scotland are often presented as magical destinations, whereas older folklore frequently carried warnings about boundaries, obligations and the consequences of disturbing hidden places.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://blog.historicenvironment.scot/2025/06/scary-fairies-scotlands-gaelic-fairy-folklore/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: blog.historicenvironment.scot">[Historic Environment Scotland Blog]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">blog.historicenvironment.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Historic Environment Scotland Blog Scary fairies!</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Scotland&#x27;s Gaelic fairy folkloreJune 24, 2025 — 24 Jun 2025 — In this blog Catriona Murray explores Scotland&#x27;s Gaelic fairy folklore and...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 24, 2025</span></span></span>

For Banffshire, the lasting value of fairy tradition is not that it proves a supernatural presence in the county’s hills and woods. Its importance is cultural: these stories reveal how generations of people interpreted their surroundings, remembered forgotten places and created a shared sense that the landscape contained more history than could immediately be seen.

Banffshire’s fairy stories therefore belong to a wider heritage of hidden landscapes — places where geography, memory and imagination meet. The hills, woods and lonely paths of the county became more than scenery; they became part of a living tradition about how people relate to the unknown.
