## Introduction

Loughareema is one of [County Antrim]({{ 'county-antrim/' | relative_url }})’s most memorable haunted landscapes because its ghost stories grow from something real and visible: a lake that can be full one day and almost gone soon after. Set in the upland country near Ballycastle, on the route between Cushendall and Ballycastle, it is widely known as the Vanishing Lake. Official tourism material describes it as a place where “one day” there may be a sparkling lake and a few days later only cracked mud, while geological sources explain that its water drains away through an underground system rather than by an ordinary surface outflow.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://discovernorthernireland.com/listing/loughareema-%28the-vanishing-lake%29/71015101/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: discovernorthernireland.com">[Discover Northern Ireland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">discovernorthernireland.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Discover Northern IrelandLoughareema (The Vanishing Lake)Best known among locals as &#x27;The Vanishing Lake&#x27;, Loughareema is a mysterious pla...</span></span></span>

The haunting tradition is not just decorative. Loughareema’s old road, sudden water, poor visibility and recorded fatal accidents made it a natural setting for stories of ghostly return. Local retellings centre especially on the drowning of Colonel John Magee McNeill and his coachman David McNeill in 1898, followed by later stories of a drowned breadman, phantom hoofbeats, cries in the dark, and the idea that the lake itself is unlucky or inhabited by dangerous water spirits.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.belfastentries.com/places/places-to-see/loughareema-the-vanishing-lake/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: belfastentries.com">[Belfast Entries]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">belfastentries.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Loughareema Inquest</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Belfast EntriesLoughareema - The Vanishing Lake and its GhostsAugust 19, 2022 — 19 Aug 2022 — On 30th September 1898, Colonel John Magee...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 19, 2022</span></span></span>

## Where Is Loughareema?

Loughareema lies in the historic county of Antrim, in the north-east of Northern Ireland, in the upland country associated with the Glens and the road over the hills towards Ballycastle. The official Placenames Database of Ireland places the lake in County Antrim, in the barony of Cary, the civil parish of Culfeightrin and the townland of Ballyvennaght; it records the Irish place-name as Loch an Mhadhma, with Loughaveema as the English form and Loughareema as another local English name.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.logainm.ie/en/118053" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: logainm.ie">[logainm.ie]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">logainm.ie</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">names. Loughareema. local name (What is this?)Read more...</span></span></span>

For a folklore reader, the setting matters as much as the map reference. This is not a sheltered village pond or a picturesque demesne lake. Sources repeatedly describe Loughareema as remote, upland, boggy, bleak or mist-prone, with a road running through or beside the basin. The Geological Society calls it a remote upland lake and notes that its frequently foggy, treeless, blanket-bog surroundings make it an uneasy place to linger; Discover Northern Ireland presents it as a mysterious stop on the wider County Antrim visitor landscape.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.geolsoc.org.uk/science-and-policy/100-great-geosites/landscape/loughareema/?srsltid=AfmBOor0Rp7wGeORQe2pSapZTRXHRnOHuMw3jxeTjpzglC2-rYo3Gomn" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: geolsoc.org.uk">[The Geological Society of London]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">geolsoc.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Geological Society of LondonLoughareemaThe remote upland lake of Loughareema in Co. That combination of road, bog, fog and unpredictable water is the key to Loughareema’s identity. County Antrim has many legendary places where geology and story meet, from the Giant’s Causeway to caves, cliffs and glens, but Loughareema is distinctive because its wonder is changeable. A visitor may arrive to find a lake, a mud flat, or something between the two. The place itself seems to refuse a stable form.

## How Does the Lake Appear and Disappear?

The plain explanation is geological, but it is unusual enough to feel folkloric even before the ghost stories begin. Loughareema is an ephemeral lake: it fills and drains according to rainfall, inflow, blockage and underground drainage. The Geological Society says three streams flow into it but there is no visible stream flowing out. Instead, water drains through a sink at the base of the lake into an underground system and reappears about 2.5 kilometres away at a large spring feeding the Carey River.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.geolsoc.org.uk/science-and-policy/100-great-geosites/landscape/loughareema/?srsltid=AfmBOooxoz-TQMrIPFlyqYNhKHLbTxQwNVk2gqdZ3ezdTfP88U5jRdez" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: geolsoc.org.uk">[The Geological Society of London]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">geolsoc.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Geological Society of LondonLoughareemaLocal legend tells us of the drowning of a coach and horses in the 19th century as they tried...</span></span></span>

The mechanism is not just a simple plughole. Geological sources describe Loughareema as one of Northern Ireland’s most enigmatic geological sites, partly because the drainage system is not fully understood and because the local rock setting is complicated. The Geological Society notes the problem of a sink that should be associated with Cretaceous Ulster White Limestone, while basalt occurs within metres of it; Live Science, summarising recent geological interest, similarly reports that scientists are still uncertain about the exact working and formation of the underground drainage route.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.geolsoc.org.uk/science-and-policy/100-great-geosites/landscape/loughareema/?srsltid=AfmBOooxoz-TQMrIPFlyqYNhKHLbTxQwNVk2gqdZ3ezdTfP88U5jRdez" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: geolsoc.org.uk">[The Geological Society of London]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">geolsoc.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Geological Society of LondonLoughareemaLocal legend tells us of the drowning of a coach and horses in the 19th century as they tried...</span></span></span>

The most reader-friendly way to understand the cycle is this: water gathers in the basin after rain, but the lake-bed drain may be blocked by debris such as peat, silt or vegetation. When the blockage holds, the water rises and the old crossing becomes dangerous. When pressure or movement clears the blockage, the lake can drain rapidly into the hidden system below. A road-name study for the former Moyle council area describes Loughareema Road as prone to flooding after heavy rain and says the lake can empty suddenly, perhaps when silt blocking the outflow is disturbed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://gordon-mccoy.squarespace.com/s/Moyle_Council_Area_P_Tempan.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gordon-mccoy.squarespace.com">[Ulster Place-Name Society]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gordon-mccoy.squarespace.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Moyle Council Area P Tempan</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Moyle Council Area P Tempan</span></span></span>

This is why the lake’s strangeness is not a one-off curiosity. It is repeated, visible and uncertain. The haunting stories depend on the same feature that interests geologists: Loughareema can look safe, passable or empty at one time, then become deep water under different conditions. A disappearing lake is already a landscape of doubt; a road through that landscape turns doubt into danger.

## Why Was the Road So Dangerous?

The older danger at Loughareema came from a practical problem: the road was made across ground that could become part of the lake. Today the A2 route is raised and protected, but local-history accounts stress that the earlier post road could be covered by water when the lake was full. Belfast Entries, drawing on newspaper reports, explains that the modern road has safety walls and is raised high enough to remain passable, whereas in earlier times the road to Ballycastle was frequently flooded.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.belfastentries.com/places/places-to-see/loughareema-the-vanishing-lake/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: belfastentries.com">[Belfast Entries]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">belfastentries.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Loughareema Inquest</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Belfast EntriesLoughareema - The Vanishing Lake and its GhostsAugust 19, 2022 — 19 Aug 2022 — On 30th September 1898, Colonel John Magee...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 19, 2022</span></span></span>

The danger is easy to underestimate from a dry photograph. When the lake was empty or low, the road line could seem straightforward. When it was covered, travellers had to judge depth, alignment and safety in poor weather, poor light, or fog. Horses could panic; wheels could leave the road edge; a driver might mistake a flooded surface for a shallow crossing. The Geological Society’s summary of the local legend makes the essential point: a road had been built through the lake when it was empty, so in darkness it could be impossible to tell whether the water level was high or low.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.geolsoc.org.uk/science-and-policy/100-great-geosites/landscape/loughareema/?srsltid=AfmBOooxoz-TQMrIPFlyqYNhKHLbTxQwNVk2gqdZ3ezdTfP88U5jRdez" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: geolsoc.org.uk">[The Geological Society of London]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">geolsoc.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Geological Society of LondonLoughareemaLocal legend tells us of the drowning of a coach and horses in the 19th century as they tried...</span></span></span>

This is the sort of setting where ghost stories often gather. Roads are ordinary places of decision: go on, turn back, hurry, wait, trust the surface, follow the old line. Water is harder to read, especially in darkness. At Loughareema the road and the lake created a repeated hazard, and repeated hazards are easily remembered as moral tales: do not rush, do not ignore warnings, do not take the strange place lightly.

## The 1898 Coach Drowning

The best-attested haunting tradition at Loughareema is tied to a real fatal accident on 30 September 1898. Belfast Entries identifies the victim as Colonel John Magee McNeill, a retired Royal Engineers officer who had been staying with his uncle, Captain Daniel McNeil of Cushendun House, and was travelling to catch the 3pm train from Ballycastle. His coachman was David McNeill, aged 56 and married.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.belfastentries.com/places/places-to-see/loughareema-the-vanishing-lake/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: belfastentries.com">[Belfast Entries]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">belfastentries.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Loughareema Inquest</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Belfast EntriesLoughareema - The Vanishing Lake and its GhostsAugust 19, 2022 — 19 Aug 2022 — On 30th September 1898, Colonel John Magee...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 19, 2022</span></span></span>

According to the account, the road was covered by water from the lough. The coachman hesitated because the horses were spirited, but the crossing was attempted. In the middle of the water, the horses panicked, reared and turned; the carriage left the road and went into the deeper lake. Belfast Entries quotes the Belfast Newsletter report of 3 October 1898 and records that an inquest was held on 3 October, with the coroner for North Antrim returning a verdict of accidental drowning for both men.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.belfastentries.com/places/places-to-see/loughareema-the-vanishing-lake/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: belfastentries.com">[Belfast Entries]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">belfastentries.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Loughareema Inquest</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Belfast EntriesLoughareema - The Vanishing Lake and its GhostsAugust 19, 2022 — 19 Aug 2022 — On 30th September 1898, Colonel John Magee...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 19, 2022</span></span></span>

This is where Loughareema’s folklore becomes more than a vague “haunted lake” label. The story has names, a date, a route, a purpose for the journey, an inquest and a physical mechanism for the accident. That makes it stronger than many local ghost tales whose origins are difficult to trace. The supernatural part comes later, in the claim that the catastrophe still repeats itself in sound or sight: an old-fashioned carriage on or near the shoreline, hoofbeats, struggling horses, and human cries in the dark.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.belfastentries.com/places/places-to-see/loughareema-the-vanishing-lake/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: belfastentries.com">[Belfast Entries]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">belfastentries.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Loughareema Inquest</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Belfast EntriesLoughareema - The Vanishing Lake and its GhostsAugust 19, 2022 — 19 Aug 2022 — On 30th September 1898, Colonel John Magee...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 19, 2022</span></span></span>

The tale also carries a recognisable moral shape. A hurried journey, a dangerous crossing, hesitation ignored, animals forced onward, and sudden loss: it has the structure of a warning story even when told without ghosts. The haunting gives the accident a second life in local memory, turning a documented drowning into a recurring presence attached to the road.

## The Breadman and Other Drowning Traditions

The coach drowning is the central case, but it is not the only fatal tradition associated with Loughareema. A second frequently repeated story concerns Dan Scullion of Ballyvoy, described in local retellings as a young bread delivery man who set out by horse-drawn cart during a blizzard. Belfast Entries says he had been advised to stay at home but worried about delivering bread to rural customers; in the poor visibility he could not see that the road had flooded, and he too was lost to the lake.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.belfastentries.com/places/places-to-see/loughareema-the-vanishing-lake/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: belfastentries.com">[Belfast Entries]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">belfastentries.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Loughareema Inquest</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Belfast EntriesLoughareema - The Vanishing Lake and its GhostsAugust 19, 2022 — 19 Aug 2022 — On 30th September 1898, Colonel John Magee...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 19, 2022</span></span></span>

This second story matters because it shifts the meaning of the lake from a single tragedy to a pattern. The coach accident may be about status, haste and a risky decision by travellers on a schedule. The breadman story is more domestic and local: duty, bad weather, rural isolation and a worker trying to reach households that depended on him. In both, the road is the trap. The lake does not need to behave like a monster; it only needs to hide the familiar way beneath water.

Some retellings also include a horse-drawn ambulance story involving John Mitchell, a driver from Dalriada Hospital in Ballycastle, who was said to have travelled in stormy weather to collect a pregnant woman from Cushendall. Belfast Entries presents this as a tale recounted in the Belfast Newsletter by “The Roamer” in 2019, which makes it a modern reported retelling rather than a deeply documented nineteenth-century case on the same evidential footing as the McNeill inquest.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.belfastentries.com/places/places-to-see/loughareema-the-vanishing-lake/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: belfastentries.com">[Belfast Entries]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">belfastentries.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Loughareema Inquest</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Belfast EntriesLoughareema - The Vanishing Lake and its GhostsAugust 19, 2022 — 19 Aug 2022 — On 30th September 1898, Colonel John Magee...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 19, 2022</span></span></span>

The difference between these stories is important. The 1898 coach accident is anchored in named newspaper and inquest reporting. The breadman and ambulance traditions are part of the wider local narrative field around the lake, valuable as folklore but harder to pin down from the publicly accessible evidence. A careful reading does not flatten them into the same category. Loughareema’s legend is strongest when it is allowed to be layered: documented tragedy, local memory, repeated warning tale and ghost story.

## Ghosts, Kelpies and the “Fairy Lough”

Loughareema’s supernatural reputation has several strands. The most common is the ghostly carriage: people are said to see or hear an old coach, horses and drowning men near the lake, especially when the lough is full. The Geological Society’s account also mentions the prospect of a kelpie or water-ghoul, placing the lake within a wider northern and Celtic pattern of dangerous water beings associated with lonely pools, lochs and crossings.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.geolsoc.org.uk/science-and-policy/100-great-geosites/landscape/loughareema/?srsltid=AfmBOooxoz-TQMrIPFlyqYNhKHLbTxQwNVk2gqdZ3ezdTfP88U5jRdez" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: geolsoc.org.uk">[The Geological Society of London]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">geolsoc.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Geological Society of LondonLoughareemaLocal legend tells us of the drowning of a coach and horses in the 19th century as they tried...</span></span></span>

The name tradition adds another layer. The Placenames Database records Loch an Mhadhma / Loughaveema, with Loughareema as a local English name, while Belfast Entries notes that in past times the lake was also known as the Fairy Lough.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.logainm.ie/en/118053" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: logainm.ie">[logainm.ie]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">logainm.ie</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">names. In Irish and Ulster tradition, fairy associations often attach to places where ordinary rules seem unreliable: hills, raths, thorn trees, wells, bogs, old roads and sudden water.

The kelpie element is especially revealing because it links animal panic, water danger and shapeshifting fear. Kelpies are often imagined as horse-like water beings that lure or drag people into water. At Loughareema, even without a fully developed kelpie narrative, the setting invites the motif: real horses drowned there, the road crosses a vanishing body of water, and the most famous ghost sound is the struggle of hooves. The folklore does not have to invent the horse-and-water association; history supplied it.

This is also why Loughareema’s ghost story remains persuasive as a local legend. It is not a random apparition pasted onto a scenic lake. Its supernatural imagery grows directly from the known hazards of the place: horses, road, water, fog, night travel, sudden depth and the fear of hearing an old accident replayed.

## How Old and Well-Attested Is the Tradition?

The underlying landscape phenomenon is well attested by official tourism, geological and conservation sources. Discover Northern Ireland identifies Loughareema as the Vanishing Lake; the Geological Society explains the inflow, underground drainage and continuing scientific uncertainty; the Earth Science Conservation Review records it as a disappearing lake and glacial feature, located at Lough Bridge on the main Cushendall to Ballycastle road.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://discovernorthernireland.com/listing/loughareema-%28the-vanishing-lake%29/71015101/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: discovernorthernireland.com">[discovernorthernireland.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">discovernorthernireland.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Discover Northern IrelandLoughareema (The Vanishing Lake)Best known among locals as &#x27;The Vanishing Lake&#x27;, Loughareema is a mysterious pla...</span></span></span>

The 1898 coach accident is the strongest historical anchor for the haunting tradition because it is tied to newspaper reporting and an inquest. Belfast Entries cites the Belfast Newsletter of 3 and 4 October 1898 for the accident and inquest, including the witness account of the horses panicking in the flooded road.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.belfastentries.com/places/places-to-see/loughareema-the-vanishing-lake/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: belfastentries.com">[Belfast Entries]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">belfastentries.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Loughareema Inquest</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Belfast EntriesLoughareema - The Vanishing Lake and its GhostsAugust 19, 2022 — 19 Aug 2022 — On 30th September 1898, Colonel John Magee...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 19, 2022</span></span></span> That does not prove the ghost, of course, but it does prove that the ghost story grew around a real death at the lake, not merely around an invented gothic set-piece.

The broader supernatural tradition is more mixed. Claims of a phantom coach, cries, hoofbeats, a kelpie, unlucky waters or a Fairy Lough are preserved mostly in local-history, tourism and popular retelling sources rather than in a single early folklore collection. The Geological Society includes the ghost and kelpie material in its public geosite account, which shows that these stories are now part of the recognised cultural identity of the site, even though they are presented as legend rather than verified experience.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.geolsoc.org.uk/science-and-policy/100-great-geosites/landscape/loughareema/?srsltid=AfmBOooxoz-TQMrIPFlyqYNhKHLbTxQwNVk2gqdZ3ezdTfP88U5jRdez" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: geolsoc.org.uk">[The Geological Society of London]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">geolsoc.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Geological Society of LondonLoughareemaLocal legend tells us of the drowning of a coach and horses in the 19th century as they tried...</span></span></span>

That mixed evidence is typical of place-based folklore. Loughareema is not “old” in the same way as a medieval saint’s well or an early mythological cycle. Its most famous ghost is modern by folklore standards, crystallising around a late nineteenth-century accident. Yet the motifs it draws on — dangerous water, fairy places, horse apparitions, fatal roads and weather-bound warnings — are much older patterns in the story culture of Ireland, Scotland and Ulster.

## Why Roads and Water Attract Ghost Stories

Loughareema is a useful case study in why haunted-road stories often attach to liminal places: places that are between one thing and another. A road is meant to be dry, directed and human-made. A lake is wet, shifting and indifferent. At Loughareema the two overlap. The result is a landscape where the ordinary promise of a road — that it will carry you safely from one settlement to another — becomes uncertain.

This uncertainty is not imagined. The Earth Science Conservation Review describes the site as a disappearing lake in a glacial meltwater landscape, with management concerns around peat movement and detritus entering the lake.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.habitas.org.uk/escr/site.asp?Item=12" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: habitas.org.uk">[Habitas]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">habitas.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Habitas Loughareema</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Habitas Loughareema</span></span></span> DAERA’s Carey Valley Area of Special Scientific Interest page also notes that fen communities around Loughareema occur near a road area, while most of Loughareema is subject to changing water conditions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.daera-ni.gov.uk/protected-areas/carey-valley-assi" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: daera-ni.gov.uk">[daera-ni.gov.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">daera-ni.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on daera-ni.gov.uk.</span></span></span> In other words, the instability is ecological and geological as well as folkloric.

Ghost stories often do cultural work at such places. They mark danger, explain unease, preserve memory and make a landscape memorable. At Loughareema, the phantom coach tells later travellers that the flooded road once killed people. The breadman story emphasises bad weather and rural duty. The kelpie idea turns unpredictable water into a watchful presence. The Fairy Lough name places the basin in the old category of places not to be treated casually.

The haunting is therefore not just about fear. It is a form of local interpretation. It says: this place changes; people have died here; do not assume the road is always what it appears to be.

## Loughareema in County Antrim’s Folklore Landscape

Within County Antrim, Loughareema belongs to a family of stories where dramatic physical places carry legendary meanings. The Giant’s Causeway turns basalt columns into a giant’s road; the Glens gather fairy and banshee traditions; Slemish, holy wells and old church sites connect landscape to saints and memory. Loughareema’s contribution is different: it is not mainly a heroic or sacred place, but a haunted hazard.

That makes it especially valuable for understanding Antrim folklore beyond the famous tourist icons. Loughareema is a working example of how local legend can arise from infrastructure and environment. It is about a road as much as a lake, about travel as much as water, and about modern accidents as much as inherited supernatural belief. The county’s folklore is not confined to ancient monuments or spectacular coastlines; it also lives in awkward crossings, bog roads, weather stories and the memory of people who did not get home.

Modern retellings keep the lake active in public imagination. Discover Northern Ireland presents it as a visitor attraction; geological bodies use it as an accessible example of a puzzling ephemeral lake; local-history writers retell the coach and breadman stories; and children’s literature has even adapted the idea of Loughareema’s vanishing waters for younger readers through Paddy Donnelly’s picture book The Vanishing Lake, which the publisher describes as inspired by a mysterious lake that is sometimes full and sometimes empty.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://discovernorthernireland.com/listing/loughareema-%28the-vanishing-lake%29/71015101/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: discovernorthernireland.com">[discovernorthernireland.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">discovernorthernireland.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Discover Northern IrelandLoughareema (The Vanishing Lake)Best known among locals as &#x27;The Vanishing Lake&#x27;, Loughareema is a mysterious pla...</span></span></span>

That range of uses shows how folklore changes register. The same place can be a geological curiosity, a cautionary road story, a ghost legend, a tourism stop and a children’s imaginative landscape. What remains constant is the central wonder: Loughareema refuses to stay one thing.

## What Should Readers Take from the Vanishing Lake?

The most grounded answer to “Why is Loughareema called the Vanishing Lake?” is that its waters drain underground through a still-intriguing natural system, leaving the basin to shift between lake, wetland and exposed mud according to conditions. The most folkloric answer is that this unstable water made the old road dangerous, and real deaths at the crossing gave the place a haunted memory. Both answers are needed. Without the geology, the ghost story loses its setting; without the ghost story, the geology loses much of its local emotional force.

Loughareema’s strongest tradition is not that the supernatural has been proved, but that a strange landscape shaped a believable haunting. The lake’s changing waters, the flooded road, the 1898 coach accident, later drowning traditions and talk of phantom horses all point to the same truth about place-based folklore: stories cling where the land itself gives people reason to wonder, remember and take care.

For County Antrim, Loughareema is one of the clearest examples of a haunted landscape story rooted in physical reality. It is not merely a spooky tale beside a lake. It is a meeting point of geology, danger, road history, local mourning and supernatural imagination — a vanishing water that keeps returning in story.

    params.push('toolid=' + encodeURIComponent(cfg.tool_id || '10001'));
    if (reference) params.push('customid=' + encodeURIComponent(reference).slice(0, 256));
    return url + joiner + params.join('&');
  }
  function domainLabel(marketId) {
    var cfg = markets[marketId] || markets[defaultMarket] || {};
    return cfg.domain_label || 'eBay';
  }
  function flagClass(marketId) {
    return 'fr-ebay-market-flag fr-ebay-market-flag--' + String(marketId || '').toLowerCase().replace(/_/g, '-').replace(/[^a-z0-9-]+/g, '');
  }
  function closeMenu(section) {
    var menu = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-menu]');
    var trigger = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-trigger]');
    var picker = section.querySelector('.fr-ebay-market-picker');
    if (menu) menu.hidden = true;
    if (trigger) trigger.setAttribute('aria-expanded', 'false');
    if (picker) picker.classList.remove('fr-ebay-market-picker--open');
  }
  function availableMarkets(section) {
    var select = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-select]');
    if (select) {
      return Array.prototype.slice.call(select.options)
        .map(function (option) { return option.value; })
        .filter(function (marketId) { return !!markets[marketId]; });
    }
    return Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]'))
      .map(function (button) { return button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button'); })
      .filter(function (marketId) { return !!markets[marketId]; });
  }
  function applyMarket(section, marketId, persist) {
    var available = availableMarkets(section);
    if (available.indexOf(marketId)< 0) marketId = available[0] || defaultMarket;
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-localized-link]')).forEach(function (link) {
      var query = link.getAttribute('data-ebay-query') || '';
      var reference = link.getAttribute('data-ebay-reference') || '';
      link.setAttribute('href', buildUrl(query, reference, marketId));
    });
    var select = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-select]');
    if (select && available.indexOf(marketId) >= 0) select.value = marketId;
    var selectedText = marketId;
    if (select && select.selectedIndex >= 0) selectedText = select.options[select.selectedIndex].text;
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-selected-market-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = selectedText;
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-selected-market-flag], [data-ebay-trigger-market-flag]')).forEach(function (flag) {
      flag.className = flagClass(marketId);
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-trigger-market-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = selectedText;
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-domain-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = domainLabel(marketId);
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-option]')).forEach(function (button) {
      button.setAttribute('aria-selected', button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-option') === marketId ? 'true' : 'false');
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      var active = button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button') === marketId;
      button.setAttribute('aria-pressed', active ? 'true' : 'false');
    });
    section.setAttribute('data-ebay-selected-market', marketId);
    if (persist) {
      try { if (window.localStorage) localStorage.setItem('phoenix-ebay-market', marketId); } catch (err) {}
    }
    closeMenu(section);
  }
  sections.forEach(function (section) {
    if (section.getAttribute('data-ebay-localized-init') === '1') return;
    section.setAttribute('data-ebay-localized-init', '1');
    var available = availableMarkets(section);
    var marketId = defaultMarket;
    if (window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation && window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation.inferMarket) {
      marketId = window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation.inferMarket(available, {
        storageKey: 'phoenix-ebay-market',
        defaultMarket: defaultMarket
      });
    } else if (available.indexOf(defaultMarket)< 0) {
      marketId = available[0] || defaultMarket;
    }
    var select = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-select]');
    if (select) {
      select.addEventListener('change', function () {
        applyMarket(section, select.value, true);
      });
    }
    var trigger = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-trigger]');
    var menu = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-menu]');
    if (trigger && menu) {
      trigger.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
        event.stopPropagation();
        var expanded = trigger.getAttribute('aria-expanded') === 'true';
        menu.hidden = expanded;
        trigger.setAttribute('aria-expanded', expanded ? 'false' : 'true');
        var picker = trigger.closest('.fr-ebay-market-picker');
        if (picker) picker.classList.toggle('fr-ebay-market-picker--open', !expanded);
      });
      Array.prototype.slice.call(menu.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-option]')).forEach(function (button) {
        button.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
          event.stopPropagation();
          applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-option'), true);
        });
      });
      document.addEventListener('click', function () { closeMenu(section); });
    }
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      button.addEventListener('click', function () {
        applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button'), true);
      });
    });
    applyMarket(section, marketId, false);
  });
})();
</script>
