## Introduction

St Bennet’s Well, near Cromarty on the Moray Firth shore, is one of [Cromartyshire]({{ 'where-cromartyshire-folklore-meets-sea/' | relative_url }})’s most concentrated folklore sites: a holy spring, a lost chapel, rag offerings for healing, and a destroyed “Fairies’ Cradle” used in changeling belief all meet in one small coastal landscape. The tradition is not just a vague “magic well” story. [Hugh Miller]({{ 'hugh-miller/' | relative_url }}) recorded that sick people drank the water and left rags on a thorn-bush by the spring, while a nearby stone trough was believed to restore a child supposedly taken by fairies. Later antiquarian and heritage records confirm that the well remained known as a rag-well into the twentieth century, even after the chapel and the cradle had vanished or become archaeologically uncertain.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/files/71325/71325-h/71325-h.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[gutenberg.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Project Gutenberg</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Scenes and Legends of the North of Scotland &#124; Project Gutenberg...</span></span></span>



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/Cromartyshire_1a46f2_st_bennets_well_594464-overview.webp" | relative_url }}" alt="Overview image for Holy Well" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

That mixture matters because it shows how Cromartyshire folklore works at ground level. Christian devotion, local healing, fairy fear and family anxiety were not separate categories. At St Bennet’s Well they formed one practical system: drink, leave a piece of clothing, trust the saint, confront the fairies, and hope that sickness or mischief would be turned away.

## The Well, Chapel and Shore Setting

St Bennet’s Well is located in Cromarty parish, close to the shore south-east of Cromarty town. The Saints in Scottish Place-Names database gives the well’s position as NH 792 650 and notes that it lies about 500 metres east of Navity, a place-name which may point to early ecclesiastical connections. In modern administrative and heritage databases it is usually treated under Ross-shire or Ross and Cromarty, but for a historic-county folklore project its centre of gravity is Cromartyshire: it belongs to the Cromarty parish and coastal Black Isle portion of the old shire.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://saintsplaces.gla.ac.uk/place.php?id=1314711235" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: saintsplaces.gla.ac.uk">[Saints in Scottish Place-Names]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">saintsplaces.gla.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on gla.ac.uk.</span></span></span>

The site’s physical setting helps explain the story. M. D. Hiley’s 1935 note in *Antiquity* places St Bennet’s Well near the high-water mark on the Moray Firth shore, about one and a half miles south-south-east of Cromarty, at the foot of a small glen running inland from the coast. The Highland Historic Environment Record identifies the place as St Bennet’s Chapel and Well and records the well at NH 7923 6502, with the chapel site close by.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/D1039ADE5E06BD3363F843465D58F5B7/S0003598X00010942a.pdf/notes_and_news.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridge.org">[Cambridge University Press &amp; Assessment]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">University Press &amp; Assessment</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">University Press &amp; Assessment</span></span></span>

The chapel is much less secure than the spring. Hugh Miller wrote that, before the Reformation, Cromarty parish had several chapels, two of which were dedicated to St Duthac and St Bennet, and that springs near the ruins still bore the saints’ names. The Highland record, however, says that “nothing is known” of St Bennet’s Chapel and that by the 1966 Ordnance Survey visit there was no trace and no local knowledge of it, though earlier sources reported a heap of stones and grassy mound in 1834. The Saints in Scottish Place-Names entry is even more cautious, stating that there is no archaeological evidence for the chapel, while still recording the historic place-name from the nineteenth-century Ordnance Survey.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/files/71325/71325-h/71325-h.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[gutenberg.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Project Gutenberg</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Scenes and Legends of the North of Scotland &#124; Project Gutenberg...</span></span></span>

That uncertainty does not weaken the folklore value of the site. It sharpens it. The well is the durable element: water still had a name, a ritual use and a remembered saint long after the building associated with it had faded. In a coastal landscape where the tide, a glen, a spring and a ruined chapel met, people had a ready-made threshold place: not quite church, not quite wild shore, not quite domestic ground.

## Rag Offerings and Healing Customs

The best early description of the healing practice comes from Hugh Miller’s *Scenes and Legends of the North of Scotland; or, The Traditional History of Cromarty*. Miller says that, within living memory of his writing, a thorn-bush forming a canopy over St Bennet’s spring was renewed every season with small pieces of rag. These were left by sick people who came to drink the water as offerings to the saint.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/files/71325/71325-h/71325-h.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Scenes and Legends of the North of Scotland &#124; Project Gutenberg...</span></span></span>

This is a classic rag-well pattern, but the Cromarty detail is unusually vivid. The healing act had three linked parts: the sufferer came to the spring, drank from it, and left something personal behind. In Hiley’s 1935 report, the rule was still remembered: to secure the wish, the visitor had to drink the water and leave something of their own clothing. Hiley also saw a mixed collection of rags hanging on the branches when she visited.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/D1039ADE5E06BD3363F843465D58F5B7/S0003598X00010942a.pdf/notes_and_news.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridge.org">[Cambridge University Press &amp; Assessment]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">University Press &amp; Assessment</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">University Press &amp; Assessment</span></span></span>

The Highland Historic Environment Record confirms that St Bennet’s Well was still regarded as a rag-well in the twentieth century. It cites Hiley for offerings still being left in 1935, then records that the natural spring was still being used as a rag-well when visited by the Ordnance Survey in January 1966. The University of Glasgow place-name entry repeats that the well was still used as a clootie or rag well in 1966.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://her.highland.gov.uk/Monument/MHG8829" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: her.highland.gov.uk">[Highland Historic Environment Record]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">her.highland.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on highland.gov.uk.</span></span></span>

The mechanism behind such customs is often described as transfer. A rag, cloth or piece of clothing stands in for the sick person, the pain, or the petition. At Munlochy Clootie Well, elsewhere on the Black Isle, Forestry and Land Scotland explains the common belief that a cloth dipped in the well and tied to a tree allows sickness to fade as the cloth decays. St Bennet’s Well does not need to be forced into exactly that modern explanation, but it plainly belongs to the same wider Highland and Scottish family of healing wells where water, fabric, trees and hope work together.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://forestryandland.gov.scot/visit/destinations/munlochy-clootie-well" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: forestryandland.gov.scot">[Forestry and Land Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">forestryandland.gov.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Forestry and Land Scotland Munlochy Clootie Well Forest Visitor Guide &#124; FLS</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Forestry and Land Scotland Munlochy Clootie Well Forest Visitor Guide &#124; FLS</span></span></span>

What makes St Bennet’s Well distinctive is the saintly frame. The rags were not only “folk medicine” in a narrow sense. Miller says they were offerings to St Bennet, and the well’s name kept the saint in the ritual even after the chapel’s physical evidence grew faint. That creates a layered tradition: Christian on the surface, older-looking in its use of spring, thorn and offering, and deeply local in the way it was tied to a particular Cromarty shore.

## The Fairies’ Cradle and Changeling Belief

The strangest part of the St Bennet’s Well complex was not the spring itself but the lost stone trough called the Fairies’ Cradle. Miller places it near St Bennet’s Chapel, which he describes as perched on a steep solitary ridge above the Moray Firth. The trough, he says, was thought to draw its power from the saint like the well did. If fairies had carried away a child and left “some mischievous unthriving imp” in its place, the parents only had to lay the changeling in the trough and their own child would be restored by an invisible process.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/files/71325/71325-h/71325-h.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Scenes and Legends of the North of Scotland &#124; Project Gutenberg...</span></span></span>

That story is disturbing to modern readers because changeling beliefs often arose around vulnerable children: babies who were ill, not thriving, disabled, unusually quiet, unusually restless, or simply hard to understand in a world before modern medicine. The St Bennet’s version is not a fairy tale about adventure. It is a family crisis story. The parents fear that the child in the house is not truly theirs, and the landscape offers a ritual answer.

The “cradle” also changes the well’s meaning. The spring healed sickness through drinking and rag offerings; the trough addressed fairy replacement through exposure to a saintly object. In other words, St Bennet’s place had a double mechanism. Ordinary illness went to the water and the thorn. Fairy mischief went to the stone.

Miller says the Fairies’ Cradle was destroyed shortly before the 1745 Jacobite rising by Mr Gordon, minister of the parish, and two elders. Later summaries repeat the same tradition: the Highland Historic Environment Record notes that a stone trough named the “Fairy’s Cradle” stood nearby until about 1745, when it was destroyed, and the nineteenth-century *Folklore of Scottish Lochs and Springs* also repeats Miller’s account of its use and destruction.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/files/71325/71325-h/71325-h.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[gutenberg.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Project Gutenberg</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Scenes and Legends of the North of Scotland &#124; Project Gutenberg...</span></span></span>

That destruction is a key part of the folklore, not just an antiquarian footnote. It shows the clash between popular healing practice and post-Reformation religious discipline. The minister and elders did not merely preach against the trough; in the story they physically broke it. The action reads like an attempt to end a ritual that church authority regarded as superstition, especially because it involved fairies, saints and a curative object outside formal worship.

## Why the Tradition Survived Without the Cradle

The Fairies’ Cradle disappeared, but the well tradition outlived it. This split is revealing. A stone trough could be smashed by a minister and elders; a spring was harder to abolish. A thorn-bush could be stripped, cut back or replaced, but water emerging at the foot of the glen remained available. The physical resilience of the spring helped the custom endure after the more controversial changeling ritual had been suppressed.

The evidence also suggests that different parts of the site survived in different kinds of memory. Miller preserved the cradle as a remembered story from roughly eighty years before his own account. Hiley preserved the living rag-well custom in 1935. The Ordnance Survey and Highland records then preserved the site as heritage data in 1966 and later. Each stage changed the tradition’s form: oral belief became literary description, lived ritual became antiquarian note, and both became mapped heritage.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/files/71325/71325-h/71325-h.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[gutenberg.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Project Gutenberg</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Scenes and Legends of the North of Scotland &#124; Project Gutenberg...</span></span></span>

Hiley’s account also adds a separate strand of local moral memory. She was told by Francis Scott that the site was locally supposed to be a place of judgement, that the ground was said to be cursed because it had been stolen from the Church, and that an owner had to provide eight hundredweight of oatmeal each Christmas for the parish poor under an obligation said to have been active since 1630. This does not prove the folklore explanation of the landholding, but it shows how St Bennet’s Well attracted stories about justice, debt, charity and sacred property as well as healing.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/D1039ADE5E06BD3363F843465D58F5B7/S0003598X00010942a.pdf/notes_and_news.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridge.org">[Cambridge University Press &amp; Assessment]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">University Press &amp; Assessment</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">University Press &amp; Assessment</span></span></span>

For a reader trying to understand Cromartyshire folklore, that is the point: St Bennet’s Well was not a single-purpose “wishing well”. It was a place where bodily illness, child anxiety, ecclesiastical memory and local ideas of judgement clustered together.

## How Old and Well-Attested Is the Story?

The safest answer is that the tradition is well attested from the nineteenth century onward, with some elements remembered as older. Miller’s book was first published in 1835, and his account places the rag custom within recent local memory while placing the Fairies’ Cradle roughly eighty years further back. The cradle’s destruction is tied to the period shortly before 1745, but that date comes through tradition, not from a surviving official record of the act.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/files/71325/71325-h/71325-h.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Scenes and Legends of the North of Scotland &#124; Project Gutenberg...</span></span></span>

The well itself is more securely mapped than the chapel or the cradle. Saints in Scottish Place-Names records St Bennet’s Well as a current Ordnance Survey form and notes a nineteenth-century Ordnance Survey first-edition citation. The chapel name is also recorded from the same OS source, but the chapel is marked as obsolete and archaeologically unproven. The Highland Historic Environment Record likewise treats the chapel as a site with no surviving trace, while recording the well, the rag-well custom and the former trough tradition.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://saintsplaces.gla.ac.uk/place.php?id=1314711235" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: saintsplaces.gla.ac.uk">[Saints in Scottish Place-Names]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">saintsplaces.gla.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on gla.ac.uk.</span></span></span>

This means the St Bennet’s tradition should be presented neither as an invented modern ghost story nor as a fully documented medieval rite. It sits in the more interesting middle ground common to much Highland folklore: a real named place, a local nineteenth-century collector, twentieth-century confirmation of continuing custom, and earlier details preserved through remembered tradition.

## Why St Bennet’s Well Matters in Cromartyshire Folklore

St Bennet’s Well matters because it condenses several Cromartyshire themes into one place. Hugh Miller’s Cromarty is full of shorelines, ruined religious sites, fairy fears and old customs caught at the moment when they were fading. This well gives those themes a practical form. It is not only a story told by the fire; it is a place where people went, drank, tied cloth, and imagined illness or fairy harm being transferred away.

It also links Cromartyshire with wider Highland holy-well culture without losing its local character. The rag-offering custom has parallels across Scotland, including the well-known Munlochy Clootie Well, where cloth, water and healing remain part of public interpretation today. But St Bennet’s Well adds the lost Fairies’ Cradle, the Cromarty chapel tradition and Miller’s local voice, making it more than a generic clootie-well example.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://forestryandland.gov.scot/visit/destinations/munlochy-clootie-well" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: forestryandland.gov.scot">[Forestry and Land Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">forestryandland.gov.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Forestry and Land Scotland Munlochy Clootie Well Forest Visitor Guide &#124; FLS</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Forestry and Land Scotland Munlochy Clootie Well Forest Visitor Guide &#124; FLS</span></span></span>

The site has even entered modern creative retelling. Composer and vocalist Rylan Gleave’s 2022 album *Lawn Crypt*, written in Cromarty, includes “Cradlesong”, whose text draws directly on the St Bennet’s Chapel, holy well and changeling-trough tradition. That is a good example of how Cromarty folklore now circulates: not as an unchanged belief system, but as a source for music, heritage, walking, local identity and atmospheric storytelling.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://rylangleave.bandcamp.com/album/lawn-crypt" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: rylangleave.bandcamp.com">[Rylan Gleave]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">rylangleave.bandcamp.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Rylan Gleave Lawn Crypt &#124; Rylan Gleave</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Rylan Gleave Lawn Crypt &#124; Rylan Gleave</span></span></span>

The most honest way to answer the page’s central question, “Did St Bennet’s Well cure fairy mischief?”, is therefore this: people in Cromarty tradition believed the saint’s spring and the nearby Fairies’ Cradle could address sickness and changeling danger, but the surviving evidence shows belief and practice, not supernatural proof. The value of the tradition lies in what it reveals about how Cromartyshire people once made sense of illness, childhood vulnerability, holy places and the unseen powers thought to move through the landscape.
