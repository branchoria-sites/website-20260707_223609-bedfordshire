## Introduction

James Hogg matters to [Selkirkshire]({{ 'where-selkirkshire-turns-story-into/' | relative_url }}) folklore because he stands at the meeting point between living Ettrick song culture and printed Border literature. Born in the Ettrick Valley in 1770, remembered as “the Ettrick Shepherd”, Hogg grew up among ballads, tales, supernatural anecdotes and local memory before becoming a poet, novelist, song-maker, folklore collector and informant for Walter Scott’s *Minstrelsy of the Scottish Border*.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://jameshogg.stir.ac.uk/james-hogg/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: jameshogg.stir.ac.uk">[jameshogg.stir.ac.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">jameshogg.stir.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">james hogg</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">13 Feb 2017 — Ballads from Hogg&#x27;s family, and collected by the writer, appear in Walter Scott&#x27;s Minstrelsy of the Scottish Border, and Ho...</span></span></span>



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/Selkirkshire_8fd2aa_james_hogg_ettrick_8ccceb-overview.webp" | relative_url }}" alt="Overview image for James Hogg" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

His importance is not simply that he “preserved” old material. He also reshaped it. Hogg carried the voices of Ettrick and [Yarrow]({{ 'yarrow/' | relative_url }}) into print, but he did so as a creative writer with ambitions of his own. That makes him one of the key figures for understanding Selkirkshire’s folklore identity: not a neutral recorder of a vanished past, and not merely an inventor, but a local writer formed by oral tradition who helped turn that tradition into modern Border culture.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://eprints.gla.ac.uk/65953/1/65963.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: eprints.gla.ac.uk">[Enlighten Publications]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">eprints.gla.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Enlighten Publications Hogg, Ettrick, and Oral Tradition</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Enlighten PublicationsHogg, Ettrick, and Oral Tradition - Enlighten PublicationsJune 29, 2012 — by V Bold · 2012 · Cited by 7 — (10) Jame...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 29, 2012</span></span></span>

## Hogg’s Ettrick and Yarrow roots

Hogg’s folklore inheritance begins in place. Ettrick and Yarrow are not decorative background in his story; they are the landscape that supplied his speech, songs, work rhythms and remembered tales. Local accounts place his birth at a small cottage on Ettrickhall Farm at the top of the Ettrick Valley, and the Ettrick and Yarrow community exhibition stresses that he lived in either Ettrick or Yarrow for most of his life.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://scotlandstartshere.com/tours/the-james-hogg-trail/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: scotlandstartshere.com">[Scotland Starts Here]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">scotlandstartshere.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Scotland Starts HereThe James Hogg Trail &#124; South of ScotlandJames Hogg was born in a small cottage on Ettrickhall Farm at the top of the...</span></span></span>

This matters for Selkirkshire because Hogg’s “Border” tradition was not a vague national mood. It was rooted in upland sheep country, farm service, family singing, winter storytelling and the old cultural memory of Ettrick Forest. Hogg had little formal schooling, but he inherited what the local exhibition calls a “rich and vivid tradition of ballad and legend”; that inheritance became the raw material of his public career.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ettrickandyarrow.org.uk/james-hogg-exhibition" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ettrickandyarrow.org.uk">[ettrickandyarrow.org.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ettrickandyarrow.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on ettrickandyarrow.org.uk.</span></span></span>

The family line is especially important. Hogg’s mother, Margaret Laidlaw, is now recognised not just as “the Ettrick Shepherd’s mother” but as a tradition-bearer in her own right. Scottish Women Writers on the Web describes her as someone who passed on folklore orally in Selkirkshire and in print through the work of her son and through ballads supplied to Walter Scott.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.scottishwomenwritersontheweb.net/writers-a-to-z/anna-buchan-4egsw" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: scottishwomenwritersontheweb.net">[Scottish Women Writers on the Web]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">scottishwomenwritersontheweb.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Scottish Women Writers on the Web Margaret Laidlaw</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Scottish Women Writers on the Web Margaret Laidlaw</span></span></span>

That family setting helps explain why Hogg’s later writing can feel so close to oral performance. He was not simply studying folklore from the outside. He had grown up with songs and tales as part of domestic and working life. The University of Glasgow chapter “Hogg, Ettrick, and Oral Tradition” puts the point plainly: Hogg’s work is embedded in the oral traditions of Selkirkshire, especially the Ettrick valley and its surroundings.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://eprints.gla.ac.uk/65953/1/65963.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: eprints.gla.ac.uk">[Enlighten Publications]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">eprints.gla.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Enlighten Publications Hogg, Ettrick, and Oral Tradition</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Enlighten PublicationsHogg, Ettrick, and Oral Tradition - Enlighten PublicationsJune 29, 2012 — by V Bold · 2012 · Cited by 7 — (10) Jame...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 29, 2012</span></span></span>

For a reader tracing Selkirkshire folklore on the map, Hogg therefore belongs beside Carterhaugh, Yarrow, St Mary’s Loch and Ettrick Forest. His contribution is not a single legend-site like [Tam Lin]({{ 'tam-lin/' | relative_url }})’s Carterhaugh, but a whole channel through which local song, fairy belief, family memory and Border history entered print.

## Ballad collecting with Walter Scott

The most famous hinge in Hogg’s folklore career is his connection with Walter Scott. Scott’s *Minstrelsy of the Scottish Border* grew out of years of collecting Border ballads, and the Walter Scott Digital Archive notes that Scott began collecting seriously in the Borders from the 1790s.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.walterscott.lib.ed.ac.uk/works/poetry/minstrelsy.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: walterscott.lib.ed.ac.uk">[Walter Scott Digital Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">walterscott.lib.ed.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on ed.ac.uk.</span></span></span> Hogg entered this world as a local informant and collaborator, bringing with him material from Ettrick and his own family circle.

The University of Edinburgh’s Walter Scott site says Hogg supplied Scott with poetry and ballads that appeared, “albeit altered”, in the *Minstrelsy*. That small qualification is crucial. The printed ballads were not sound recordings of oral performance. They were edited texts, shaped by antiquarian taste, literary expectation and the practical demands of publication.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.walterscott.lib.ed.ac.uk/biography/hogg.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: walterscott.lib.ed.ac.uk">[Walter Scott Digital Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">walterscott.lib.ed.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Walter Scott Digital ArchiveSir Walter Scott and James Hogg, the Ettrick ShepherdHogg was a shepherd working on the land of Scott&#x27;s frien...</span></span></span>

The best-known example is “Auld Maitland”, associated with Hogg’s mother, Margaret Laidlaw. Hogg later told the famous story of Margaret rebuking Scott for printing and thereby “spoiling” songs that were made for singing rather than reading. The anecdote has become one of the sharpest folk-culture moments in the Scott-Hogg story because it dramatises the change from voice to page: a living song, carried by memory and performance, becomes a literary artefact.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://eprints.gla.ac.uk/65953/1/65963.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: eprints.gla.ac.uk">[Enlighten Publications]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">eprints.gla.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Enlighten Publications Hogg, Ettrick, and Oral Tradition</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Enlighten PublicationsHogg, Ettrick, and Oral Tradition - Enlighten PublicationsJune 29, 2012 — by V Bold · 2012 · Cited by 7 — (10) Jame...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 29, 2012</span></span></span>

For Selkirkshire folklore, this is a useful warning against treating printed ballads as simple windows into the past. Hogg’s Ettrick material is valuable precisely because it is close to oral tradition, but it often reaches us through layers: a singer’s memory, Hogg’s reporting, Scott’s editing, later editions, and modern scholarship. The tradition survives, but not untouched.

Hogg’s relationship with Scott was also more than a single act of collection. The James Hogg Society notes that ballads from Hogg’s family and collected by Hogg appear in Scott’s *Minstrelsy*, while Hogg’s own *Familiar Anecdotes of Sir Walter Scott* gives an unusually intimate portrait of their long, sometimes strained friendship.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://jameshogg.stir.ac.uk/james-hogg/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: jameshogg.stir.ac.uk">[jameshogg.stir.ac.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">jameshogg.stir.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">james hogg</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">13 Feb 2017 — Ballads from Hogg&#x27;s family, and collected by the writer, appear in Walter Scott&#x27;s Minstrelsy of the Scottish Border, and Ho...</span></span></span> The collaboration placed Ettrick song culture inside one of the most influential printed collections of Border tradition, but it also left lasting questions about class, authorship and who gets credit for oral knowledge.

## Oral tradition, literature and local memory

Hogg’s inheritance was not just something he handed over to Scott. He turned it into his own literature. His first substantial poetic publication, *The Mountain Bard* in 1807, was presented as a collection of ballads and songs “founded on facts and legendary tales”, and later scholarship has stressed how Hogg adapted traditional forms for readers who might never hear an Ettrick singer perform them.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Mountain_Bard" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Mountain Bard</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Mountain Bard</span></span></span>

His breakthrough came with *The Queen’s Wake* in 1813, a “legendary poem” built around an imagined contest of Scottish bards before Mary, Queen of Scots. Modern scholarship notes that the poem plays with the idea of survival through oral tradition: the Ettrick Shepherd figure can only recite the songs supposedly preserved from a larger lost body of bardic performance.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1057/9781137559050_3" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: link.springer.com">[Springer Link]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">link.springer.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Link The Queen&#x27;s Wake, National Epic and Imagined Ancestries</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Link The Queen&#x27;s Wake, National Epic and Imagined Ancestries</span></span></span> That framing made Hogg’s own persona part of the folklore machinery: the shepherd-poet becomes the living bridge between old Scotland and the modern reader.

This is where Hogg differs from a straightforward collector. He used the authority of tradition, but he also invented, revised, arranged and performed it. His later *Songs, by The Ettrick Shepherd*, published in 1831, gathered 113 songs from across his career, and modern editors have treated it as a major part of his work as a song-maker rather than merely an antiquarian archive.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.thebottleimp.org.uk/2011/05/posing-as-the-ultimate-scottish-songster-james-hoggs-songs-by-the-ettrick-shepherd-or-hogg-gold/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: thebottleimp.org.uk">[The Bottle Imp]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">thebottleimp.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Bottle Imp James Hogg&#x27;s &#x27;Songs by the Ettrick Shepherd&#x27; or Hogg GOLD!</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Bottle Imp James Hogg&#x27;s &#x27;Songs by the Ettrick Shepherd&#x27; or Hogg GOLD!</span></span></span>

That creative role does not make Hogg less important to folklore. It makes him more interesting. Folklore is often reshaped as it moves between tellers, singers, collectors, publishers and audiences. Hogg’s work shows that process in full view. In him, Selkirkshire tradition is not frozen as “authentic old belief”; it is heard, remembered, improved, argued over, printed, performed and reimagined.

The Library of Congress, in material for its Hogg symposium, describes him as an influential song-maker, folklore collector, novelist and poet who helped create and promote Scottish culture beyond Scotland.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.loc.gov/item/2024696872/?embed=resources" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: loc.gov">[The Library of Congress]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">loc.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on loc.gov.</span></span></span> That international afterlife began in the local particularity of Ettrick. The more widely Hogg travelled in print, the more strongly his public identity depended on the place-name “Ettrick”.

## What did Hogg preserve?

Hogg’s ballad inheritance should be understood in several overlapping layers.

**Family repertoire.** Margaret Laidlaw and the Hogg family circle supplied songs and tales that connected Ettrick households to older Border memory. Her importance has been reassessed in recent women’s literary history, which treats her as a tradition-bearer rather than a footnote to male writers.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.scottishwomenwritersontheweb.net/writers-a-to-z/anna-buchan-4egsw" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: scottishwomenwritersontheweb.net">[Scottish Women Writers on the Web]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">scottishwomenwritersontheweb.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Scottish Women Writers on the Web Margaret Laidlaw</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Scottish Women Writers on the Web Margaret Laidlaw</span></span></span>

**Local performance culture.** Hogg belonged to a world where songs were sung socially, not simply read privately. The famous complaint attributed to Margaret Laidlaw about songs being “made for singing” captures the difference between oral performance and printed text.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://eprints.gla.ac.uk/65953/1/65963.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: eprints.gla.ac.uk">[Enlighten Publications]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">eprints.gla.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Enlighten Publications Hogg, Ettrick, and Oral Tradition</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Enlighten PublicationsHogg, Ettrick, and Oral Tradition - Enlighten PublicationsJune 29, 2012 — by V Bold · 2012 · Cited by 7 — (10) Jame...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 29, 2012</span></span></span>

**Border historical memory.** Much of the tradition around Hogg draws on the long afterlife of reiving, family conflict, old marches, raids, loyalties and losses. In a county such as Selkirkshire, where landscape and memory are tightly joined, ballads helped turn local history into emotional geography.

**Supernatural and legendary material.** Hogg’s Ettrick world included fairies, ghosts, brownies and other forms of belief culture. The point is not that every tale he touched was ancient or literally believed, but that supernatural narrative belonged naturally to the same song-and-story environment as family history and Border violence.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://eprints.gla.ac.uk/65953/1/65963.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: eprints.gla.ac.uk">[Enlighten Publications]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">eprints.gla.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Enlighten Publications Hogg, Ettrick, and Oral Tradition</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Enlighten PublicationsHogg, Ettrick, and Oral Tradition - Enlighten PublicationsJune 29, 2012 — by V Bold · 2012 · Cited by 7 — (10) Jame...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 29, 2012</span></span></span>

**A public image of the folk poet.** Hogg himself became part of tradition. The “Ettrick Shepherd” was a literary persona, a public nickname, and eventually a local heritage figure. Modern Hogg studies repeatedly note the tension between Hogg the serious author and Hogg the rustic character imagined by metropolitan readers.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.erudit.org/en/journals/ron/2000-n19-ron431/005934ar/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: erudit.org">[Érudit]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">erudit.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on erudit.org.</span></span></span>

## Why the Ettrick inheritance is not simple “authentic folklore”

One of the most useful things Hogg offers a modern folklore reader is a case study in difficulty. His work is close to living tradition, but it is also literary, self-conscious and often playful. That means the right question is not “is this pure folklore or invention?” but “how did oral tradition and literary craft interact here?”

Hogg’s reliability as a source has been questioned and reassessed. The Glasgow study of Hogg and oral tradition notes both his deep familiarity with Ettrick culture and his ambivalent attitude towards it, shaped partly by his desire for literary and economic success.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://eprints.gla.ac.uk/65953/1/65963.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: eprints.gla.ac.uk">[Enlighten Publications]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">eprints.gla.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Enlighten Publications Hogg, Ettrick, and Oral Tradition</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Enlighten PublicationsHogg, Ettrick, and Oral Tradition - Enlighten PublicationsJune 29, 2012 — by V Bold · 2012 · Cited by 7 — (10) Jame...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 29, 2012</span></span></span> He knew the old material intimately, but he also wanted to succeed in the world of publishers, reviewers and polite readers.

Scott presents a similar problem from another angle. His *Minstrelsy* made Border ballads famous, but it did so through editorial intervention. The University of Edinburgh’s phrase “albeit altered” is a neat reminder that the printed page is evidence, not a transparent copy of oral song.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.walterscott.lib.ed.ac.uk/biography/hogg.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: walterscott.lib.ed.ac.uk">[Walter Scott Digital Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">walterscott.lib.ed.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Walter Scott Digital ArchiveSir Walter Scott and James Hogg, the Ettrick ShepherdHogg was a shepherd working on the land of Scott&#x27;s frien...</span></span></span>

This does not weaken Selkirkshire’s folklore claim. It makes it more honest. In Hogg’s case, the inheritance is valuable because it shows a tradition in motion at a moment of change: rural oral culture meeting Romantic publishing, local memory becoming national literature, and singers’ repertoires being transformed into books.

## Why Hogg still matters in Selkirkshire

Hogg remains central to the cultural identity of Ettrick and Yarrow because he gives the district a named human link between landscape, oral tradition and literature. The Ettrick and Yarrow exhibition describes how the James Hogg collection developed from commemorative activity in the Borders, moved through Aikwood Tower and Bowhill, and eventually “come home” to the Ettrick and Yarrow area.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ettrickandyarrow.org.uk/james-hogg-exhibition" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ettrickandyarrow.org.uk">[ettrickandyarrow.org.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ettrickandyarrow.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on ettrickandyarrow.org.uk.</span></span></span>

Modern remembrance also keeps returning to the landscape. The James Hogg Trail presents his life through places associated with Ettrick, Yarrow and the surrounding Borders, turning literary biography into a route through country that shaped the work.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://scotlandstartshere.com/tours/the-james-hogg-trail/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: scotlandstartshere.com">[Scotland Starts Here]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">scotlandstartshere.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Scotland Starts HereThe James Hogg Trail &#124; South of ScotlandJames Hogg was born in a small cottage on Ettrickhall Farm at the top of the...</span></span></span> For visitors, that matters because Hogg is best understood not as a detached name in Scottish literature, but as someone whose reputation is tied to valleys, farms, hills, songs and local memory.

The continuing academic interest is strong too. The University of Stirling’s James Hogg research pages, the James Hogg Society, and the Stirling/South Carolina research edition of his collected works all show that Hogg has moved far beyond the old stereotype of a colourful rustic poet.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://jameshogg.stir.ac.uk/james-hogg/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: jameshogg.stir.ac.uk">[jameshogg.stir.ac.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">jameshogg.stir.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">james hogg</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">13 Feb 2017 — Ballads from Hogg&#x27;s family, and collected by the writer, appear in Walter Scott&#x27;s Minstrelsy of the Scottish Border, and Ho...</span></span></span> He is now studied as a major Scottish writer whose folklore inheritance shaped poetry, song, fiction and national cultural memory.

For a folklore map of Selkirkshire, Hogg’s value is therefore different from a single ghost story or fairy hill. He explains how such traditions travel. Through him, Ettrick song and story passed from hearth, hillside and local singer into Scott’s Border antiquarianism, Hogg’s own literary experiments, later musical collections, heritage trails, exhibitions and modern scholarship.

## The local takeaway

James Hogg’s Ettrick ballad inheritance is the story of a county tradition crossing a threshold. On one side is oral Selkirkshire: family singers, shepherds, local tales, supernatural belief, Border memories and songs carried by performance. On the other side is printed Border folklore: Scott’s *Minstrelsy*, Hogg’s poems and songs, edited collections, literary fame and heritage retelling.

Hogg stands in the doorway between them. He preserved material that might otherwise have been lost, but he also changed it by writing it down, reshaping it and attaching it to the public identity of “the Ettrick Shepherd”. That is why he remains one of Selkirkshire’s most important folklore figures: not because he gives us untouched ancient song, but because he shows how local tradition survives by being remembered, argued over, adapted and sung again.

    if (select && select.selectedIndex >= 0) selectedText = select.options[select.selectedIndex].text;
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-selected-market-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = selectedText;
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-selected-market-flag], [data-ebay-trigger-market-flag]')).forEach(function (flag) {
      flag.className = flagClass(marketId);
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-trigger-market-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = selectedText;
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-domain-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = domainLabel(marketId);
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-option]')).forEach(function (button) {
      button.setAttribute('aria-selected', button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-option') === marketId ? 'true' : 'false');
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      var active = button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button') === marketId;
      button.setAttribute('aria-pressed', active ? 'true' : 'false');
    });
    section.setAttribute('data-ebay-selected-market', marketId);
    if (persist) {
      try { if (window.localStorage) localStorage.setItem('phoenix-ebay-market', marketId); } catch (err) {}
    }
    closeMenu(section);
  }
  sections.forEach(function (section) {
    if (section.getAttribute('data-ebay-localized-init') === '1') return;
    section.setAttribute('data-ebay-localized-init', '1');
    var available = availableMarkets(section);
    var marketId = defaultMarket;
    if (window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation && window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation.inferMarket) {
      marketId = window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation.inferMarket(available, {
        storageKey: 'phoenix-ebay-market',
        defaultMarket: defaultMarket
      });
    } else if (available.indexOf(defaultMarket)< 0) {
      marketId = available[0] || defaultMarket;
    }
    var select = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-select]');
    if (select) {
      select.addEventListener('change', function () {
        applyMarket(section, select.value, true);
      });
    }
    var trigger = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-trigger]');
    var menu = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-menu]');
    if (trigger && menu) {
      trigger.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
        event.stopPropagation();
        var expanded = trigger.getAttribute('aria-expanded') === 'true';
        menu.hidden = expanded;
        trigger.setAttribute('aria-expanded', expanded ? 'false' : 'true');
        var picker = trigger.closest('.fr-ebay-market-picker');
        if (picker) picker.classList.toggle('fr-ebay-market-picker--open', !expanded);
      });
      Array.prototype.slice.call(menu.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-option]')).forEach(function (button) {
        button.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
          event.stopPropagation();
          applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-option'), true);
        });
      });
      document.addEventListener('click', function () { closeMenu(section); });
    }
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      button.addEventListener('click', function () {
        applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button'), true);
      });
    });
    applyMarket(section, marketId, false);
  });
})();
