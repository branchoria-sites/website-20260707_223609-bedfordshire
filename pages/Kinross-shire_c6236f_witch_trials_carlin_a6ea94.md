## Introduction

[Kinross-shire]({{ 'where-kinross-shires-legends-still-cling/' | relative_url }})’s witch traditions have two very different centres of gravity: the documented Crook of Devon witchcraft proceedings of 1662, and the hill legend of Carlin Maggie, the stone witch below Bishop Hill. One belongs to court records, indictments and executions; the other belongs to landscape folklore, walking routes and a striking pillar of rock. Together they show why witchcraft memory in this small county must be handled carefully: some stories preserve real legal violence, while others turn a natural landmark into a supernatural tale.



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/Kinross-shire_c6236f_witch_trials_carlin_a6ea94-overview.webp" | relative_url }}" alt="Overview image for Witches" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

The Crook of Devon trials took place during Scotland’s major witch-hunting wave of 1661–62. Thirteen people were accused in proceedings held at Crook of Devon, and most were sentenced to be strangled and burned at Lamblaires near the village. Carlin Maggie, by contrast, is a 10-metre rock pillar on Bishop Hill, explained in local lore as a witch turned to stone by the Devil. The two traditions are often grouped as “witch stories”, but they matter in different ways: one asks for historical remembrance, the other for folklore interpretation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://dundeescottishculture.org/history/the-crook-of-devon-witches/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: dundeescottishculture.org">[dundeescottishculture.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">dundeescottishculture.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Dundee Scottish Culture The Crook of Devon Witches – Centre for Scottish Culture</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Dundee Scottish Culture The Crook of Devon Witches – Centre for Scottish Culture</span></span></span>

## The Crook of Devon proceedings

The Crook of Devon trials are among the clearest examples of witchcraft history attached to Kinross-shire because they survive in printed legal material rather than only in later legend. R. Burns Begg’s nineteenth-century publication of the trial records, “Notice of Trials for Witchcraft at Crook of Devon, Kinross-shire, in 1662”, gives the basic structure: the court sat on 3 April, 23 April, 5 May, 21 July and 8 October 1662, under Mr Alexander Colville of Blair as Justice-Depute.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://journals.socantscot.org/index.php/psas/article/download/6264/6233/6222" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journals.socantscot.org">[Society of Antiquaries of Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journals.socantscot.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Society of Antiquaries of Scotland</span></span></span>

The accused named in modern summaries of the case were Agnes Murie, Bessie Henderson, Isabel Rutherford, Robert Wilson, Bessie Neil, Margaret Lister, Janet Paton elder and younger, Agnes Brugh, Margaret Hoggin, Janet Brugh, Christian Garvie or Grieve, and Agnes Pittendreich. Dundee University’s Centre for Scottish Culture summarises the trials as four court sittings between April and October, while Begg’s printed record describes five diets of court. That small difference is a useful warning: even in a well-attested case, later retellings may simplify the legal chronology.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://dundeescottishculture.org/history/the-crook-of-devon-witches/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: dundeescottishculture.org">[Dundee Scottish Culture]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">dundeescottishculture.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Dundee Scottish Culture The Crook of Devon Witches – Centre for Scottish Culture</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Dundee Scottish Culture The Crook of Devon Witches – Centre for Scottish Culture</span></span></span>

The sentences were brutal and precise. On 3 April 1662, Agnes Murie, Bessie Henderson and Isabel Rutherford were condemned to be taken to “the Lamblaires, bewest the Cruick Miln” the following day, strangled by the hangman and then burned to ashes. On 23 April, Robert Wilson, Bessie Neil, Margaret Lister, Janet Paton and Agnes Brugh received the same doom. Further convictions followed in May, July and October, including Janet Brugh and Christian Grieve.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://journals.socantscot.org/index.php/psas/article/download/6264/6233/6222" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journals.socantscot.org">[Society of Antiquaries of Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journals.socantscot.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Society of Antiquaries of Scotland</span></span></span>

Two cases stand out because they complicate any neat list of “the Crook of Devon witches”. Margaret Hoggin, described as nearly eighty years old, was not recorded as convicted and may have died before sentence; Begg notes that she was later referred to as deceased. Agnes Pittendreich was pregnant and was released for the time being, with no later proceedings found in the surviving record. Christian Grieve’s case is also unsettling: in July the assize would neither convict nor clear her, but in October the same jury convicted her and she too was sentenced to die.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://journals.socantscot.org/index.php/psas/article/download/6264/6233/6222" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journals.socantscot.org">[Society of Antiquaries of Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journals.socantscot.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Society of Antiquaries of Scotland</span></span></span>

What makes the Crook of Devon material so valuable, and so disturbing, is its everyday texture. The accusations were not only about grand diabolism. They were also about quarrels, illness, failed crops, damaged animals and neighbourly resentments. Dundee’s account notes allegations such as Margaret Lister causing “falling sickness”, Janet Paton cursing a horse, and Agnes Murie striking a man dumb after a dispute over lime for her fields. This is where the folklore of witchcraft meets the social history of a small rural district: misfortune was given a human target.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://dundeescottishculture.org/history/the-crook-of-devon-witches/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: dundeescottishculture.org">[Dundee Scottish Culture]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">dundeescottishculture.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Dundee Scottish Culture The Crook of Devon Witches – Centre for Scottish Culture</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Dundee Scottish Culture The Crook of Devon Witches – Centre for Scottish Culture</span></span></span>

The confessions also contain the expected language of Scottish witchcraft belief: renouncing baptism, giving oneself to Satan, receiving a witch’s mark, attending night meetings and naming accomplices. Begg’s record describes alleged meetings at places such as Gibson’s Craig, Stanriegate, Turfhills, the Bents of Balruddrie and Heathery Brow, with Satan appearing as a man. One confession described a night gathering where people danced and a piper played. These details are valuable as evidence of what interrogators and courts were prepared to accept, not as proof that such events actually happened.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://journals.socantscot.org/index.php/psas/article/download/6264/6233/6222" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journals.socantscot.org">[Society of Antiquaries of Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journals.socantscot.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Society of Antiquaries of Scotland</span></span></span>

## What the trials reveal about fear and authority

The Crook of Devon proceedings sit within a wider Scottish legal culture created by the Witchcraft Act of 1563, under which witchcraft could be punished by death. Historic Environment Scotland explains that the Act emerged in a post-Reformation setting where Church and state sought to enforce godliness and feared that people believed to be in league with the Devil could endanger the whole community.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://blog.historicenvironment.scot/2022/06/the-witchcraft-act-and-its-impact-in-scotland/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: blog.historicenvironment.scot">[Historic Environment Scotland Blog]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">blog.historicenvironment.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on historicenvironment.scot.</span></span></span>

This matters because the Crook of Devon cases were not a spontaneous village scare acting outside authority. The records show a formal court, a judge, a jury, clerks, officers, ministers, lairds and named local men witnessing or supporting proceedings. Tullibole Castle’s local importance is also part of the landscape of the case: Trove, Scotland’s national historic environment portal, records Tullibole Castle in the parish of Fossoway, former county Kinross-shire, and describes it as an early seventeenth-century laird’s house associated with John Halliday in 1608.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://journals.socantscot.org/index.php/psas/article/download/6264/6233/6222" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journals.socantscot.org">[Society of Antiquaries of Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journals.socantscot.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Society of Antiquaries of Scotland</span></span></span>

For modern readers, the key issue is not whether the accused “really believed” parts of their confessions. The safer reading is that confessions in witchcraft trials are documents produced under pressure, shaped by the expectations of examiners, ministers and courts. Dundee’s account points to sleep deprivation as the most likely explanation for the fantastical confessions, while Historic Environment Scotland notes the wider use of coercive methods such as searching for the Devil’s mark and relying on neighbours’ testimony.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://dundeescottishculture.org/history/the-crook-of-devon-witches/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: dundeescottishculture.org">[Dundee Scottish Culture]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">dundeescottishculture.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Dundee Scottish Culture The Crook of Devon Witches – Centre for Scottish Culture</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Dundee Scottish Culture The Crook of Devon Witches – Centre for Scottish Culture</span></span></span>

The Crook of Devon proceedings therefore deserve to be remembered as local history, not romanticised as spooky entertainment. They show how a small district could transform illness, poverty, quarrels and rumour into capital charges. They also show how women were especially vulnerable, though Robert Wilson’s presence reminds us that Scottish witchcraft accusations were not exclusively directed at women. The University of Edinburgh’s Survey of Scottish Witchcraft records nearly 4,000 people accused across early modern Scotland, and Crook of Devon was one local node in that much larger national pattern.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://witches.hca.ed.ac.uk/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: witches.hca.ed.ac.uk">[Witches]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">witches.hca.ed.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on ed.ac.uk.</span></span></span>

## Lamblaires, Tullibole and modern remembrance

The most charged place-name in the Crook of Devon story is Lamblaires. The trial record repeatedly identifies it as the place of execution, west of Cruick Mill. That legal wording turns a field-name into a site of memory: not a vague “somewhere nearby”, but a named execution landscape attached to the village.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://journals.socantscot.org/index.php/psas/article/download/6264/6233/6222" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journals.socantscot.org">[Society of Antiquaries of Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journals.socantscot.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Society of Antiquaries of Scotland</span></span></span>

Modern remembrance has focused strongly on Tullibole and the Witches’ Maze. Dundee’s Centre for Scottish Culture notes that the maze was unveiled at Tullibole Castle in 2012 as a memorial to those indicted at Crook of Devon. Fife Walking describes the Tullibole Witches’ Maze walk as a route from Crook of Devon to a memorial for the eleven people executed in 1662, and warns that Tullibole Castle itself is a private house and function venue rather than a general public attraction.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://dundeescottishculture.org/history/the-crook-of-devon-witches/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: dundeescottishculture.org">[Dundee Scottish Culture]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">dundeescottishculture.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Dundee Scottish Culture The Crook of Devon Witches – Centre for Scottish Culture</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Dundee Scottish Culture The Crook of Devon Witches – Centre for Scottish Culture</span></span></span>

The maze changes the way the story is encountered today. Instead of surviving only in a printed antiquarian paper or a specialist database, the Crook of Devon trials have become part of a walkable memorial landscape. A visitor can connect the village hall area, Lamblaires memory, Tullibole Castle grounds and nearby rural paths. That is powerful, but it also carries a responsibility: the “witches” were not picturesque Halloween figures, but people condemned by a legal system that treated confession, rumour and religious fear as evidence.

There is also a useful distinction between memorial language and historical precision. Many modern accounts speak of “eleven innocent witches” because eleven people were executed. Historically, they were accused people convicted of witchcraft under the law of their time; morally and evidentially, modern scholarship treats those convictions as miscarriages of justice. Calling them “innocent” is therefore understandable in public remembrance, but the word “witches” should be read as a label imposed by accusers and courts, not as a description of what they were.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://dundeescottishculture.org/history/the-crook-of-devon-witches/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: dundeescottishculture.org">[Dundee Scottish Culture]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">dundeescottishculture.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Dundee Scottish Culture The Crook of Devon Witches – Centre for Scottish Culture</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Dundee Scottish Culture The Crook of Devon Witches – Centre for Scottish Culture</span></span></span>

## Carlin Maggie on Bishop Hill

Carlin Maggie is a very different kind of witch tradition. She is not known from a trial record. She is a rock pillar on the upper west-facing slope of Bishop Hill, overlooking the [Loch Leven]({{ 'loch-leven/' | relative_url }}) side of Kinross-shire. Living Lomonds’ “Carlin Maggie Geology Hotspot” leaflet describes her as a 10-metre pillar, an isolated outcrop of dolerite formed by natural erosion of the sill. The same leaflet gives the local legend: Maggie was a witch turned into stone in a contest with the Devil, not to be released until wind and weather set her free.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://d1ssu070pg2v9i.cloudfront.net/pex/fcct/2019/07/02165111/Carlin-Maggie-Geology-Hotspot.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: d1ssu070pg2v9i.cloudfront.net">[Cloudfront]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">d1ssu070pg2v9i.cloudfront.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Carlin Maggie Geology Hotspot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Carlin Maggie Geology Hotspot</span></span></span>

The geology is part of the story rather than a boring explanation that kills it. The University of St Andrews Bishop Hill geoheritage leaflet explains that the pillar is quartz dolerite and likely formed by erosion from wind, rain and ice. The accompanying page image shows the isolated pillar standing below the cliff, exactly the kind of shape that invites a human or supernatural explanation. A stone that looks almost made, but is natural, is fertile ground for folklore.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.st-andrews.ac.uk/assets/university/schools/school-of-earth-and-environmental-sciences/documents/geoheritage-leaflets/bishop-hill.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: st-andrews.ac.uk">[University of St Andrews]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">st-andrews.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">University of St Andrews</span></span></span>

Several versions of the legend survive in local and walking sources. Fife Walking says that the Devil and Maggie quarrelled, that he first threw rocks at her, and that the Devil’s Burdens on West Lomond are linked to those rocks before he turned her to stone. The Fife Place-name Data entry for Devil’s Burdens preserves a fuller literary version from Charles Gulland’s 1877 poem “The Lomond Hills”: Carlin Maggie, queen of witches meeting in Glen Vale between West Lomond and Bishop Hill, mocked Satan as he carried a load of rocks; he threw them down, chased her, and turned her into a stone pillar on Bishop Hill.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://fifewalking.com/find-a-walk/lomond-hills/bishop-hill/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: fifewalking.com">[Fife Walking]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">fifewalking.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Fife Walking Bishop Hill – Fife Walking</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Fife Walking Bishop Hill – Fife Walking</span></span></span>

That connection to West Lomond matters because it makes Carlin Maggie more than a single odd rock. The story links Bishop Hill, Glen Vale, the Devil’s Burdens and the Lomond Hills into one legendary route. It also shows how folklore crosses the edge of Kinross-shire’s historic county identity: Bishop Hill and Portmoak belong naturally to the Kinross-shire story, while the Lomond Hills landscape pulls the tale towards neighbouring Fife traditions.

## Law, legend and local retelling

The Crook of Devon trials and Carlin Maggie are often placed under the same “witches” heading, but they should not be flattened into the same kind of evidence. The Crook of Devon material is historically documented, legally violent and morally serious. Carlin Maggie is a place legend attached to a geological feature. One tells us how courts killed people; the other tells us how a community made a strange stone memorable.

That difference is the central interpretive risk. It is tempting to make the trials more folkloric than they were, turning them into a ghostly tale of covens and curses. It is also tempting to make Carlin Maggie more historical than she is, as though she were an identifiable woman. The better reading is more interesting: Kinross-shire preserves both witchcraft as prosecution and witchcraft as landscape imagination.

Carlin Maggie’s “head” adds a modern layer to the legend. The Living Lomonds leaflet says the topmost stones fell in the late 1980s as a result of wind and frost, so Maggie “lost her head”. That small geological change sounds as if it belongs inside the story itself: the old claim that wind and weather would one day free her has been partly answered by the slow damage of weathering.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://d1ssu070pg2v9i.cloudfront.net/pex/fcct/2019/07/02165111/Carlin-Maggie-Geology-Hotspot.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: d1ssu070pg2v9i.cloudfront.net">[Cloudfront]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">d1ssu070pg2v9i.cloudfront.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Carlin Maggie Geology Hotspot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Carlin Maggie Geology Hotspot</span></span></span>

The Crook of Devon story has also changed through modern retelling, but in a different direction. The Witches’ Maze and local walking routes have turned legal records into public remembrance. The effect is not to revive belief in witchcraft, but to acknowledge the victims of a system that confused fear with justice. In a county where much folklore clings to landmarks, the contrast is powerful: Lamblaires asks the visitor to remember human suffering, while Carlin Maggie asks the walker to read a cliff as a story.

## Why these witch traditions matter to Kinross-shire

Kinross-shire is a small historic county, and that smallness makes its witch traditions unusually concentrated. Crook of Devon, Tullibole, Bishop Hill, Portmoak, Glen Vale and Loch Leven sit close enough to be imagined as one connected landscape. A reader can move from the documentary world of indictments and executions to the folklore world of stones, slopes and Devil legends without leaving the county’s immediate orbit.

The Crook of Devon trials matter because they give Kinross-shire a place in the national history of Scottish witch-hunting. The case is not vague hearsay: it has names, dates, court sittings, sentences and execution places. Carlin Maggie matters because she shows the other side of witch tradition: the way a dramatic landform becomes a local character, retold through poems, place-names, geology leaflets and walking guides.

Together, they make a useful rule for reading county folklore: ask what kind of story is being told. Is it a court record, an antiquarian publication, a memorial, a walking-route legend, a poem, a geological explanation, or a modern tourist retelling? Kinross-shire’s witch traditions are strongest when those layers are kept visible. The result is not less mysterious, but more honest: a landscape where real persecution and legendary stone both survive, each demanding a different kind of attention.
