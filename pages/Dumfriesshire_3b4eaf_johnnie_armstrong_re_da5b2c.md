## Introduction

Johnnie Armstrong of Gilnockie is one of [Dumfriesshire]({{ 'where-dumfriesshires-strange-stories/' | relative_url }})’s great border legends: a real sixteenth-century reiver whose death became a ballad of pride, betrayal and local identity. He belonged to the Armstrong country of Eskdale and the Debatable Land, a hard-to-govern frontier zone where kinship, cattle raiding, fortified towers and shifting Anglo-Scottish loyalties shaped daily life. In 1530, King James V moved against him and Armstrong was hanged at Carlenrig with a large group of his followers. The execution is well remembered, but the meaning of it is still contested: was Johnnie a dangerous outlaw brought to justice, a local protector betrayed under safe conduct, or both at once?

That tension is why the story still matters in Dumfriesshire folklore. Gilnockie Tower gives the legend a visible place on the River Esk; Carlenrig gives it a death-site; and the ballad “Johnie Armstrong”, later classified as Child Ballad 169 and Roud 76, turns a Border power struggle into a dramatic memory of honour, kingship and broken trust.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://canmore.org.uk/site/67509/gilnockie-tower" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: canmore.org.uk">[canmore.org.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">canmore.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Gilnockie Tower &#124; PlacePlace: Gilnockie Tower, rebuilt at Hollows. ## Gilnockie Tower and the Armstrong country

Johnnie Armstrong’s legend is rooted in eastern Dumfriesshire, especially the Esk valley around Canonbie, Langholm and the old Armstrong sphere of influence. This was not folklore floating free of place. It attached to tower houses, river crossings, riding roads and a frontier society where the border between Scotland and England was often less important in practice than family allegiance, local protection and access to cattle, land and armed men.

Gilnockie Tower, also known historically as Hollows Tower, is the strongest modern landmark in the story. Canmore, the National Record of the Historic Environment, records Gilnockie Tower at Hollows and links the place-name tradition to Johnnie Armstrong’s byname “of Gilnockie”. The same record points to older antiquarian sources on Eskdale, Liddesdale and the Debatable Land, showing how long the tower has been folded into Armstrong memory.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://canmore.org.uk/site/67509/gilnockie-tower" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: canmore.org.uk">[Canmore]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">canmore.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Gilnockie Tower &#124; PlacePlace: Gilnockie Tower, rebuilt at Hollows. Johnnie Armstrong receives his name Gilnockie from the locality...</span></span></span>

There is, however, an important distinction between heritage memory and archaeological certainty. Historic Environment Scotland’s scheduled monument record for nearby Gilnockie Castle says some writers have linked that earthwork to Johnnie Armstrong and to the ballad, but states plainly that the theory remains unproven. Canmore’s separate record for Gilnockie Castle likewise notes that the name has been attached to the site because some authorities thought Johnnie had a tower at the east end of Gilnockie Bridge, while others disputed it; the surviving feature is an earthwork, and there is “no trace of any tower” there.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://portal.historicenvironment.scot/apex/f?p=1505%3A300%3A%3A%3A%3A%3AVIEWTYPE%2CVIEWREF%3Adesignation%2CSM11996" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: portal.historicenvironment.scot">[Historic Environment Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">portal.historicenvironment.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Historic Environment ScotlandGilnockie Castle, earthwork NE of Gilnockie Bridge (SM11996)26 Mar 2008 — Some writers have linked this monu...</span></span></span>

That uncertainty actually makes the tradition more interesting. Dumfriesshire’s Johnnie Armstrong is not just a museum label or a single ruin. He belongs to a cluster of remembered places: Hollows/Gilnockie Tower, the Esk valley, the Debateable Land, Staplegordon, Carlenrig and the wider Armstrong landscape. Historic Environment Scotland’s record for Barntalloch Castle at Staplegordon notes that the Eskdale estates passed through the Douglases and Maxwells and that the tenantry of Staplegorton was granted to John Armstrong “of Gilnockie” in 1525, placing him within a real network of landholding, lordship and frontier administration rather than only in later romance.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://portal.historicenvironment.scot/apex/f?p=1505%3A300%3A%3A%3A%3A%3AVIEWTYPE%2CVIEWREF%3Adesignation%2CSM685" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: portal.historicenvironment.scot">[Historic Environment Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">portal.historicenvironment.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on historicenvironment.scot.</span></span></span>

Modern retellings often call Gilnockie Tower the ancestral home of Clan Armstrong and present it as a gateway into Border Reiver history. That is a heritage framing rather than a neutral archive statement, but it shows how the legend now functions: visitors encounter Johnnie through a standing tower, clan identity, reiver tourism and a landscape that still feels close to the old frontier.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://gilnockietower.co.uk/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gilnockietower.co.uk">[Gilnockie Tower]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gilnockietower.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on gilnockietower.co.uk.</span></span></span>

## Carlenrig, James V and the disputed betrayal

The core story is simple enough to remember and complicated enough to argue about. Johnnie Armstrong, a powerful Border reiver, was summoned or brought before King James V in 1530 and hanged at Carlenrig, also spelled Caerlanrig or Carlanrig, with many of his men. The numbers vary in later accounts, but 36 followers is a common figure in heritage retellings, while other sources give higher totals. Scotland Starts Here’s entry for the Johnnie Armstrong Memorial says he was tricked into meeting the young James V at Carlenrig in 1530 and hanged with 36 unarmed men.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://scotlandstartshere.com/point-of-interest/johnnie-armstrong-memorial/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: scotlandstartshere.com">[Scotland Starts Here]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">scotlandstartshere.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on scotlandstartshere.com.</span></span></span>

The historical setting was James V’s attempt to impose royal authority on the Borders. Armstrong was not merely a picturesque cattle thief. He was part of a powerful reiving family operating in a zone where official justice was weak and local armed power mattered. Reiving could include raiding, blackmail in the older sense of protection money, retaliation and cross-border violence. That made Armstrong a useful local strongman to some, a menace to others, and an embarrassment to a crown trying to look sovereign on both sides of diplomacy with England.

The ballad version sharpens the incident into betrayal. In Child’s printed version, Johnie Armstrong is a great outlaw figure with “eight score men” in his hall, richly mounted and splendidly equipped. News of his power reaches the king; the king sends for him; Armstrong rides in magnificence; and the meeting ends not in pardon but death. The poem’s emotional centre is not legal procedure but violated honour: a man who appears before royal power believing some form of grace is possible finds that the king has already chosen execution.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://sacred-texts.com/neu/eng/child/ch169.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sacred-texts.com">[Internet Sacred Text Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sacred-texts.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on sacred-texts.com.</span></span></span>

The difficulty is that the ballad is not a court record. It preserves how the event was sung, shaped and morally understood, not necessarily how every step occurred. Modern ballad commentary is careful on this point: the execution certainly happened, but the idea that James lured Armstrong by a promise of pardon or safe conduct may be part history, part Border grievance and part poetic intensification.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://singyonder.substack.com/p/roud-76-johnnie-armstrong" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: singyonder.substack.com">[singyonder.substack.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">singyonder.substack.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ROU D 76: Johnnie Armstrong</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ROU D 76: Johnnie Armstrong</span></span></span>

That uncertainty should not be flattened into either “it was all true” or “it was only legend”. Border folklore often lives in the charged space between official violence and local memory. From the crown’s view, Armstrong could be framed as a rebel and extortioner. From the Armstrong side, and from later ballad tradition, his execution could be remembered as a shameful breach of trust. Both readings grew from the same frontier world.

## How ballads turn history into identity

“Johnie Armstrong” is one of the Border reiver ballads that helped turn local violence into shared memory. Francis James Child classified it as Child Ballad 169, and the Vaughan Williams Memorial Library indexes the song tradition under Roud 76. Those catalogue numbers matter because they show that Johnnie’s story belongs not just to one tourist site or one family tradition, but to the wider collected record of English and Scottish balladry.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://sacred-texts.com/neu/eng/child/ch169.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sacred-texts.com">[Internet Sacred Text Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sacred-texts.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on sacred-texts.com.</span></span></span>

Sir Walter Scott played a major role in fixing the ballad’s place in literary Border culture. The Walter Scott educational site notes that “Johnie Armstrang” appeared in Scott’s *Minstrelsy of the Scottish Border* from the first edition in 1802 and in later editions covered by the project. Scott’s own note, preserved in the *Minstrelsy* text, calls Johnie Armstrong “a noted personage, both in history and tradition”, a phrase that neatly captures why this page belongs in a folklore project as well as a history project.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://walterscott.eu/education/ballads/reiver-ballads/johnie-armstrang/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: walterscott.eu">[walterscott.eu]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">walterscott.eu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">johnie armstrang</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">johnie armstrang</span></span></span>

The ballad does several things at once. It makes Armstrong grander than a mere criminal by stressing his retinue, horses, clothing and generosity. It makes the king colder and more absolute by placing royal authority against Border honour. It gives the execution a memorable moral shape: Johnnie is not just killed; he is denied the recognition he believes his power and loyalty have earned. That is why the story can still be retold as tragedy even when the historical Armstrong remains a violent reiver.

The song also gives Dumfriesshire a distinctive form of local hero-making. Armstrong is not a saint, fairy figure, witch, giant or ghost. He is a Border man whose legend is built from real political violence. In that sense he belongs beside other Dumfriesshire and Border traditions where memory clings to named places: towers, ruined chapels, old roads, gallows sites and ballad landscapes. The supernatural is not the main element here; the legendary force comes from the way the tale asks who gets to define justice.

## Hero, reiver or border legend?

The most useful answer is that Johnnie Armstrong is all three, depending on the lens. Calling him a “hero” reflects the ballad and clan-memory view: he is brave, splendid, loyal to his own people and betrayed by a king. Calling him a “reiver” keeps the hard historical edge: he belonged to a raiding society that could terrorise farms and settlements, especially across the English side of the frontier. Calling him a “border legend” explains why the story has lasted: it gives a dramatic human face to the uneasy relationship between local power and central authority.

A fair reading needs to hold those parts together. The old Dictionary of National Biography entry calls Armstrong a “freebooter of the border-country” and says he lived at the Hollows near Langholm, while also preserving the traditional claim that he did not molest Scots but was feared from the Borders to Newcastle. That kind of description is already half history, half legend: it condemns him as a raider while repeating the local defence that he protected his own side.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Dictionary_of_National_Biography%2C_1885-1900/Armstrong%2C_John_%28d.1528%29" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: en.wikisource.org">[Wikisource]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">en.wikisource.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Armstrong, John (d.1528</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Armstrong, John (d.1528</span></span></span>

The modern heritage version often leans towards romance. Scotland Starts Here describes Gilnockie Tower as the residence of Johnnie Armstrong around 500 years ago and says his story was later romanticised by Walter Scott. That is a useful warning. The Johnnie many readers meet today has passed through ballads, antiquarian collecting, clan pride, tourism, folk performance and local storytelling.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://scotlandstartshere.com/point-of-interest/gilnockie-tower/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: scotlandstartshere.com">[Scotland Starts Here]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">scotlandstartshere.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on scotlandstartshere.com.</span></span></span>

Yet romanticised does not mean invented. The execution, the Armstrong landscape and the ballad tradition are all well attested in different ways. Historic records and monuments keep the place-based skeleton; the ballad supplies the emotional flesh. The result is a Dumfriesshire legend that feels unusually grounded: a real man, a real frontier, a real royal crackdown, and a song tradition that keeps asking whether power and justice were the same thing at Carlenrig.

## Why the legend still belongs to Dumfriesshire

Johnnie Armstrong’s story is sometimes treated as part of the wider Scottish Borders, and Carlenrig itself lies in Teviotdale rather than at Gilnockie. Even so, the legend’s Dumfriesshire centre of gravity is strong. Gilnockie Tower stands near Canonbie in the historic county’s eastern borderlands; the Armstrong country reaches through Eskdale and the old Debatable Land; and the remembered route between tower and execution-site gives the tale its tragic geography.

That matters for a county folklore map because the story shows how Dumfriesshire’s legendary culture is not only about supernatural beings or uncanny places. It is also about historical memory made strange by repetition: a king’s summons, a richly dressed riding party, a gallows at Carlenrig, a ballad lament, a tower that still draws visitors, and a local name that refuses to settle into one moral category.

The legend also links naturally to neighbouring Border themes without losing its Dumfriesshire focus. It sits beside reiver traditions of Liddesdale, Annandale and Cumberland; it connects to tower-house heritage across the Anglo-Scottish frontier; and it foreshadows later Armstrong stories such as Kinmont Willie, where rescue, loyalty and defiance again become the stuff of Border song. For readers following Dumfriesshire folklore, Johnnie Armstrong is therefore not a detachable “famous outlaw” anecdote. He is one of the clearest examples of how a county’s landscape can turn violence, politics and kinship into legend.

## What visitors and readers should notice today

The strongest way to understand Johnnie Armstrong today is to read the places and the ballad together. Gilnockie Tower shows the defensive world of the reiver families: compact, fortified, watchful and tied to river routes. The uncertain archaeology around Gilnockie Castle reminds us that heritage memory can preserve a name even when the physical evidence is debated. Carlenrig gives the story its fatal end, but the ballad gives that end its emotional afterlife.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://portal.historicenvironment.scot/apex/f?p=1505%3A300%3A%3A%3A%3A%3AVIEWTYPE%2CVIEWREF%3Adesignation%2CSM11996" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: portal.historicenvironment.scot">[historicenvironment.scot]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">portal.historicenvironment.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Historic Environment ScotlandGilnockie Castle, earthwork NE of Gilnockie Bridge (SM11996)26 Mar 2008 — Some writers have linked this monu...</span></span></span>

A good folklore reading also notices what has changed over time. In the sixteenth century, Armstrong’s death could serve as a warning that the king’s reach extended into the Border lands. In ballad tradition, it became a warning about trusting royal grace. In nineteenth-century antiquarian culture, especially through Scott, it became part of a romantic Border past. In modern Dumfriesshire and south-of-Scotland heritage, it helps visitors connect towers, clan history, reiver routes and local identity.

That layered afterlife is the real reason Johnnie Armstrong endures. He is not remembered because the evidence is perfectly tidy. He is remembered because the story remains morally unsettled. The outlaw may also be a protector. The king may also be a betrayer. The tower may be both monument and memory-work. In Dumfriesshire folklore, Johnnie Armstrong stands at the point where history becomes a song people still want to argue with.
