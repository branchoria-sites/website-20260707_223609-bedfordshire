## Introduction

Lyme Regis has a black dog because a very local [Dorset]({{ 'why-dorsets-legends-still-haunt-the/' | relative_url }}) ghost story fastens a national folklore motif to one particular western edge of the town: a farmhouse, an old hearth, a hidden box of Stuart coins, and a lane said to be haunted after midnight. In the best-known version, the dog first appears indoors as a quiet but uncanny presence. Only when a drunken farmer attacks it with a poker does it flee to the attic, vanish through the ceiling, and reveal buried treasure. After that, the creature leaves the house and becomes a road-haunting spirit of Haye Lane, also remembered as Dog Lane.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://sacred-texts.com/neu/eng/efft/efft65.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sacred-texts.com">[Internet Sacred Text Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sacred-texts.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on sacred-texts.com.</span></span></span>



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/Dorset_51e44a_lyme_black_dog_65487a-overview.webp" | relative_url }}" alt="Overview image for Black Dog" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

The story matters because it is not just “another black dog” tale dropped into Dorset. It sits at Lyme Regis’s border country, where Dorset meets Devon, where old lanes climb inland from the coast, and where modern suburbs still preserve scraps of rural route, hedgebank and place-name memory. Dorset Council’s Historic Towns Survey describes Little Park/Haye Lane as an ancient minor route running roughly parallel to Uplyme Road, with Haye Lane, Roman Road and nearby lanes retaining a narrow, rural aspect in places.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.dorsetcouncil.gov.uk/documents/35024/283310/Lyme_Regis_Part_6_Historic_Urban_Character_Area_6_February_2011.pdf/0decc79e-d60f-0c62-f921-f78d5a3897f2?t=1619393202342&amp;version=1.0" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: dorsetcouncil.gov.uk">[Dorset Council]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">dorsetcouncil.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on dorsetcouncil.gov.uk.</span></span></span>

## The farmhouse, the poker and the hidden treasure

The oldest useful form of the tale places the haunting about a mile from Lyme Regis, in a farmhouse that had once been part of an older mansion damaged or reduced during the Parliamentary wars. The detail is important: this is not a vague moorland apparition but a domestic ghost story tied to a room, a chimney corner and a surviving fragment of a larger house. The farmer and his predecessors are said to have used a sitting-room with a broad old fireplace, the sort of interior where evening labour, drink, gossip and unease could all gather around the hearth.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://sacred-texts.com/neu/eng/efft/efft65.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sacred-texts.com">[Internet Sacred Text Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sacred-texts.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on sacred-texts.com.</span></span></span>

In the tale, the black dog does not begin as a killer. It repeatedly takes the seat opposite the farmer by the fire. Its presence is unsettling, but it does no harm. That makes the Lyme Regis story different from the most violent black-dog legends of East Anglia or northern England, where the animal may be treated as a death omen or demonic attacker. Here the dog is frightening because it is inexplicable and persistent, not because it immediately assaults anyone. The farmer’s neighbours urge him to drive it out, but he tolerates it because it costs nothing, eats nothing and interferes with no one.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://sacred-texts.com/neu/eng/efft/efft65.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sacred-texts.com">[Internet Sacred Text Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sacred-texts.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on sacred-texts.com.</span></span></span>

The turning point comes when the farmer returns home drunk, stung by taunts about his lack of courage. He seizes an iron poker and chases the dog upstairs. The animal runs into an attic, springs upward and disappears through the ceiling. When the farmer strikes the place where it vanished, an old-fashioned box falls down, full of gold and silver coins from the reign of Charles I. The haunting has therefore behaved like a treasure-guardian story: the supernatural creature terrifies the household, but its disturbance exposes something concealed in the fabric of the building.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://sacred-texts.com/neu/eng/efft/efft65.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sacred-texts.com">[Internet Sacred Text Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sacred-texts.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on sacred-texts.com.</span></span></span>

That treasure element gives the Lyme Regis dog its special flavour. Many British black dogs patrol roads, churchyards, streams or lonely places; some are death omens, some are demonic, and some are comparatively harmless. The Dictionary of English Folklore notes that phantom black dogs are usually large, black, shaggy and glowing-eyed, and that they often patrol specific lanes; it also singles out the Lyme Regis case as a farm-haunting dog whose pursuit with a poker dislodged a hidden box of coins.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.tadshistory.com/TADS/books/A_Dictionary_of_English_Folklore_ABEE.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: tadshistory.com">[Tadley and District History Society]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">tadshistory.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Tadley and District History Society</span></span></span>

The Charles I detail also gives the story a historical scent, even if it should not be treated as proof that the event happened. Coins of that reign evoke the Civil War period, while the ruined-mansion setting gives the tale a plausible old-house atmosphere. Folklore often works like this: it does not need to be documentary history to use historical memory effectively. A story of war-damaged property, hidden money and a restless black animal makes sense in a landscape where old houses, altered estates and local roads preserve memories longer than written explanations do.

## Haye Lane, Dog Lane and border folklore

Once the treasure is found, the black dog no longer belongs inside the farmhouse. The tale moves it outside, onto the lane leading to the house. In the collected version, the dog was never again seen indoors but continued to haunt the lane at midnight. The road was said to have long borne the name “Dog Lane”, and a roadside inn with the sign of the Black Dog helped carry the legend into everyday local memory.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://sacred-texts.com/neu/eng/efft/efft65.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sacred-texts.com">[Internet Sacred Text Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sacred-texts.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on sacred-texts.com.</span></span></span>

This movement from house to lane is what makes the Lyme Regis tradition so useful for understanding Dorset folklore. Dorset’s legends often attach to visible features: chalk figures, old stones, ruined churches, wells, parish routes and hollow ways. Here the key feature is not a spectacular monument but a road. A lane can hold fear differently from a house. It is crossed in darkness, remembered by walkers, renamed in gossip, and retold whenever someone asks why a place or business is called “Black Dog”.

Haye Lane’s geography strengthens that reading. Dorset Council’s Historic Towns Survey places the wider Uplyme Road character area on the north-west side of Lyme Regis, with Uplyme Road climbing towards the Dorset/Devon border. It identifies Little Park/Haye Lane as another minor ancient route, and says that Haye Lane, Roman Road and Clappentail Lane have, in places, a narrow rural aspect with dense vegetation and hedgebanks.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.dorsetcouncil.gov.uk/documents/35024/283310/Lyme_Regis_Part_6_Historic_Urban_Character_Area_6_February_2011.pdf/0decc79e-d60f-0c62-f921-f78d5a3897f2?t=1619393202342&amp;version=1.0" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: dorsetcouncil.gov.uk">[Dorset Council]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">dorsetcouncil.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on dorsetcouncil.gov.uk.</span></span></span>

That is exactly the kind of landscape where black-dog stories travel well. A half-rural lane on the edge of town creates a boundary in several senses at once: between settlement and countryside, Dorset and Devon, daylight use and night fear, ordinary dogs and spectral ones. Uplyme itself lies in East Devon, close to Lyme Regis and the Dorset border; local Uplyme history also shows long connections with Lyme Regis through landholding, roads and parish memory.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.uplymeparishcouncil.gov.uk/about-uplyme/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: uplymeparishcouncil.gov.uk">[uplymeparishcouncil.gov.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">uplymeparishcouncil.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on uplymeparishcouncil.gov.uk.</span></span></span><span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://uplymehall.co.uk/history/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: uplymehall.co.uk">[Uplyme Hall]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">uplymehall.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Uplyme Hall History of Uplyme &#124; Uplyme Village Hall</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Uplyme Hall History of Uplyme &#124; Uplyme Village Hall</span></span></span>

The border point should not be overplayed into a grand theory, but it helps explain why the legend feels so locally rooted. Lyme Regis is a Dorset town at the far west of the historic county, while Uplyme belongs to Devon. Wikishire describes Dorset as a West Country county on the English Channel coast, bordering Devon to the west, and notes Lyme Regis at the western end of Dorset’s coast near that border.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://wikishire.co.uk/wiki/Dorset" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wikishire.co.uk">[Wikishire]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wikishire.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wikishire.co.uk.</span></span></span>

Haye Lane also has built-history anchors. Historic England’s listing for Haye House places it on the east side of Haye Lane and records an early nineteenth-century exterior, with a date of 1802 under a roof slate, while the Dorset Historic Towns Survey suggests Haye House is probably medieval in date and was certainly in existence in the early post-medieval period.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://historicengland.org.uk/listing/the-list/list-entry/1229807" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: historicengland.org.uk">[Historic England]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">historicengland.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Historic England Haye House, Lyme Regis</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Historic England Haye House, Lyme Regis</span></span></span><span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.dorsetcouncil.gov.uk/documents/35024/283310/Lyme_Regis_Part_6_Historic_Urban_Character_Area_6_February_2011.pdf/0decc79e-d60f-0c62-f921-f78d5a3897f2?t=1619393202342&amp;version=1.0" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: dorsetcouncil.gov.uk">[Dorset Council]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">dorsetcouncil.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on dorsetcouncil.gov.uk.</span></span></span>

That does not prove Haye House is the exact farmhouse of the tale. It does show why the story is comfortable here. The lane has antiquity, named buildings, old route status and a position at the edge of Lyme Regis. Folklore does not always need a single verified building to survive; it needs a credible local setting where the narrative can be repeatedly placed.

## How old and well-attested is the Lyme Regis black dog?

The Lyme Regis black dog is well attested as a printed folklore item, but not as a proven event. Its strongest nineteenth-century source is Edwin Sidney Hartland’s English Fairy and Other Folk Tales, first published in 1890, where the story appears among “spectre-dog” traditions. Hartland’s account includes the farmhouse, the hearth-side dog, the poker, the attic, the Charles I coins, Dog Lane, the Black Dog inn sign and a reported sighting from 1856.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://sacred-texts.com/neu/eng/efft/index.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sacred-texts.com">[Internet Sacred Text Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sacred-texts.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Internet Sacred Text Archive English Fairy and Other Folk Tales Index</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Internet Sacred Text Archive English Fairy and Other Folk Tales Index</span></span></span><span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://sacred-texts.com/neu/eng/efft/efft65.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sacred-texts.com">[Internet Sacred Text Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sacred-texts.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on sacred-texts.com.</span></span></span>

John Symonds Udal then preserved the Dorset version in Dorsetshire Folk-Lore, published in 1922. Modern folklore reference works continue to cite Udal’s account, and The Dictionary of English Folklore uses the Lyme Regis example to illustrate the wider class of black dogs that haunt farms, lanes or treasure sites.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://books.google.com/books/about/Dorsetshire_Folk_lore.html?id=hgTaAAAAMAAJ" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: books.google.com">[Google Books]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">books.google.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Books Dorsetshire Folk-lore</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Books Dorsetshire Folk-lore</span></span></span><span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.tadshistory.com/TADS/books/A_Dictionary_of_English_Folklore_ABEE.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: tadshistory.com">[Tadley and District History Society]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">tadshistory.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Tadley and District History Society</span></span></span>

The 1856 sighting reported in Hartland gives the legend one of its most memorable later details. A woman returning down Dog Lane with her husband said she saw an animal approach, first dog-sized and then as large as a young calf, with the look of a shaggy black dog and fiery eyes. Her husband, in the story, saw nothing. The creature passed close, made the air feel cold and damp, grew larger as it went away, and vanished like a cloud shortly after midnight.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://sacred-texts.com/neu/eng/efft/efft65.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sacred-texts.com">[Internet Sacred Text Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sacred-texts.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on sacred-texts.com.</span></span></span>

That episode is valuable folklore evidence, not courtroom evidence. It shows how the tale was being narrated: the dog appears to one witness but not another; it changes size; it affects the air; the time is checked afterwards; and the lane itself becomes the stage. These are classic features of ghost-story telling. They make the experience memorable and repeatable, whether or not a modern reader treats the apparition as supernatural.

There is also a useful caution built into Hartland’s surrounding chapter. Before giving the Lyme Regis story, the same section includes a rationalising anecdote about a “black dog apparition” that turned out to be an ordinary gamekeeper’s dog.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://sacred-texts.com/neu/eng/efft/efft65.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sacred-texts.com">[Internet Sacred Text Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sacred-texts.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on sacred-texts.com.</span></span></span> That does not debunk the Lyme story, but it reminds readers that collectors often recorded both belief and scepticism. A haunted lane could be a place of genuine fear, a story used to explain darkness and danger, or even a reputation useful to people who preferred a road to be avoided at night.

## Why this black dog is not quite Black Shuck

Readers often meet British black dogs through famous names such as Black Shuck, the Barghest or the Padfoot. The Lyme Regis dog belongs to the same broad family but has a different role. It is not mainly a church invader, a death omen or a hellhound. It is a house-haunter that becomes a lane-haunter after revealing treasure.

The Dictionary of English Folklore is helpful here because it stresses variety. Some black dogs are treated as ghosts, others as supernatural creatures or manifestations of the Devil. They are usually solitary and often patrol specific lanes, but their meanings vary: in some places they are harmful, in others harmless unless disturbed, and in a few cases connected with guarding treasure.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.tadshistory.com/TADS/books/A_Dictionary_of_English_Folklore_ABEE.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: tadshistory.com">[Tadley and District History Society]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">tadshistory.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Tadley and District History Society</span></span></span>

The Lyme Regis story sits in that last, more ambiguous corner. The dog frightens people, but it also reveals hidden wealth. It is attacked first. It leaves the house only after being pursued. Its later midnight appearances on Dog Lane are eerie, but the core narrative does not make it a straightforward monster. That ambiguity is part of its local appeal: it is scary enough for a ghost walk, but strange enough to feel older and more layered than a simple “evil hound” tale.

The treasure motif may also explain why the dog remains memorable in a county already rich in supernatural stories. Dorset has giants, witches, white hares, fairy roads, ghostly churches and uncanny stones. Lyme Regis’s black dog earns its place because it combines several themes in one compact narrative: old-house haunting, buried money, Civil War memory, night travel, border lanes and a named local road.

## How the legend survives in modern retellings

The Lyme Regis black dog survives today less as a living belief than as a piece of local identity. The old narrative is still repeated in folklore summaries, ghost-story collections, local history pages and visitor material. Modern retellings usually keep the same core sequence: farmhouse, harmless dog, drunken attack with poker, attic, treasure, and a haunting transferred to Haye Lane or Dog Lane.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://mythical-beasts.fandom.com/wiki/Black_Dog_of_Lyme_Regis" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: mythical-beasts.fandom.com">[mythical-beasts.fandom.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">mythical-beasts.fandom.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Black Dog of Lyme Regis &#124; Mythical Beasts</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Black Dog of Lyme Regis &#124; Mythical Beasts</span></span></span><span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.davidcastleton.net/black-dog-legends-england-britain-ghosts-hellhounds/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: davidcastleton.net">[David Castleton Blog - The Serpent&#x27;s Pen]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">davidcastleton.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">David Castleton Blog</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">David Castleton Blog</span></span></span>

Local naming helps the story endure. The Black Dog name has been attached to hospitality and visitor culture around Lyme Regis and Uplyme, including the Old Black Dog at Lyme Road and the Black Dog Tea Room. Tourism listings place the Old Black Dog in the Uplyme/Lyme Regis area, while Visit Dorset’s material for nearby Haye Farm directs visitors by reference to the Black Dog B&B or tea room and Haye Lane.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.uktourismonline.co.uk/entry.php?id=10889&amp;type=2" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: uktourismonline.co.uk">[UK Tourism Online]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">uktourismonline.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on uktourismonline.co.uk.</span></span></span><span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.visit-dorset.com/listing/haye-farm-holidays/7521301/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: visit-dorset.com">[Visit Dorset]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">visit-dorset.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">haye farm holidays</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">haye farm holidays</span></span></span>

This kind of survival is typical of modern local folklore. The tale is no longer dependent on everyone believing that a spectral dog physically patrols the lane. It persists through place-names, business names, walking routes, Halloween features, local social media, ghost walks and short online summaries. The story becomes a way of noticing the landscape: a visitor who might otherwise see only a road on the edge of town is invited to imagine an older, darker route with a reputation.

There is a risk in modern retelling, though. Some online versions flatten the story into a generic “haunted road” item or add unverified details about vanishing pets, cursed inns or named manors without showing where those additions come from. The strongest version remains the older collected narrative: a quiet hearth-side apparition, a foolish act of violence, an accidental discovery, and a lane that keeps the dog after the house loses it.

For Dorset folklore, that is enough. The Lyme Regis black dog does not need to be inflated into a county-wide demon or a cinematic monster. Its power lies in its scale: one farmhouse, one poker blow, one box of coins, and one border lane where the ordinary act of walking home after midnight becomes a story people still recognise.
