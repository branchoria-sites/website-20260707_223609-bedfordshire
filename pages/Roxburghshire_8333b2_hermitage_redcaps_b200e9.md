## Introduction

Hermitage Castle feels haunted because its folklore is not decorative: it grows out of Liddesdale’s hard Border history, its lonely landscape and a cluster of stories about cruel power. The best-known tradition centres on Robin Redcap, a murderous goblin or familiar spirit said to serve “Bad Lord Soulis”, the feared lord of Hermitage. In the legend, Soulis is protected from ordinary weapons until the people of the valley finally take him to Ninestane Rig and destroy him in a way that sounds more like ritual vengeance than lawful execution.



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/Roxburghshire_8333b2_hermitage_redcaps_b200e9-overview.webp" | relative_url }}" alt="Overview image for Hermitage" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

The important point is not that every grisly detail can be treated as literal history. The value of the Hermitage tradition is that it shows how [Roxburghshire]({{ 'where-roxburghshires-legends-meet-the/' | relative_url }}) turned memories of lordship, raiding, treason, ruined towers and exposed moorland into fear lore. Historic Environment Scotland describes Hermitage as “the strength of Liddesdale” and notes that, for much of its working life, it was central to control of the Scottish Middle March. It also preserves the modern description of the castle as “the guardhouse of the bloodiest valley in Britain”.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.historicenvironment.scot/visit/all/hermitage-castle/history-and-stories/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: historicenvironment.scot">[Historic Environment Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">historicenvironment.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on historicenvironment.scot.</span></span></span>

## Why Hermitage Castle became Roxburghshire’s darkest Border setting

Hermitage Castle stands in Liddesdale, now within the Scottish Borders council area but historically within Roxburghshire’s Border story. The surrounding chapel and graveyard site is recorded with the former county as Roxburghshire, which matters for a historic-county folklore project: the legend belongs to the old Border county landscape even though visitors now usually encounter it through Scottish Borders tourism.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.trove.scot/place/67914" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: trove.scot">[Trove Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">trove.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on trove.scot.</span></span></span>

The place has the right physical grammar for grim legend. It is not a romantic cliff-top castle or a courtly ruin softened by gardens. Historic Environment Scotland calls Hermitage “a lonely spot” in “deepest Liddesdale”, where the ruin itself heightens the sense of foreboding; its public interpretation also stresses that the real history of torture, treason and violence is already dark enough without needing much invention.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.historicenvironment.scot/visit/all/hermitage-castle/history-and-stories/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: historicenvironment.scot">[Historic Environment Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">historicenvironment.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on historicenvironment.scot.</span></span></span> The scheduled monument record adds that the castle is an “impressive upstanding stone building” set among large earthworks, with a chapel, enclosures, a probable deer trap, park boundary and later farmstead around it.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://portal.historicenvironment.scot/apex/f?p=1505%3A300%3A%3A%3A%3A%3AVIEWTYPE%2CVIEWREF%3Adesignation%2CSM90161" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: portal.historicenvironment.scot">[Historic Environment Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">portal.historicenvironment.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on historicenvironment.scot.</span></span></span>

That setting helps explain why Hermitage became a natural home for redcap folklore. Redcaps are not airy fairies of green hills and music. In Border tradition they belong to ruined towers, murder, night shelter, blood and old stone. Hermitage is therefore not just a convenient address for a monster; it is almost the ideal redcap landscape. It sits where military control, local terror and supernatural imagination can all be made to look like parts of the same story.

The castle’s reputation also comes from its strategic role. Historic Environment Scotland says Hermitage was fought over repeatedly and that even its building in the 13th century helped bring Scotland and England close to war.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.historicenvironment.scot/visit/all/hermitage-castle/history-and-stories/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: historicenvironment.scot">[Historic Environment Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">historicenvironment.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on historicenvironment.scot.</span></span></span> In folklore terms, that gives the ruin an unusually dense emotional charge. It was not merely a noble residence. It was a symbol of who controlled Liddesdale, who could enforce fear, and who might be feared in return.

## Robin Redcap and the redcap goblin tradition

Robin Redcap is the named supernatural figure most closely tied to Hermitage Castle. In the Hermitage version, he appears as the familiar or helper of Lord Soulis, assisting the lord’s tyranny and magical protection. This is not a gentle household spirit. The redcap type is generally described in Border folklore as a violent goblin haunting ruined castles on or near the Anglo-Scottish frontier, especially places associated with wickedness, tyranny or bloodshed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Redcap" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wikipedia.org.</span></span></span>

The creature’s most memorable trait is the bloody cap. Later folklore summaries, drawing on 19th-century collections, describe redcaps as soaking their caps in the blood of victims and needing to keep that blood fresh. William Henderson’s *Notes on the Folk-lore of the Northern Counties of England and the Borders* gives the classic antiquarian picture: a dangerous goblin of Border ruins, not easily overcome by human strength, but vulnerable to sacred words or signs.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.icysedgwick.com/redcaps/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: icysedgwick.com">[Icy Sedgwick]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">icysedgwick.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Icy Sedgwick What Are Redcaps And Why Should You Avoid Their Lair?</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Icy Sedgwick What Are Redcaps And Why Should You Avoid Their Lair?</span></span></span>

Hermitage’s Robin Redcap is more specific than the general species. In John Leyden’s ballad “Lord Soulis”, printed in Walter Scott’s *Minstrelsy of the Scottish Border*, Redcap is bound into Soulis’s magical security. The opening places Soulis in Hermitage Castle and has him consult “Old Redcap sly” about the death he must die. Redcap promises protection against lance, arrow, sword and knife, and says that neither forged steel nor hempen rope will confine him until impossible ropes of sifted sand are made.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://greydogtales.com/blog/redcap-coffman-doom-lord-soulis/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: greydogtales.com">[Grey Dog Tales]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">greydogtales.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Grey Dog Tales Redcap, Coffman, and the Doom of Lord Soulis</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Grey Dog Tales Redcap, Coffman, and the Doom of Lord Soulis</span></span></span>

That is the key to the tale’s atmosphere. Robin Redcap is not simply a monster in the corner of the ruin. He is the supernatural logic behind bad power. Soulis cannot be killed in normal ways because his violence is imagined as magically armoured. The people of Liddesdale therefore need a punishment that is equally abnormal. This is why the story moves from the castle to Ninestane Rig and from ordinary justice to boiling in lead or a cauldron.

Scott’s note on redcaps is important because it shows how Hermitage fits a wider southern Scottish pattern. He presents “Redcap” as a popular name for a class of spirits haunting old castles and says every ruined tower in the south of Scotland was supposed to have one.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Page%3ANotes_on_the_folk-lore_of_the_northern_counties_of_England_and_the_borders.djvu/276" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: en.wikisource.org">[Wikisource]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">en.wikisource.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wikisource.org.</span></span></span> That does not prove that every tower had a detailed living oral tale, but it does show how natural the association between ruined Border masonry and violent spirits had become by the time Scott and his circle were turning Border lore into print.

## Bad Lord Soulis between history and legend

The Lord Soulis of folklore is a near-perfect villain: cruel landlord, sorcerer, tyrant, child-murderer in some retellings, and master of a redcap familiar. The historical William de Soulis was real, but the legend has swallowed and rearranged him. A careful reading has to hold both facts together: there was a powerful de Soulis connection with Liddesdale and Hermitage, but the famous boiling story is legendary rather than a secure record of medieval execution.

The historical William II de Soulis was involved in the politics of Robert the Bruce’s Scotland. Modern summaries of the historical evidence connect him with the 1320 conspiracy against Bruce; after confession and judgement, he was imprisoned in Dumbarton Castle, where he died, rather than being boiled by his tenants at Ninestane Rig.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_II_de_Soules" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">William II de Soules</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">William II de Soules</span></span></span> This is one of the clearest places where the Hermitage tradition separates into two layers: political history on one side, local vengeance legend on the other.

The legendary Soulis, however, tells us something history alone does not. In the tale, he becomes the image of unbearable lordship. Stories say he practised the black arts, was protected by Robin Redcap, and preyed on the people around Hermitage. Undiscovered Scotland’s biography gives a typical modern retelling: stories circulated that Soulis kidnapped local children and used their blood in dark rituals, with Robin Redcap summoned during those rites.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.undiscoveredscotland.co.uk/usbiography/s/williamdesoules.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: undiscoveredscotland.co.uk">[Undiscovered Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">undiscoveredscotland.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on undiscoveredscotland.co.uk.</span></span></span> Such details should be treated as folklore, not court record. Their function is to make tyranny visible as monstrosity.

John Leyden’s “Lord Soulis” intensifies this process. It is not a neutral transcript of peasant testimony; it is a literary ballad “founded upon local tradition” and included in Scott’s influential Border collection. The National Library of Scotland notes that Scott’s *Minstrelsy of the Scottish Border* first appeared in 1802 and helped establish his literary career, while the collection itself mixed fieldwork, manuscript material, collaborators and literary shaping.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://blog.nls.uk/walter-scott-and-the-minstrelsy-of-the-scottish-border/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: blog.nls.uk">[blog.nls.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">blog.nls.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">walter scott and the minstrelsy of the scottish border</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">walter scott and the minstrelsy of the scottish border</span></span></span> That matters because the Soulis story many readers know today has passed through antiquarian and Romantic hands.

This does not make it worthless as folklore. It makes it more interesting. The Hermitage Soulis is a Border memory worked over by oral tradition, local explanation, ballad poetry and later tourism. The legend is not reliable evidence that a lord was literally boiled in lead; it is evidence that Liddesdale preserved a powerful story about what happens when a castle’s authority becomes unbearable.

## Ninestane Rig and Liddesdale’s dark memory

Ninestane Rig is the landscape anchor that keeps the Soulis legend from floating away into generic ghost story. The site is a stone circle near Hermitage, and Historic Environment Scotland records Nine Stones, Ninestone Rig as a scheduled monument comprising a Neolithic ritual monument.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://portal.historicenvironment.scot/apex/f?p=1505%3A300%3A%3A%3A%3A%3AVIEWTYPE%2CVIEWREF%3Adesignation%2CSM1688" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: portal.historicenvironment.scot">[Historic Environment Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">portal.historicenvironment.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on historicenvironment.scot.</span></span></span> In archaeological terms, it long predates Lord Soulis. In folklore terms, that deep age gives the legend a ready-made stage for extraordinary punishment.

The traditional story says the people seized Soulis and took him to Ninestane Rig, where he was wrapped in lead and boiled, often in a cauldron suspended from stones. Wikishire’s account of Ninestane Rig summarises the standard legend and also notes the correction: William II de Soulis was not in fact boiled alive but died in Dumbarton Castle, while some elements may have become confused with traditions about an earlier de Soulis figure.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://wikishire.co.uk/wiki/Ninestane_Rig" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wikishire.co.uk">[Wikishire]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wikishire.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Ninestane Rig</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Ninestane Rig</span></span></span>

This is exactly the sort of uncertainty that makes Border folklore worth reading carefully. The legend does not operate like a chronicle. It compresses different fears into one memorable scene: the ancient stones, the hated lord, the failure of steel and rope, the people’s collective action, and a death that feels designed to get around magic. In Leyden’s ballad, True [Thomas]({{ 'thomas/' | relative_url }}) himself appears and ordinary bonds fail; the story borrows authority from another great Border supernatural figure while keeping Hermitage at the centre.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/files/45778/45778-h/45778-h.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on gutenberg.org.</span></span></span>

Ninestane Rig also changes how the reader experiences the wider Liddesdale landscape. The castle is the place of oppression; the stone circle is the place of judgement. One is medieval lordship, the other prehistoric mystery. The legend ties them together so that a walk or map-reading of the area becomes a route through fear memory: from stronghold to ritual place, from enclosed power to exposed moorland.

## What is old tradition, and what is later retelling?

The Hermitage redcap tradition is best understood as layered rather than simply “true” or “made up”. Several layers can be separated without draining the story of its force.

**Historical layer:** Hermitage was genuinely important in Border power politics. It controlled Liddesdale and was tied to conflict, lordship and the Scottish Middle March. Historic Environment Scotland’s account of the site makes clear that the castle’s real history is violent enough to explain why legends gathered there.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.historicenvironment.scot/visit/all/hermitage-castle/history-and-stories/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: historicenvironment.scot">[Historic Environment Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">historicenvironment.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on historicenvironment.scot.</span></span></span>

**Antiquarian and ballad layer:** The form in which many readers meet Robin Redcap and Lord Soulis comes through Scott and Leyden. Leyden’s “Lord Soulis” gives the tale a dramatic poetic structure, while Scott’s notes connect Robin Redcap to a wider class of old-castle spirits in southern Scotland.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/files/45778/45778-h/45778-h.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on gutenberg.org.</span></span></span>

**Local explanatory layer:** The boiling at Ninestane Rig explains a place by attaching a memorable event to it. Whether or not the event happened, the story makes the stones part of Liddesdale’s moral landscape. It turns an ancient monument into the scene of communal justice against supernatural tyranny.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://portal.historicenvironment.scot/apex/f?p=1505%3A300%3A%3A%3A%3A%3AVIEWTYPE%2CVIEWREF%3Adesignation%2CSM1688" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: portal.historicenvironment.scot">[Historic Environment Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">portal.historicenvironment.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on historicenvironment.scot.</span></span></span>

**Modern visitor layer:** Today, Hermitage is marketed and remembered as an eerie Border ruin with tales of murder, treason, Mary Queen of Scots, reivers and supernatural dread. Historic Environment Scotland’s visitor pages lean into the “awesome, eerie ruin” and its “splendid isolation”, while still grounding the atmosphere in the castle’s documented history.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.historicenvironment.scot/visit/all/hermitage-castle/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: historicenvironment.scot">[Historic Environment Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">historicenvironment.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on historicenvironment.scot.</span></span></span>

The risk in retelling the story is making it sound like a verified medieval atrocity. The better approach is more rewarding: treat the boiling of Soulis and Robin Redcap as traditions that reveal how people imagined cruel power, not as courtroom evidence. The tale is strongest when its uncertainty remains visible.

## Why this legend matters within Roxburghshire folklore

Hermitage Castle’s redcap story is one of Roxburghshire’s sharpest examples of Border folklore because it joins monster lore to local history so tightly. A redcap is already a creature of ruined towers and blood. Liddesdale supplies the landscape. Hermitage supplies the stronghold. Soulis supplies the tyrant. Ninestane Rig supplies the ancient place of reckoning.

That makes the tradition different from a simple haunted-castle tale. Many castles have ghosts; Hermitage has a whole fear system. Its legend asks what kind of place produces a creature like Robin Redcap, and the answer is: a place where violence, authority and memory have had centuries to harden into story.

It also helps connect Roxburghshire to neighbouring Border traditions without losing the county focus. Redcaps belong to a wider Anglo-Scottish Border pattern, but Robin Redcap is firmly localised at Hermitage. Thomas the Rhymer belongs to the broader Eildon and Border fairy world, yet his appearance in Leyden’s Soulis material shows how Border legends could cross-reference one another. Reiver history belongs to the whole frontier, but Hermitage gives Roxburghshire a particularly grim emblem of that world.

For modern readers, the most honest reading is also the most memorable. Hermitage feels haunted not because one can prove Robin Redcap stalked its chambers, but because the castle, the valley and the stories all point in the same direction. This is a place where Roxburghshire’s folklore turns fear into geography: the redcap in the ruin, the bad lord in the tower, and the stones on the rig waiting beyond the walls.
