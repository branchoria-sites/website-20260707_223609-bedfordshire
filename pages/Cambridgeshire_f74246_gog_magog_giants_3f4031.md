## Introduction

The Gog Magog Hills are the place where [Cambridgeshire]({{ 'cambridgeshire/' | relative_url }})’s giant lore becomes landscape. Rising south-east of Cambridge, around Wandlebury and Magog Down, they are not mountains by any ordinary standard, but in a county famous for fenland flatness they feel dramatic: chalk ridges, old earthworks, beechwoods, open views and names that seem to point towards giants. The tradition is not one simple ancient survival. It is a layered story: a medieval haunted hillfort, a lost or disputed chalk-cut giant, student games on the hills, later antiquarian reports, and modern local tales of Gog and Magog buried under the chalk or shaping the hills in anger. The strongest evidence shows that Wandlebury was a real Iron Age hillfort and a long-lived legendary place; the giant figures themselves are more elusive, partly recorded in early modern sources and partly tangled with later speculation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://historicengland.org.uk/listing/the-list/list-entry/1009395" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: historicengland.org.uk">[historicengland.org.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">historicengland.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Historic EnglandWandlebury Camp: a multivallate hillfort, earlier univallate hillfort, Iron Age cemetery and 17th century formal garden r...</span></span></span>

## The hills south-east of Cambridge

The Gog Magog Hills lie just beyond Cambridge, with Wandlebury Hill and Magog Down forming the best-known public face of the landscape. Wandlebury is about 6.5 km south-east of Cambridge, in Wandlebury Country Park, and the Prehistoric Society describes the site as an Iron Age hillfort on the Gog Magog Downs, signposted from the A1307 Cambridge to Haverhill road.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.prehistoricsociety.org/sites/prehistoricsociety.org/files/resources/ps-signpost_wandlebury.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: prehistoricsociety.org">[The Prehistoric Society]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">prehistoricsociety.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Prehistoric Society Microsoft Word</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Prehistoric Society Microsoft Word</span></span></span> The Museum of Cambridge gives the same everyday orientation: these are the hills reached from the road to Haverhill, now popular for walkers, nature lovers and picnics.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.museumofcambridge.org.uk/2021/03/gog-magog-the-cambridgeshire-giants/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: museumofcambridge.org.uk">[The Museum of Cambridge]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">museumofcambridge.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on museumofcambridge.org.uk.</span></span></span>

For folklore, the location matters. Cambridgeshire has two very different imaginative landscapes. In the north, fenland lore often gathers around water, isolation, drainage, [witches]({{ 'witches/' | relative_url }}), charms and the uneasy border between safe ground and dangerous wetland. South-east of Cambridge, the Gog Magog Hills work differently. They are chalk country: raised, visible, dry, cuttable and already marked by ancient earthworks. This is why stories here attach themselves not to marsh lights or fen spirits, but to buried giants, hill figures, ancient games, heroic combat and the possibility that the turf itself once carried a huge human outline.

Wandlebury Camp gives the hills their strongest historical anchor. Historic England describes it as occupying a commanding plateau at the crest of the Gog Magog Hills, around 600 m south-west of the Via Devana Roman road. The monument began as an Early Iron Age slight univallate hillfort and was later altered into a larger multivallate hillfort; post-medieval changes added a mansion, stables and formal gardens within the old defences.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://historicengland.org.uk/listing/the-list/list-entry/1009395" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: historicengland.org.uk">[Historic England]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">historicengland.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Historic EnglandWandlebury Camp: a multivallate hillfort, earlier univallate hillfort, Iron Age cemetery and 17th century formal garden r...</span></span></span> Those facts do not prove any giant tradition, but they explain why the place invited legend. A ringed hilltop, visibly ancient and partly altered by later owners, is exactly the sort of site where local memory can turn earthworks into the remains of forgotten powers.

Recent archaeology has kept Wandlebury in public view. The University of Cambridge reported that work begun in late 2024 marked the start of Cambridgeshire’s first complete geophysical survey of an Iron Age hillfort. The same report notes that the 1955–56 excavations by Grahame Clark and Brian Hartley ran alongside T. C. Lethbridge’s contentious investigation of the Gog Magog hill figures.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.arch.cam.ac.uk/news/cambridgeshire-first-complete-geophysical-survey-iron-age-hillfort-begins-wandlebury" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: arch.cam.ac.uk">[Architecture at Cambridge]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">arch.cam.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cam.ac.uk.</span></span></span> This overlap is revealing: Wandlebury is not just a scenic local walk, but a place where archaeology, folklore and disputed interpretation have repeatedly met.

## Giants, landscape names and local memory

The name “Gog Magog” pulls Cambridgeshire into a much wider legendary tradition. In British pseudo-history, Gogmagog is the giant opponent associated with the founding legends of Britain, while later English tradition also made Gog and Magog into guardian giants of London civic pageantry. The Museum of Cambridge summarises the local version by connecting Gogmagog with the giant race of Albion, the combat with Corineus, and the later idea that the story became attached to the hills near Cambridge when a giant’s image was cut into one of them.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.museumofcambridge.org.uk/2021/03/gog-magog-the-cambridgeshire-giants/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: museumofcambridge.org.uk">[The Museum of Cambridge]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">museumofcambridge.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on museumofcambridge.org.uk.</span></span></span>

The most important point for readers is that the Cambridgeshire hills are not simply “where Gogmagog lived” in an unbroken ancient tale. The evidence is more complicated and more interesting. Peter Meadows, writing for *Antiquity*, identifies Wandlebury, or Gogmagog, as a flat-topped hill about 8 km south-east of Cambridge and sets out the evidence for two reported chalk-cut giant figures, plus a later “goddess” figure claimed by T. C. Lethbridge in the 1950s. Meadows is careful: all of these figures are now covered, lost or obliterated, and the evidence has to be reconstructed from written references, antiquarian testimony and later excavation claims.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://antiquity.ac.uk/projgall/meadows343" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: antiquity.ac.uk">[antiquity.ac.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">antiquity.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Gogmagog &#124; Antiquity Journal</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Gogmagog &#124; Antiquity Journal</span></span></span>

The earliest useful evidence for a giant figure belongs to the early modern period rather than to prehistory. Meadows notes that Bishop Joseph Hall’s *Mundus alter et idem*, around 1605, refers to a giant picture that Cambridge scholars went to see at the Gog Magog Hills. John Layer, writing around 1640, said he had seen a giant cut in the turf, and suggested that Cambridge scholars had made it. Samuel Dale, visiting in 1722 and again in 1729, described a giant figure cut in the middle of the hillfort area and called Gogmagog or Hogmagog.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://antiquity.ac.uk/projgall/meadows343" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: antiquity.ac.uk">[antiquity.ac.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">antiquity.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Gogmagog &#124; Antiquity Journal</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Gogmagog &#124; Antiquity Journal</span></span></span>

That makes the Wandlebury giant an unusually vivid but uncertain local tradition. It is better attested than a vague “there were giants here” rumour, because named observers recorded a turf-cut figure. Yet it is not safely datable as ancient, and one strand of testimony even points towards Cambridge students rather than prehistoric worshippers. The figure may have belonged to the culture of games, student mischief and public spectacle on the hills as much as to old myth.

The Gogmagog games strengthen that reading. Meadows notes that Wandlebury had long been a centre for sports, known as the Gogmagog Olympics; Cambridge University’s vice-chancellor banned scholars from attending in 1574, and later accounts still describe bull-baiting, running, jumping, shooting, wrestling and other exercises on the hills.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://antiquity.ac.uk/projgall/meadows343" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: antiquity.ac.uk">[antiquity.ac.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">antiquity.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Gogmagog &#124; Antiquity Journal</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Gogmagog &#124; Antiquity Journal</span></span></span> In that setting, a turf-cut giant could have acted as a local emblem: part mythic mascot, part joke, part spectacle, and part claim that this modest Cambridgeshire hilltop belonged in the same imaginative world as the giants of Albion.

## The lost chalk giants

The idea of a lost chalk giant at Wandlebury sits beside better-known English hill figures such as the Cerne Abbas Giant, the Long Man of Wilmington and the Uffington White Horse. This comparison is useful, but it can also mislead. Some chalk figures are visible, maintained and dateable through archaeology or documentary records; the Wandlebury figures are not. A 2026 *Guardian* travel feature on English chalk figures describes Wandlebury as the eastern end of a chalk-land journey, but calls Lethbridge’s claimed discoveries speculative and notes that nothing remains of the figures.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.theguardian.com/travel/2026/apr/07/walk-through-mysterious-giant-chalk-figures-southern-england" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theguardian.com">[The Guardian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theguardian.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">He recounts encounters with notable figures such as the Cerne Abbas Giant, King George III’s equestrian figure in Weymouth, and the renow...</span></span></span>

The best-supported Wandlebury figure is the one reported in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries on the plateau. According to Meadows, this giant was in the centre of the games arena and was probably not visible from a distance. Its disappearance may be connected with the transformation of the hillfort interior into a private estate landscape. Samuel Dale recorded in 1730 that the old road over the hill had been stopped after the building of a new brick house near the stables, and Meadows links this to Francis, second Earl of Godolphin, who also laid out a garden over the former games area.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://antiquity.ac.uk/projgall/meadows343" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: antiquity.ac.uk">[antiquity.ac.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">antiquity.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Gogmagog &#124; Antiquity Journal</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Gogmagog &#124; Antiquity Journal</span></span></span>

A second alleged figure belongs to the slopes rather than the plateau. Meadows records later reports that a giant once visible from Sawston, about 3 km to the south-west, had been remembered by an old informant whose story reached Lethbridge through Samuel Cowles and other local antiquarian channels. Lethbridge investigated the south-west slope in 1954 and claimed to find not merely a warrior figure but a goddess in a horse-drawn chariot. Scholarly opinion soon rejected the “goddess” as natural features, plough marks and holes from uprooted trees.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://antiquity.ac.uk/projgall/meadows343" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: antiquity.ac.uk">[antiquity.ac.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">antiquity.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Gogmagog &#124; Antiquity Journal</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Gogmagog &#124; Antiquity Journal</span></span></span>

This is where the Gog Magog Hills become a cautionary tale about folklore and evidence. Lethbridge was not an outsider with no archaeological standing; he had been involved in Cambridge archaeology. But his Wandlebury hill-figure claims went far beyond what most archaeologists accepted. The Prehistoric Society’s site guide says that he used iron rods to probe for disturbed chalk and believed he had found a warrior, a hooded goddess and a sun god, but that his 1957 publication was ridiculed and he resigned from the Cambridge University Museum of Archaeology and Ethnology.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.prehistoricsociety.org/sites/prehistoricsociety.org/files/resources/ps-signpost_wandlebury.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: prehistoricsociety.org">[The Prehistoric Society]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">prehistoricsociety.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Prehistoric Society Microsoft Word</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Prehistoric Society Microsoft Word</span></span></span>

The useful conclusion is not that the whole tradition is worthless. It is that the tradition contains different layers of reliability. A turf-cut giant seen by early modern observers is plausible and historically interesting. Lethbridge’s prehistoric pantheon of chalk deities is far weaker. Modern retellings often blend them together, but a grounded Cambridgeshire folklore page should keep them apart.

## Wandlebury’s older supernatural story

Before the early modern giant figure, Wandlebury already had a medieval supernatural reputation. The Prehistoric Society quotes Gervase of Tilbury’s *Otia Imperialia*, written around 1211, describing a place called Wandlebria near Cambridge: a level hilltop ringed by earthworks, entered through a single gate, where a warrior who came alone by moonlight and called for an opponent would be met by an armed supernatural knight.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.prehistoricsociety.org/sites/prehistoricsociety.org/files/resources/ps-signpost_wandlebury.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: prehistoricsociety.org">[The Prehistoric Society]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">prehistoricsociety.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Prehistoric Society Microsoft Word</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Prehistoric Society Microsoft Word</span></span></span>

This is not a giant story in the simple sense. It is a haunted hillfort story: a challenge, a moonlit ride, a mysterious armed opponent and a dangerous test of courage. Yet it belongs on the Gog Magog page because it shows that Wandlebury was already being imagined as an uncanny martial enclosure centuries before the best evidence for the chalk giant. In other words, the place was legendary before the name became strongly tied to Gogmagog.

The medieval tale also explains why Wandlebury could absorb giant lore so easily. A hillfort is already a human-made scar on the landscape. To later viewers, its banks and ditches could suggest vanished armies, buried rulers, ancient champions or supernatural guardians. When the Gogmagog name and turf-cut giant entered the local record, they did not arrive on neutral ground. They attached themselves to a site already understood as old, enclosed, dangerous and story-rich.

## Cherry Hinton and giants under the chalk

The Gog Magog tradition also survived in smaller local tales, especially around Cherry Hinton. The Museum of Cambridge records that children in Cherry Hinton were discouraged from playing in local chalk pits in case the buried giants Gog and Magog woke and chased them. In the same local account, Gog quarrels with his wife Magog and throws earth at her; the missed throw forms a hill, and another hill is created when Gog buries Magog alive in anger.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.museumofcambridge.org.uk/2021/03/gog-magog-the-cambridgeshire-giants/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: museumofcambridge.org.uk">[The Museum of Cambridge]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">museumofcambridge.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on museumofcambridge.org.uk.</span></span></span>

These stories feel different from the antiquarian Wandlebury material. They are not trying to explain a seventeenth-century turf figure or a medieval Latin marvel. They are domestic, cautionary and local: a way of making chalk pits dangerous to children, turning irregular hills into the result of giant behaviour, and giving the landscape a family drama. Their value lies less in age than in function. They show how the great name of Gog Magog could be brought down to village scale.

The same Museum of Cambridge account notes a dark stone in the car park of the Robin Hood pub at the Cherry Hinton crossroads, about three feet across, with a footprint carved into it.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.museumofcambridge.org.uk/2021/03/gog-magog-the-cambridgeshire-giants/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: museumofcambridge.org.uk">[The Museum of Cambridge]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">museumofcambridge.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on museumofcambridge.org.uk.</span></span></span> Such objects are common in local legend-making: a mark in stone becomes a trace of a giant, saint, devil, horse or hero. Whether the footprint is old, playful or modern, it gives the story a physical anchor. A child can point to it. A walker can detour to it. A pub car park becomes part of a mythic map.

This is one reason the Gog Magog Hills matter within Cambridgeshire’s county identity. They let the county claim a giant-haunted chalk upland as well as college ghosts and fenland witchcraft. The hills do not need to be high to feel large in the local imagination. Against the surrounding low country, a 70-metre chalk rise with a buried giant can become a mountain of memory.

## How chalk-country legends differ from fenland lore

The Gog Magog Hills show how landscape shapes folklore. In Cambridgeshire’s fenland traditions, supernatural danger often comes from openness, water, distance, weather and isolation. Stories attach to drainage, marsh edges, lonely farms, remedies, charms and uncertain boundaries. In the chalk hills south-east of Cambridge, the key materials are different: visible slopes, cut turf, dry ground, ancient earthworks and old roads.

Chalk-country legend is especially good at making marks visible. A figure can be cut into turf. A ditch can become the ring of an ancient camp. A hill can be a giant’s grave. A footprint can be pressed into stone. Even when the Wandlebury figures are lost, the logic of the legend remains visual: people look at the ground and ask what shape is hidden there.

The archaeology strengthens rather than flattens this sense of place. Wandlebury’s hillfort was first constructed in the fourth century BC, fell into disrepair, and was refortified in the early first century AD; later investigations found evidence of earlier occupation, storage pits, weaving activity, animal bone and human remains, including unusual burials.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.prehistoricsociety.org/sites/prehistoricsociety.org/files/resources/ps-signpost_wandlebury.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: prehistoricsociety.org">[The Prehistoric Society]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">prehistoricsociety.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Prehistoric Society Microsoft Word</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Prehistoric Society Microsoft Word</span></span></span> Historic England similarly records Iron Age structures, storage pits, coins, brooches, beads and other finds, as well as later formal gardens and estate works inside the defences.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://historicengland.org.uk/listing/the-list/list-entry/1009395" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: historicengland.org.uk">[Historic England]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">historicengland.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Historic EnglandWandlebury Camp: a multivallate hillfort, earlier univallate hillfort, Iron Age cemetery and 17th century formal garden r...</span></span></span> The result is a landscape where real antiquity and legendary exaggeration sit close together.

That closeness can confuse modern retellings. It is tempting to say that the giants are “ancient” because the hillfort is ancient. The evidence does not support that neat leap. The hillfort is prehistoric; the ghostly knight is medieval; the plateau giant is early modern in the surviving record; the Lethbridge figures are twentieth-century claims; and the Cherry Hinton giant tales are locally remembered folklore whose age is harder to pin down. Keeping those layers separate makes the story more interesting, not less.

## Why the Gog Magog Hills still work as folklore

The modern appeal of the Gog Magog Hills comes from their mixture of accessibility and mystery. Wandlebury is open to the public, and Historic England notes that Wandlebury Camp is publicly accessible.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://historicengland.org.uk/listing/the-list/list-entry/1009395" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: historicengland.org.uk">[Historic England]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">historicengland.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Historic EnglandWandlebury Camp: a multivallate hillfort, earlier univallate hillfort, Iron Age cemetery and 17th century formal garden r...</span></span></span> Cambridge Past, Present & Future describes the country park as 150 acres of parkland with woods, meadows, views, an Iron Age hillfort and an eighteenth-century country estate.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cambridgeppf.org/wandlebury-country-park/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridgeppf.org">[Cambridge PPF]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridgeppf.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cambridgeppf.org.</span></span></span> This means the legend is not sealed away in a manuscript. It is attached to a place people still walk, picnic, run through and explore.

The hills also suit modern folklore because they carry unresolved questions. Was the early modern giant a student-made joke, a festival emblem, a local imitation of older hill figures, or a survival of something earlier? Did a second slope figure ever exist, or did memory, tree lines and Lethbridge’s enthusiasm create a more elaborate pattern than the chalk could bear? Why did a modest ridge become one of Cambridgeshire’s most mythic landscapes? These are not empty mysteries; they are questions created by real but incomplete evidence.

For a public reader, the most grounded way to understand the tradition is to see the Gog Magog Hills as a case family rather than a single tale. At its centre is Wandlebury, a genuine ancient hillfort with a medieval supernatural story. Around it cluster the early modern turf-cut giant, the Gogmagog games, the later Godolphin estate, Lethbridge’s disputed buried gods, Cherry Hinton’s buried giants and modern walking-place identity. Together they give Cambridgeshire a mythic chalk landscape that contrasts sharply with its fenland lore while still belonging unmistakably to the county.

The giants of the Gog Magog Hills are therefore “hidden” in two senses. No confirmed giant figure can be seen today, and the strongest claims about buried chalk gods remain disputed. But the giant tradition is still hidden in the names, the ring of Wandlebury, the old accounts of a turf-cut figure, the cautionary tales told around chalk pits, and the way the hills continue to stand out against the flatter country around Cambridge.
