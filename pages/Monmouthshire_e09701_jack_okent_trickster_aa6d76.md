## Introduction

Jack O’Kent is the border trickster who turns [Monmouthshire]({{ 'where-monmouthshires-border-legends/' | relative_url }})’s hills, scars and stones into the leftovers of wagers with the Devil. In the local stories, he is not simply a giant, magician or folk hero; he is the clever figure who wins by reading the terms of a bargain better than his opponent. Around Abergavenny and [Trellech]({{ 'trellech/' | relative_url }}), that cleverness becomes visible in the land itself: the Skirrid’s broken-looking flank, the proud cone of Sugar Loaf, the distant Malvern Hills, and the Bronze Age stones at Trellech are all drawn into his contests. The tradition belongs to the wider Welsh Marches, especially the Herefordshire-Monmouthshire border, but its Monmouthshire power lies in how it makes familiar landmarks feel deliberately shaped, thrown or dropped. Jack’s tales matter because they show border folklore doing what it does best: turning geography into argument, memory and mischief.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nationaltrust.org.uk/visit/wales/sugar-loaf-skirrid-and-usk-valley/skirrid-history-and-legends" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nationaltrust.org.uk">[nationaltrust.org.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nationaltrust.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nationaltrust.org.uk.</span></span></span>

## The Skirrid, Sugar Loaf and Malvern wager

The best Monmouthshire landscape tale begins as a bet about height. Jack O’Kent argues with the Devil over which is higher: Sugar Loaf, near Abergavenny, or the Malvern Hills across the border. Jack is right. Sugar Loaf stands at 596 metres, while Worcestershire Beacon, the highest point of the Malvern Hills, is about 425 metres. The Devil, furious at being beaten, tries to fix the result by carrying soil to the Malverns. In the National Trust’s retelling, his apron strings break as he crosses the Skirrid, dropping the load and forming the tump at the northern end of the hill.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nationaltrust.org.uk/visit/wales/sugar-loaf-skirrid-and-usk-valley/visiting-the-sugar-loaf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nationaltrust.org.uk">[nationaltrust.org.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nationaltrust.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nationaltrust.org.uk.</span></span></span>

This is a classic Jack O’Kent mechanism: a landscape feature is explained as the accidental residue of a failed trick. The Devil has the strength, but Jack has the measurement. The joke depends on a very local kind of knowledge. Anyone who has seen Sugar Loaf from the Usk Valley understands why it might be imagined as a rival to the Malverns; anyone who knows the actual heights can see why the Devil loses. The story turns topography into a courtroom argument, with Jack as the sharp border advocate and the Devil as the bad loser.

The Skirrid is especially well suited to this kind of legend because its shape already looks interrupted. The name “Skirrid” is derived from “Ysgyryd”, meaning to shake or tremble, and the National Trust notes the massive landslide on its northern tip, with smaller mud flows and landslides still occurring. The hill’s Christian nickname, the Holy Mountain, also has a separate tradition attached to the idea that a landslide happened at the Crucifixion. Jack’s story does not replace that religious explanation; it sits beside it, showing how one striking landform can carry several kinds of memory at once.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nationaltrust.org.uk/visit/wales/sugar-loaf-skirrid-and-usk-valley/skirrid-history-and-legends" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nationaltrust.org.uk">[National Trust]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nationaltrust.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nationaltrust.org.uk.</span></span></span>

Geology gives the sober explanation. The Skirrid is an Old Red Sandstone hill with unstable slopes, and its visible broken edge is associated with landslipping. Folklore gives the memorable explanation. In one Jack O’Kent version, the scar is the result of the Devil’s failed attempt to heighten the Malverns; in another, Jack himself leaps from Sugar Loaf and leaves the mark of his heel on the Skirrid. The point is not that the tale preserves a geological fact in disguise, but that it translates an obvious physical feature into a story people can carry, repeat and locate with their eyes.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Skirrid_Fawr" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[wikipedia.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Skirrid Fawr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Skirrid Fawr</span></span></span>

## Thrown stones and border trickster tales

Jack’s Monmouthshire landscape tricks do not stop at hills. At Trellech, the three great standing stones known as Harold’s Stones are also drawn into his rivalry with the Devil. Cadw describes the monument as a north-east to south-west alignment of three puddingstone uprights, probably dating to the Bronze Age, with the largest stone about 3 metres high. Coflein places such grouped standing-stone alignments in the later Neolithic or early Bronze Age and notes that their original function is uncertain, possibly ritual and perhaps astronomical.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cadwpublic-api.azurewebsites.net/reports/sam/FullReport?id=372&amp;lang=en" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cadwpublic-api.azurewebsites.net">[cadwpublic-api.azurewebsites.net]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cadwpublic-api.azurewebsites.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Scheduled Monument</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Scheduled Monument</span></span></span>

Folklore gives those stones a livelier arrival. One version says Jack and the Devil held a throwing match on Trellech Beacon, the hill east of the village. Jack throws, the Devil throws further, and then Jack throws further still; the Devil storms off, defeated again. Other versions say the stones were thrown from the Skirrid, or from Sugar Loaf, during a game of strength or quoits. The details shift, but the shape of the tale is stable: the Devil challenges, Jack outdoes him, and the landscape keeps the score.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.themodernantiquarian.com/site/103/harolds-stones" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: themodernantiquarian.com">[themodernantiquarian.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">themodernantiquarian.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on themodernantiquarian.com.</span></span></span>

This does not make Harold’s Stones “Jack O’Kent monuments” in an archaeological sense. They are protected prehistoric remains, and their age is far older than the Jack legend in any recoverable form. Their association with Jack is better understood as later explanatory folklore: a way of answering the ordinary but powerful question, “How did these huge stones get here?” Visit Monmouthshire presents the Bronze Age dating alongside possibilities that they were markers, seasonal indicators or ceremonial stones, while local legend adds Jack and the Devil to the interpretive mix.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.visitmonmouthshire.com/things-to-do/harolds-stones-p2321381" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: visitmonmouthshire.com">[Visit Monmouthshire]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">visitmonmouthshire.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Visit Monmouthshire Harold&#x27;s Stones</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Visit Monmouthshire Harold&#x27;s Stones</span></span></span>

The name “Harold’s Stones” shows how crowded one place-name can become. One tradition links the stones to Harold Godwinson and an eleventh-century victory; another says they mark fallen chieftains; Jack’s tale turns them into missiles or gaming pieces. That layering is typical of Monmouthshire folklore. A prehistoric monument, a medieval historical memory and a border trickster legend can all cling to the same stones without doing the same job. The historian asks what can be dated and evidenced; the folklore reader asks why this place kept attracting explanations.

## Why Jack keeps beating the Devil

Jack O’Kent belongs to a large family of “clever bargain” figures: people who deal with a dangerous supernatural opponent and survive by twisting the terms. In some wider Jack stories, he tricks the Devil over crops by making him choose “tops” or “bottoms” and then planting the crop that makes the Devil’s choice useless. In another, he promises the first soul over a bridge and sends a dog across. These are not Monmouthshire hill stories, but they explain why the landscape stories work: Jack’s power is not brute force alone, but cunning.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jack_o%27_Kent" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Jack o&#x27; Kent</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Jack o&#x27; Kent</span></span></span>

That matters because the Monmouthshire tales often look, at first glance, like giant folklore. Stones are thrown, hills are dented, earth is carried in huge loads. Yet Jack is not merely a big man hurling rocks. He is a legal-minded trickster, a borderland negotiator, and sometimes a wizard or cleric. Oxford Reference summarises the tradition as one in which nineteenth-century tales were collected, while an allusion in a play of 1597 shows that Jack O’Kent was already famous earlier. St Mary’s Kentchurch, preserving the Herefordshire side of the tradition, identifies his first printed appearance with Anthony Munday’s play about John a Kent and John a Cumber, dated around 1590, where he appears as an old hermit with supernatural powers.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.oxfordreference.com/display/10.1093/oi/authority.20110803100015126?d=%2F10.1093%2Foi%2Fauthority.20110803100015126&amp;p=emailAw8r25Ys23zzM" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: oxfordreference.com">[Oxford Reference]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">oxfordreference.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on oxfordreference.com.</span></span></span>

The Devil in these tales is dangerous but oddly vulnerable. He can move earth, demand souls and challenge humans, but he can also be trapped by wording, pride and impatience. Jack’s victories are comic, but they are not harmless jokes. They express a border culture in which survival often depends on wit: knowing a boundary, reading a bargain, exploiting a loophole, understanding terrain better than an outsider. The Devil may be supernatural, but in these stories he is also the arrogant challenger who does not know the country as well as Jack does.

## Why the legend crosses county lines

Jack O’Kent cannot be kept neatly inside modern Monmouthshire. His strongest identity is Marcher: he belongs to the old border zone where Wales and England, Herefordshire and Monmouthshire, hill country and river country meet. That is why a Monmouthshire page can centre him without pretending he is only Monmouthshire’s property. St Mary’s Kentchurch places him in the Herefordshire-Monmouthshire border tradition, while the National Trust’s Skirrid material anchors one of his most vivid landscape stories firmly in the Sugar Loaf, Skirrid and Usk Valley landscape.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://stmaryskentchurch.org/legends-of-john-okent" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: stmaryskentchurch.org">[St. Mary&#x27;s Kentchurch]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">stmaryskentchurch.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on stmaryskentchurch.org.</span></span></span>

The border-crossing is built into the wager itself. Sugar Loaf is a Monmouthshire mountain; the Malverns are across the border; the Skirrid is the place where the Devil’s attempted correction goes wrong. The story works as a triangle of landmarks. It asks readers and listeners to imagine sightlines, distances and rival heights across county and national edges. In that sense, Jack is not a loose import from somewhere else. He is a figure made for a border landscape, and Monmouthshire supplies some of his best scenery.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nationaltrust.org.uk/visit/wales/sugar-loaf-skirrid-and-usk-valley/visiting-the-sugar-loaf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nationaltrust.org.uk">[nationaltrust.org.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nationaltrust.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nationaltrust.org.uk.</span></span></span>

Historic county geography also helps explain why the tale feels so at home here. Monmouthshire is a south-east Welsh border county with the Black Mountains reaching into its north-east, and the older county frame is broader than the present principal area. Folklore often follows older roads, parishes, market towns, estates and visible landmarks more readily than council boundaries. Jack’s stories move in exactly that way: from Kentchurch and the Herefordshire edge to Abergavenny, Sugar Loaf, the Skirrid and Trellech.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://realcounties.com/county/monmouthshire/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: realcounties.com">[Historic Counties Institute]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">realcounties.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Historic Counties Institute Monmouthshire</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Historic Counties Institute Monmouthshire</span></span></span>

This is why Jack O’Kent is useful for reading Monmouthshire folklore as a whole. He does not belong to a sealed-off “Welsh myth” box or an “English legend” box. He is a border character, and the tales use the land as a shared stage. The Devil crosses it with a load of soil; Jack jumps across it; stones fly across it; stories travel with walkers, antiquaries, church traditions and local guides.

## How old and well-attested is the Monmouthshire version?

The safest answer is layered. Jack O’Kent as a legendary figure is old enough to have been known by the late sixteenth century, and his tales were collected and retold in later centuries. The specific Monmouthshire landscape explanations are well embedded in local and tourist retelling, especially around the Skirrid, Sugar Loaf and Trellech, but they should not be treated as securely datable prehistoric or medieval traditions just because they are attached to ancient places.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.oxfordreference.com/display/10.1093/oi/authority.20110803100015126?d=%2F10.1093%2Foi%2Fauthority.20110803100015126&amp;p=emailAw8r25Ys23zzM" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: oxfordreference.com">[oxfordreference.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">oxfordreference.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on oxfordreference.com.</span></span></span>

That distinction is important at Harold’s Stones. The stones are prehistoric; the Harold association is historically implausible as an origin story; the Jack O’Kent throwing tale is a folklore explanation attached later to an already mysterious monument. Archaeologist Howard Williams, writing about Harold’s Stones, notes the familiar later association with Jack O’Kent and the Devil and cautions that such satanic associations around ancient monuments often have early modern origins. That does not make the tradition worthless. It simply shifts the question from “Did Jack throw them?” to “Why did later communities explain ancient stones through Jack and the Devil?”<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://howardwilliamsblog.wordpress.com/2018/11/06/harolds-stones-trellech/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: howardwilliamsblog.wordpress.com">[Archaeo]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">howardwilliamsblog.wordpress.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Archaeo Harold&#x27;s Stones, Trellech</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Archaeo Harold&#x27;s Stones, Trellech</span></span></span>

The answer is that Jack’s stories are excellent at domesticating strangeness. A landslip becomes the Devil’s spill. A ridge scar becomes a heel mark. A stone alignment becomes a throwing match. These explanations are not scientific accounts, but they are culturally precise. They show how people turned awkward, prominent, half-understood features into tales about wit overcoming power.

## How Jack O’Kent is retold today

Modern retellings keep Jack visible in the public landscape. The National Trust uses the Skirrid story in visitor interpretation, presenting the hill as a place of history and legend rather than only a walking route. Visit Monmouthshire presents Harold’s Stones as a Bronze Age prehistoric site while also leaving room for local traditions. St Mary’s Kentchurch preserves the border legend from the Herefordshire side, including the burial-wall story and the wider identity of John O’Kent.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nationaltrust.org.uk/visit/wales/sugar-loaf-skirrid-and-usk-valley/skirrid-history-and-legends" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nationaltrust.org.uk">[nationaltrust.org.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nationaltrust.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nationaltrust.org.uk.</span></span></span>

These modern accounts tend to soften Jack into an entertaining folk character, but the deeper pattern remains strong. He is still the person who makes landscape readable as story. Walkers on the Skirrid can see the landslip and remember the Devil’s dropped soil. Visitors at Trellech can stand among the Bronze Age stones and understand why a later tale imagined them as the result of a contest. Looking from Sugar Loaf towards distant hills, the height wager becomes more than a joke: it becomes a way of feeling how wide the old border imagination was.

Within Monmouthshire’s wider folklore, Jack O’Kent sits beside saints’ wells, standing stones, haunted inns, Arthurian Caerleon and winter visiting customs as a different kind of tradition: not a ghostly haunting or a sacred survival, but a comic explanation of form. He makes the county’s hills and stones feel argued into shape. That is why the stories have lasted. They give the landscape a personality, and they make Monmouthshire’s border position part of the joke.
