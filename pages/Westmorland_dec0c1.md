## Introduction

Westmorland should be understood as a historic county rather than a modern administrative area. Much of the old county now lies within Cumbria’s successor arrangements, especially the modern Westmorland and Furness area, but the older county boundaries remain important for folklore because traditional stories often follow parishes, dialect areas, roads and landscapes rather than modern council lines.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://britishfolklore.com/westmorland/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: britishfolklore.com">[britishfolklore.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">britishfolklore.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">British FolkloreDecember 1, 2023...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 1, 2023</span></span></span>

## The landscape that shaped Westmorland’s legends

Westmorland’s folklore grew from a landscape of fells, moorland passes, rivers and isolated farms. For earlier communities, these were not empty spaces but places filled with memory and meaning. A lonely road could become the setting for a warning tale; a spring or ancient stone could acquire a sacred reputation; a hilltop could become associated with supernatural beings or forgotten events.

The county’s prehistoric monuments are among the strongest anchors for this kind of traditional imagination. Around Shap, for example, there is a remarkable concentration of prehistoric remains including stone circles, stone alignments and burial monuments. Archaeologists identify these as monuments dating mainly from the Neolithic and Bronze Age, although later generations often surrounded such places with their own explanations and stories.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://journals.socantscot.org/index.php/psas/article/view/4879" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journals.socantscot.org">[journals.socantscot.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journals.socantscot.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">November 30, 1863...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 30, 1863</span></span></span>

The Shap Stone Avenue and nearby monuments show how ancient landscapes could continue to attract curiosity long after their original purposes were forgotten. The surviving stones became part of a cultural landscape where archaeology and folklore overlap: the physical evidence is ancient, while the stories attached to it belong to many later periods of interpretation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://wikishire.co.uk/wiki/Shap_Stone_Avenue" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wikishire.co.uk">[Wikishire]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wikishire.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Wikishire Shap Stone Avenue</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Shap Stone Avenue - WikishireMay 31, 2017...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 31, 2017</span></span></span>

## The black dogs of Westmorland

One of the most recognisable northern English folklore traditions is the phantom black dog. Across Britain these creatures appear in many forms: sometimes as terrifying omens, sometimes as guardians of particular places, and sometimes as strange household or farm spirits. Westmorland has its own versions of this tradition, including stories connected with the Capelthwaite figure near Milnthorpe.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://caspar-test.fandom.com/wiki/Capelthwaite" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: caspar-test.fandom.com">[caspar-test.fandom.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">caspar-test.fandom.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Capelthwaite &#124; Mythical Beasts Wiki &#124; Fandom</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Capelthwaite &#124; Mythical Beasts Wiki &#124; Fandom</span></span></span>

The Capelthwaite is unusual because it does not fit the simplest image of the evil black dog. Local tradition describes a supernatural being associated with a barn near Milnthorpe that could appear as a large black dog but was also capable of taking other animal forms. In some accounts it helped with farm work, such as gathering sheep, while also behaving as a troublesome and unpredictable spirit. This places it closer to the northern tradition of household spirits and boggarts than purely a death omen.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://caspar-test.fandom.com/wiki/Capelthwaite" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: caspar-test.fandom.com">[caspar-test.fandom.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">caspar-test.fandom.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Capelthwaite &#124; Mythical Beasts Wiki &#124; Fandom</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Capelthwaite &#124; Mythical Beasts Wiki &#124; Fandom</span></span></span>

Another strand of black dog folklore is linked with lonely routes and dangerous landscapes. Traditions recorded around Shap Fell describe beliefs that phantom black dogs could appear on roads and be interpreted as warnings connected with danger or death. Such stories reflect the experience of travellers crossing difficult terrain where fog, darkness and sudden weather changes made journeys genuinely hazardous.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://occult-world.com/shap-fell/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: occult-world.com">[Occult World]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">occult-world.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Occult World Shap Fell – OCCULT WORLD</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Occult World Shap Fell – OCCULT WORLD</span></span></span>

The importance of these stories is not simply whether people believed they were literally true. The black dog acted as a way of expressing fear of isolation, respect for dangerous places and the uncertainty of travelling through wild country after dark.

## Fairies, spirits and the hidden world

Westmorland belongs to a wider northern tradition in which the countryside was imagined as inhabited by unseen beings. Fairies, spirits and other supernatural figures were often associated with particular hills, caves, springs and remote places. These beliefs were not always stories of harmless magical creatures; they could include warnings about respecting boundaries, behaving properly and avoiding places at the wrong time.

The nineteenth century saw increasing attempts to record such traditions before they disappeared. William Henderson’s *Notes on the Folk-Lore of the Northern Counties of England and the Borders* collected customs, beliefs and stories from northern England and neighbouring regions, becoming one of the important Victorian records of local folklore.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://wellcomecollection.org/works/trqfq7fk" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wellcomecollection.org">[Wellcome Collection]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wellcomecollection.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wellcomecollection.org.</span></span></span>

Antiquarians and folklore collectors often preserved traditions that had previously existed mainly through speech. Their work is valuable, but it also requires care: some accounts represent older oral traditions, while others may reflect Victorian fascination with the mysterious past. Folklore is not a fixed archive of ancient facts; it is a continuing process of retelling.

## Witches, cunning folk and village belief

Like many rural parts of England, Westmorland preserved stories about witches, charms and people believed to possess unusual knowledge. These traditions usually belonged to everyday concerns: healing illness, protecting livestock, explaining misfortune or dealing with unexplained events.

The figure of the witch in local folklore was often more complicated than the image of a purely evil character. Some people labelled as witches were feared, while others were respected as healers or wise individuals. Stories about charms, herbal knowledge and supernatural abilities often reveal community attitudes towards outsiders, illness and uncertainty.

Later retellings sometimes transformed local belief into dramatic tales of curses and hauntings, but the original traditions were usually closely connected with ordinary rural life. They show how communities attempted to understand events before modern explanations were available.

## Ancient stones and legendary landscapes

Westmorland’s standing stones have long encouraged stories because they preserve a visible connection with a distant past. Long Meg and Her Daughters, Shap’s stone monuments and other prehistoric sites remain powerful examples of places where archaeology and folklore meet. Historic England records these monuments as prehistoric ceremonial landscapes, while local traditions have continued to give them imaginative meanings.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://historicengland.org.uk/local/locations/westmorland-and-furness/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: historicengland.org.uk">[Historic England]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">historicengland.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Historic England Westmorland and Furness</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Historic EnglandWestmorland and Furness - Local Heritage Hub &#124; Historic England...</span></span></span>

The Nine Standards near Kirkby Stephen provide another example of how landscape features become wrapped in legend. The line of stone cairns has attracted traditional explanations involving armies and ancient events, while historians have also suggested more practical interpretations such as boundary marking. The tension between explanation and legend is part of what makes such places culturally interesting.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://britishfolklore.com/nine-standards" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: britishfolklore.com">[britishfolklore.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">britishfolklore.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Nine Standards</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">British FolkloreDecember 1, 2023...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 1, 2023</span></span></span>

These sites demonstrate a recurring pattern in Westmorland folklore: old monuments survive physically, but each generation adds new layers of meaning.

## Animals, lost creatures and local memory

Animal legends form another important part of Westmorland’s storytelling traditions. One example is the tale of the “last wolf” associated with Humphrey Head near the southern edge of the historic county. According to local legend, a wolf that had troubled communities was hunted down and killed there, although the story belongs more to folklore and later literary retelling than to securely documented medieval history. Modern heritage projects continue to present the tale as part of local storytelling culture.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.westmorlandandfurness.gov.uk/westmorland-and-furness-council-news/2026/story-trail-celebrates-folklore-and-history-grange-library" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: westmorlandandfurness.gov.uk">[Westmorland and Furness Council]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">westmorlandandfurness.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Westmorland and Furness CouncilStory trail celebrates folklore and history at Grange Library &#124; Westmorland and Furness CouncilMarch 13, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 13, 2026</span></span></span>

Such stories often preserve emotional truths rather than straightforward historical records. They remember fears about predators, wilderness and the struggle between settled communities and the natural world.

## How Westmorland folklore survives today

Westmorland’s folklore continues through museums, heritage projects, walking routes, local publications and community storytelling. Modern audiences are often drawn to the same qualities that shaped older traditions: mysterious landscapes, ancient monuments and stories attached to specific places.

Recent community projects have deliberately used folklore to connect people with local history, showing that legends remain part of living heritage rather than merely relics of the past. A folklore story trail in the Grange area, for example, brought together local history, landscape stories and community participation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.westmorlandandfurness.gov.uk/westmorland-and-furness-council-news/2026/story-trail-celebrates-folklore-and-history-grange-library" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: westmorlandandfurness.gov.uk">[Westmorland and Furness Council]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">westmorlandandfurness.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Westmorland and Furness CouncilStory trail celebrates folklore and history at Grange Library &#124; Westmorland and Furness CouncilMarch 13, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 13, 2026</span></span></span>

The lasting appeal of Westmorland’s folklore lies in the way it connects people to place. A stone circle, a lonely fell road or an old village tale becomes more than a landmark: it becomes part of a shared memory of how people have understood and imagined the landscape over centuries.
