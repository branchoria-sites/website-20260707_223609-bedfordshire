## Introduction

Brent Pelham’s dragon legend is one of [Hertfordshire]({{ 'where-hertfordshires-old-stories-still/' | relative_url }})’s clearest examples of a folk story fastened to real village landmarks. The tale says that Piers Shonks, lord of the manor, killed a dragon that had been ravaging the district, only to face the Devil, who claimed the monster as his own. Shonks escaped the Devil’s threat by arranging to be buried in the wall of St Mary’s Church: neither inside the church nor outside in the churchyard. That odd burial place, the carved tomb, the lost yew tree, and the earthworks of a moated manor have kept the story alive as a local landscape legend rather than a free-floating fairy tale.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.hertsmemories.org.uk/content/herts-history/towns-and-villages/brent_pelham/piers-shonks-hertfordshire-dragon-slayer" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: hertsmemories.org.uk">[hertsmemories.org.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">hertsmemories.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">piers shonks hertfordshire dragon slayer</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">piers shonks hertfordshire dragon slayer</span></span></span>



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/Hertfordshire_38dc7a_brent_pelham_dragon_17af29-overview.webp" | relative_url }}" alt="Overview image for Dragon Slayer" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

The likely answer to “Did Brent Pelham really have a dragon?” is no in the literal sense, but the legend is unusually rich in visible evidence. The strongest evidence is not for a monster, but for how a medieval tomb, Christian imagery, parish memory, local family names, and a vanished boundary tree became woven into one of Hertfordshire’s most memorable heroic traditions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.theguardian.com/books/2019/oct/25/hollow-places-christopher-hadley-review-antiquarian-wild-goose-chase" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theguardian.com">[theguardian.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theguardian.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Guardian Hollow Places by Christopher Hadley review – dragons</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Guardian Hollow Places by Christopher Hadley review – dragons</span></span></span>

## The dragon, the yew and the village landscape

In the traditional story, the dragon’s lair lay beneath the roots of an ancient yew tree in fields outside Brent Pelham. Some retellings place it between Great Pepsells and Little Pepsells, turning a field boundary into a scene of danger and heroism. The monster is said to have damaged crops, threatened livestock, and terrorised the neighbourhood until Piers Shonks rode out with weapons, a servant, and swift hounds to hunt it down.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.hertsmemories.org.uk/content/herts-history/towns-and-villages/brent_pelham/piers-shonks-hertfordshire-dragon-slayer" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: hertsmemories.org.uk">[Herts Memories]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">hertsmemories.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">piers shonks hertfordshire dragon slayer</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">piers shonks hertfordshire dragon slayer</span></span></span>

That landscape detail matters. Many English dragon legends are attached to hills, wells, hollow trees, caves or churchyards, but the Brent Pelham story is especially localised. It does not simply say that a dragon once troubled Hertfordshire; it points the imagination towards a yew tree, a lair, a manor site, and a church. The result is a legend that can be walked through as a village map: the dragon outside the settlement, the hero’s manor nearby, and the tomb inside St Mary’s.

The yew is no longer there, but later accounts preserve its importance. The tradition says the tree was felled around the early nineteenth century, and one account notes that the hollow beneath its roots was remembered as a useful gap or stile on a footpath between the two fields. Even if this does not prove anything about a dragon, it shows how a striking natural feature can hold a story in place for generations.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Piers_Shonks" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Piers Shonks</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Piers Shonks</span></span></span>

The yew also gives the legend a deeper folk texture. Yews often stand near churches, boundaries and burial places, and an ancient tree with a hollow or root-cave is exactly the kind of feature that invites story. In Brent Pelham, the tree’s remembered hollow becomes the dragon’s den; the field boundary becomes a threshold between ordinary farmland and monster country. That is how local folklore often works: it gives a familiar feature an explanation that is more dramatic than any survey map.

The village itself strengthens the effect. Brent Pelham is small, rural, and still centred around St Mary’s Church and older houses, with the church officially listed by Historic England as a Grade II building. The parish setting allows the story to feel rooted rather than theatrical: a dragon is imagined not in some distant heroic kingdom, but in a Hertfordshire farming landscape of lanes, fields, church walls and manor traces.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://historicengland.org.uk/listing/the-list/list-entry/1347737" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: historicengland.org.uk">[Historic England]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">historicengland.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Historic England Church of St Mary, Brent Pelham</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Historic England Church of St Mary, Brent Pelham</span></span></span>

## Piers Shonks, the Devil and the church-wall burial

The second half of the legend is the part that makes it more than a simple dragon-slaying tale. After Shonks kills the beast, the Devil appears, furious because the dragon was his creature or favourite. He vows to claim Shonks’s soul after death, whether Shonks is buried inside the church or outside it. Shonks’s answer is legalistic, pious and clever: he is buried in the church wall, occupying the loophole between the Devil’s two conditions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.hertsmemories.org.uk/content/herts-history/towns-and-villages/brent_pelham/piers-shonks-hertfordshire-dragon-slayer" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: hertsmemories.org.uk">[Herts Memories]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">hertsmemories.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">piers shonks hertfordshire dragon slayer</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">piers shonks hertfordshire dragon slayer</span></span></span>

This trick is one reason the Brent Pelham legend remains memorable. Shonks is not only a strong hero; he is a boundary hero. He defeats the dragon with force, then defeats the Devil with placement. The church wall becomes more than architecture. It is a spiritual border, a piece of sacred geography where words, bodies and salvation are argued over.

There are several versions of how Shonks’s burial place was chosen. In one, he deliberately orders burial in the wall. In another, as he lies dying, he fires an arrow and declares that he should be buried where it lands; the arrow flies into St Mary’s and lodges in the north wall. This arrow version gives the story a neat legendary logic, turning the final act of a hunter into the origin of the tomb’s strange position.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.atlasobscura.com/places/tomb-of-piers-shonks" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: atlasobscura.com">[Atlas Obscura]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">atlasobscura.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Atlas Obscura Tomb of Piers Shonks in Brent Pelham</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Atlas Obscura Tomb of Piers Shonks in Brent Pelham</span></span></span>

The tomb itself is the great physical anchor. It is set in a recess in the north wall of the nave at St Mary’s, and local and modern heritage accounts identify it as the tomb of Piers Shonks. The carved slab is usually dated stylistically to the thirteenth or fourteenth century, while the legendary date associated with Shonks is 1086. That mismatch is one of the main reasons historians treat the story as legend rather than straightforward biography.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.quintetchurches.com/brentpelham.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: quintetchurches.com">[quintetchurches.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">quintetchurches.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Quintet Churches Brent Pelham</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Quintet Churches Brent Pelham</span></span></span>

The carving helps explain why the story developed. Accounts of the tomb describe Christian symbols, including the Evangelists, an angel bearing a soul, a cross, and a dragon-like creature. A cross thrust towards or through a dragon’s mouth is exactly the kind of image that could invite a local explanation: someone must have killed a dragon here. The angel with the soul and the wall-burial then help generate the Devil episode. In this reading, the tomb did not merely preserve the legend; it may have helped create it.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Piers_Shonks" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Piers Shonks</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Piers Shonks</span></span></span>

That does not make the story unimportant. Folklore often grows from the need to explain puzzling objects. A tomb in a wall, a dragon on a carving, and a name added or associated later can become the seed of a tale that feels older than any written source. Brent Pelham’s legend is powerful precisely because the village gives readers and visitors something to look at while the story works on the imagination.

## How old is the Brent Pelham dragon story?

The tradition presents Shonks as a figure of the late eleventh century, and the date 1086 is commonly attached to him. That date is attractive because it sits near the Norman Conquest and the Domesday world, giving the legend an air of great antiquity. But the visible tomb slab is generally understood to be later than that, and modern commentators repeatedly note that the date is too early for the monument as it survives.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://hundredparishes.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2025/04/BRENT-PELHAM-and-MEESDEN.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: hundredparishes.org.uk">[Hundred Parishes]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">hundredparishes.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">BRENT PELHAM and MEESDEN</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">BRENT PELHAM and MEESDEN</span></span></span>

The earliest well-known printed antiquarian treatment is associated with Sir Henry Chauncy’s county history of 1700, which helped preserve many Hertfordshire local traditions. Later, William Blyth Gerish gave the legend fresh antiquarian life in *A Hertfordshire St. George*, first published around 1900 and later connected with wider county folklore collecting. A 1900 image of the tomb from Gerish’s work survives through Wikimedia Commons, showing how the monument had already become the key visual proof for the story by the turn of the twentieth century.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File%3AThe_tomb_of_Piers_Shonks%2C_1900.jpg" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: commons.wikimedia.org">[wikimedia.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">commons.wikimedia.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">File:The tomb of Piers Shonks, 1900.jpg</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">File:The tomb of Piers Shonks, 1900.jpg</span></span></span>

This matters because it helps separate three different layers:

**The medieval object:** the tomb and its carving, probably later than the claimed 1086 date.

**The local tradition:** the story of Shonks, the dragon, the Devil and the church-wall burial, preserved by parish memory and antiquarian writing.

**The modern heritage legend:** the version now retold by local history websites, church guides, visitor pages and popular folklore books.

Modern research has treated the legend as a case study in how land and story shape one another. Christopher Hadley’s *Hollow Places* begins with Piers Shonks and uses the Brent Pelham dragon as a way into broader questions about evidence, memory, landscape and the attraction of old local tales. Reviews of the book emphasise that the date and tomb do not line up neatly, but that the legend remains historically interesting because of the layered evidence around it.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.theguardian.com/books/2019/oct/25/hollow-places-christopher-hadley-review-antiquarian-wild-goose-chase" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theguardian.com">[theguardian.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theguardian.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Guardian Hollow Places by Christopher Hadley review – dragons</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Guardian Hollow Places by Christopher Hadley review – dragons</span></span></span>

The sensible conclusion is that the Brent Pelham dragon legend is old enough to be a genuine Hertfordshire tradition, but not old in the simple way the legend claims. It is not a reliable report from 1086. It is a layered story, with medieval art, early modern antiquarianism, nineteenth- and twentieth-century folklore collecting, and present-day local heritage all contributing to the version readers meet now.

## Why the tomb may have made the dragon

The most interesting possibility is that the story grew backwards from the tomb. A medieval viewer, or a later parish storyteller, sees a carved dragon, a cross, an angel with a soul, and a burial in the wall. The question almost asks itself: who is buried here, and why is there a dragon? The answer becomes Piers Shonks, dragon-slayer of Brent Pelham.

This does not require anyone to invent the whole tale at once. Folklore often builds by adding explanations to awkward details. The dragon on the carving explains the monster. The wall tomb explains the loophole in the Devil’s threat. The angel explains the contested soul. The Evangelists, sometimes described with winged forms, may even have encouraged the detail of Shonks’s winged hounds in some versions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Piers_Shonks" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Piers Shonks</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Piers Shonks</span></span></span>

The name is another layer. Sources report traditions connecting the story with a Shank or Shonks family and with a manor site called Shonkes. That does not prove that a historical Piers Shonks killed a dragon, but it does suggest that the legend may have attached itself to real local names and property memories. A hero’s name, a manor earthwork, and a puzzling tomb are enough for a village legend to gain a strong sense of identity.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Piers_Shonks" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Piers Shonks</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Piers Shonks</span></span></span>

The carved date 1086 is especially revealing. It gives the story a dramatic historical anchor, but it also creates trouble for evidence. If the tomb carving is later, the date cannot simply be read as proof. Instead, it should be understood as part of the legend’s self-presentation: a way of saying that this is not just a tale, but an ancient local claim.

## How a local monster tale became heritage

Today, Piers Shonks is part of Brent Pelham’s public identity. St Mary’s Church is not famous as a cathedral, pilgrimage centre or great medieval landmark; it is famous because it holds the tomb of a dragon-slayer. The church’s own history pages and local heritage material place the building within the village’s ecclesiastical past, while public-facing folklore and local-history sites foreground the Shonks tomb as the memorable feature visitors come to see.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.quintetchurches.com/brentpelham.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: quintetchurches.com">[quintetchurches.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">quintetchurches.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Quintet Churches Brent Pelham</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Quintet Churches Brent Pelham</span></span></span>

The legend has also moved into accessible community heritage. Herts Memories, a Hertfordshire local history and memory project, gives a clear retelling of the dragon story and even includes a children’s craft activity based on making a Brent Pelham dragon. That shift is important: the tale is no longer only an antiquarian curiosity or a church guide anecdote, but a piece of family-friendly county folklore.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.hertsmemories.org.uk/content/herts-history/towns-and-villages/brent_pelham/piers-shonks-hertfordshire-dragon-slayer" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: hertsmemories.org.uk">[Herts Memories]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">hertsmemories.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">piers shonks hertfordshire dragon slayer</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">piers shonks hertfordshire dragon slayer</span></span></span>

This is a modern form of folklore transmission: not oral fireside storytelling, but digital heritage tourism, where an unusual grave, a monster story and a village location become shareable.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.atlasobscura.com/places/tomb-of-piers-shonks" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: atlasobscura.com">[Atlas Obscura]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">atlasobscura.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Atlas Obscura Tomb of Piers Shonks in Brent Pelham</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Atlas Obscura Tomb of Piers Shonks in Brent Pelham</span></span></span>

The story’s survival also depends on its compactness. It has all the elements a local legend needs:

* **A named hero:** Piers Shonks, local lord, hunter or sometimes giant.
* **A named place:** Brent Pelham, not a vague “long ago” setting.<span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://www.quintetchurches.com/brentpelham.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: quintetchurches.com">[quintetchurches.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">quintetchurches.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Quintet Churches Brent Pelham</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Quintet Churches Brent Pelham</span></span></span>

* **A lost but remembered landmark:** the ancient yew and its root hollow.

* **A moral shape:** courage against the monster, wit against evil, and sacred space as protection.

This is why Brent Pelham’s dragon remains one of Hertfordshire’s best folklore case studies. It is not just a story about a beast. It is a story about how a parish explains its own strange and beautiful survivals.

## What the legend says about Hertfordshire folklore

Piers Shonks belongs naturally beside Hertfordshire’s other strongly localised legends, such as Jack O’Legs at Weston or the witchcraft traditions around Tring and Walkern. These stories are not grand national myths imposed on the county from outside. They are attached to churches, roads, fields, stones, inns, manors and remembered legal or parish settings. Brent Pelham’s dragon is a perfect example of that pattern.

The legend also shows how Hertfordshire folklore often sits at the meeting point of rural memory and documentary culture. On one side are fields, trees, hounds, monsters and the Devil. On the other are tomb styles, manor names, church architecture, antiquarian books and listed-building records. The pleasure of the Piers Shonks story comes from moving between those worlds without pretending they are the same kind of evidence.

For readers, the most useful way to approach the tale is not to ask only, “Was there really a dragon?” A better question is: why did this village, this tomb and this landscape produce such a durable dragon story? The answer lies in the fit between object and narrative. A carved dragon needs a slayer. A wall burial needs a reason. A vanished yew needs a memory. Brent Pelham gave all three to Piers Shonks.

## Visiting the story today

The heart of the legend is St Mary’s Church at Brent Pelham, where the tomb is set in the north wall of the nave. Historic England lists the church as a Grade II building, and the wider village setting still reinforces the tale’s quiet rural character. Visitor accounts note that the church is small and access may depend on ordinary local opening patterns rather than formal attraction hours, so the legend is best approached as parish heritage rather than as a managed tourist site.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://historicengland.org.uk/listing/the-list/list-entry/1347737" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: historicengland.org.uk">[Historic England]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">historicengland.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Historic England Church of St Mary, Brent Pelham</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Historic England Church of St Mary, Brent Pelham</span></span></span>

Seeing the tomb changes the story. On the page, Piers Shonks can sound like another English dragon-slayer. In the church, the legend becomes more specific: a dark carved slab, a recess in the wall, Christian symbols, a village silence, and the thought that generations of visitors have stood there trying to decide what came first — the dragon, the carving, or the explanation.

That uncertainty is not a weakness. It is the reason the Brent Pelham dragon legend still works. It gives Hertfordshire a monster story with enough physical detail to feel close, enough contradiction to invite investigation, and enough charm to survive as living local heritage rather than a dead antiquarian footnote.
