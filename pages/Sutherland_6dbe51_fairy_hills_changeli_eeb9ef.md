## Introduction

[Sutherland]({{ 'where-sutherlands-old-stories-still/' | relative_url }})’s fairy hills were not gentle storybook places. In the county’s nineteenth-century folklore record, they were dangerous social spaces: hills that could be damaged by careless digging, caverns where music stole a year from a man’s life, and hidden communities blamed for replacing difficult children with changelings. The strongest source for these traditions is Charlotte Louisa [Dempster]({{ 'dempster/' | relative_url }}), who gathered Sutherland tales in 1859 and published much of them in *The Folk-Lore Journal* in 1888; the National Library of Scotland also identifies her manuscript as “Tales from the people of Sutherland”.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Folk-Lore_Journal/Volume_6/Folk-Lore_of_Sutherlandshire_%28September%29" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: en.wikisource.org">[Wikisource]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">en.wikisource.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Folk-Lore Journal/Volume 6/Folk-Lore of Sutherlandshire (September</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Folk-Lore Journal/Volume 6/Folk-Lore of Sutherlandshire (September</span></span></span>



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/Sutherland_6dbe51_fairy_hills_changeli_eeb9ef-overview.webp" | relative_url }}" alt="Overview image for Fairy Hills" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

What makes Sutherland’s fairy belief distinctive is the mixture of fear and familiarity. The fairies are frightening, but they are not vague monsters. They have favourite hills, servants, hunting days, music, hounds, social rules and quarrels with humans. They threaten the household at its most vulnerable point — the newborn child — but they also behave like a parallel landed society, with hunts, pipes and punishments of their own.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Folk-Lore_Journal/Volume_6/Folk-Lore_of_Sutherlandshire_%28December%29" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: en.wikisource.org">[Wikisource]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">en.wikisource.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Folk-Lore Journal/Volume 6/Folk-Lore of Sutherlandshire (December</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Folk-Lore Journal/Volume 6/Folk-Lore of Sutherlandshire (December</span></span></span>

## Why Sutherland’s fairies were dangerous

The fairies in Dempster’s Sutherland material are most often called the “little people” or the “good people”, but the polite names do not make them safe. They are powerful because they sit beside ordinary life rather than outside it. Their stories begin with crofts, barns, servants, babies, christenings, mills, ploughing and journeys to Tain or Dornoch. The supernatural enters through the door of the kitchen, the side of a hill, a burn, a cavern mouth, or the lonely place where a tired walker sits down at dusk.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Folk-Lore_Journal/Volume_6/Folk-Lore_of_Sutherlandshire_%28December%29" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: en.wikisource.org">[Wikisource]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">en.wikisource.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Folk-Lore Journal/Volume 6/Folk-Lore of Sutherlandshire (December</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Folk-Lore Journal/Volume 6/Folk-Lore of Sutherlandshire (December</span></span></span>

Dempster’s own collecting context matters. She said she had gathered the tales “from the mouths of the folk” in the summer of 1859, and by 1888 she believed that this kind of collection would no longer be possible in Sutherland in the same way. She linked the loss to the decline of older oral literature, language change, reading habits, religious hostility to old beliefs, and wider modernisation. That does not make every tale a literal record of belief, but it does show that Dempster understood these stories as survivals of a living local narrative culture, not simply as literary inventions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Folk-Lore_Journal/Volume_6/Folk-Lore_of_Sutherlandshire_%28September%29" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: en.wikisource.org">[Wikisource]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">en.wikisource.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Folk-Lore Journal/Volume 6/Folk-Lore of Sutherlandshire (September</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Folk-Lore Journal/Volume 6/Folk-Lore of Sutherlandshire (September</span></span></span>

The danger of Sutherland fairies works through three main mechanisms:

**First, they own or occupy parts of the landscape.** A fairy hill is not just scenery; it is a dwelling, a boundary and a warning. To dig into it, build from it, or trespass at the wrong moment is to disturb a hidden community.

**Second, they interfere with family continuity.** Changelings turn anxiety about babies, illness, crying, feeding and baptism into a story about theft and substitution.

**Third, they have their own society.** Music, dancing, hunting, servants and food make them feel almost human, but not quite. The result is more unsettling than a simple monster tale because the fairies seem close enough to negotiate with and alien enough to ruin a life.

## The little people of Torr Berrichan

Dempster names three conical hills as the principal fairy haunts of Sutherland, adding that two of them were called Torr Berrichan. These hills were associated with “dressed fairies”: land fairies who wore green, kept hounds, used horns and bagpipes, enjoyed reel-tunes, and hunted several days a week.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Folk-Lore_Journal/Volume_6/Folk-Lore_of_Sutherlandshire_%28December%29" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: en.wikisource.org">[Wikisource]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">en.wikisource.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Folk-Lore Journal/Volume 6/Folk-Lore of Sutherlandshire (December</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Folk-Lore Journal/Volume 6/Folk-Lore of Sutherlandshire (December</span></span></span>

This is a striking image. Sutherland’s fairies are not merely small glowing beings or vague spirits. They are imagined through the language of rural hierarchy and sporting culture. They have meets and hunting calls “like their betters”, which makes them a miniature version of a human elite. In a county of estates, deer forests, crofts and social difference, that detail gives the fairy world a local flavour. The fairies are below human sight, but they are not below human status; if anything, they mimic or parody power.

The Donald Gow episode makes the point neatly. Donald is resting after ploughing when he hears the fairy hunt and the faint blowing of horns. Two ugly hounds run up and sniff him, but a voice calls them off: he is “only old Donald Gow”. The scene is frightening, but it is also oddly comic. Donald is spared not because he has defeated the fairies, but because he has been recognised as harmless.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Folk-Lore_Journal/Volume_6/Folk-Lore_of_Sutherlandshire_%28December%29" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: en.wikisource.org">[Wikisource]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">en.wikisource.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Folk-Lore Journal/Volume 6/Folk-Lore of Sutherlandshire (December</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Folk-Lore Journal/Volume 6/Folk-Lore of Sutherlandshire (December</span></span></span>

That small moment tells us a great deal about Sutherland fairy belief. The fairies are dangerous, but they are not random. They know local people. They move through familiar ground. They may terrify a ploughman, yet they also obey commands within their own company. The fairy hill is therefore not a remote fantasy kingdom; it is a neighbouring estate with unpredictable inhabitants.

## Fairy hills and damage to the land

The hill of Durcha, near the burn of Invernauld on the Rosehall estate, gives another side of the tradition. Dempster records that the place was still believed to be haunted by fairies who had chased a man into the sea and destroyed a new mill because earth for the mill-dam embankment had been dug from the side of their hill.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Folk-Lore_Journal/Volume_6/Folk-Lore_of_Sutherlandshire_%28December%29" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: en.wikisource.org">[Wikisource]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">en.wikisource.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Folk-Lore Journal/Volume 6/Folk-Lore of Sutherlandshire (December</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Folk-Lore Journal/Volume 6/Folk-Lore of Sutherlandshire (December</span></span></span>

This is one of the clearest Sutherland examples of a fairy hill functioning as protected ground. The point is not simply that fairies live in hills. The hill has rights. Human improvement — in this case mill-building — becomes dangerous when it treats the mound as ordinary material. The story turns an act of practical engineering into a violation of a hidden household.

For modern readers, this is one of the most revealing parts of the belief. Fairy stories often preserve a kind of moral geography. Certain banks, mounds, burns and holes are not neutral. They are places where custom warns people to be careful, to avoid greed, to respect old ground, or at least to remember that not every useful patch of earth should be cut away. Later local retellings of the Durcha tradition continue to identify it as a fairy-haunted place, though the underlying source remains Dempster’s nineteenth-century account.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.themodernantiquarian.com/post/56600/folklore/durcha" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: themodernantiquarian.com">[The Modern Antiquarian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">themodernantiquarian.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Modern Antiquarian Durcha (Broch) – Folklore by Rhiannon</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Modern Antiquarian Durcha (Broch) – Folklore by Rhiannon</span></span></span>

The same hill is also the setting for a time-slip fairy dance. A man returning from Tain after registering the birth of his first child and buying whisky for the christening rests near a cavern with a companion. Hearing pipes and dancing, he enters, disappears, and is eventually pulled out by his friend after a year and a day. To the dancer, almost no time has passed; he only complains that he has been stopped before finishing his reel.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Folk-Lore_Journal/Volume_6/Folk-Lore_of_Sutherlandshire_%28December%29" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: en.wikisource.org">[Wikisource]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">en.wikisource.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Folk-Lore Journal/Volume 6/Folk-Lore of Sutherlandshire (December</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Folk-Lore Journal/Volume 6/Folk-Lore of Sutherlandshire (December</span></span></span>

The tale turns a joyous family errand into a near-disaster. The man has gone to confirm a child’s place in the human community — through registration, baptism and christening celebration — but fairy music interrupts him. In Sutherland’s fairy stories, the hill is dangerous because it can pull a person out of ordinary time just when family life needs him most.

## Changeling children and household fear

The best-known Sutherland changeling tale in Dempster’s collection begins with a tailor and his wife who have one son. The child is difficult: he cries constantly, is cross, and gives more pain than pleasure. When the parents leave him at home under the care of a farm-servant called Donald, the child suddenly speaks and behaves with unnatural authority. He sends Donald to a brae, tells him to knock three times, and has him borrow “Johnnie’s flail” from a fairy little man. With this fairy tool, the child and servant thresh a whole quantity of straw impossibly quickly.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Folk-Lore_Journal/Volume_6/Folk-Lore_of_Sutherlandshire_%28December%29" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: en.wikisource.org">[Wikisource]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">en.wikisource.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Folk-Lore Journal/Volume 6/Folk-Lore of Sutherlandshire (December</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Folk-Lore Journal/Volume 6/Folk-Lore of Sutherlandshire (December</span></span></span>

When the parents return, Donald tells the truth. The tailor and his wife conclude that the child is not theirs. Their solution is severe: they put the suspected changeling in a creel and set it on the fire. The being vanishes up the chimney, and the real child is then found shivering outside the door, recognised by his mother from his frock.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Folk-Lore_Journal/Volume_6/Folk-Lore_of_Sutherlandshire_%28December%29" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: en.wikisource.org">[Wikisource]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">en.wikisource.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Folk-Lore Journal/Volume 6/Folk-Lore of Sutherlandshire (December</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Folk-Lore Journal/Volume 6/Folk-Lore of Sutherlandshire (December</span></span></span>

This is a disturbing story, and it should be read carefully. As folklore, it gives narrative shape to real household fears: a baby who will not stop crying, a child who seems unlike other children, parents exhausted by care, and the terror that something has gone wrong beyond ordinary explanation. It also shows how fairy belief could imagine rescue through an ordeal that modern readers will rightly find alarming.

Dempster includes another changeling case from Betsey Ross of Altass. A woman leaves her baby under a hedge while she is shearing, checking on it from time to time. When she returns to feed it, the baby cries in a dreadful way. A reaping man tells her to lay it down and leave it “as you value your child”. Later, he explains that he had seen the fairies carry off the true infant and leave a changeling behind; when the changeling was ignored and unfed, the fairies took it back and restored the human child.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Folk-Lore_Journal/Volume_6/Folk-Lore_of_Sutherlandshire_%28December%29" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: en.wikisource.org">[Wikisource]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">en.wikisource.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Folk-Lore Journal/Volume 6/Folk-Lore of Sutherlandshire (December</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Folk-Lore Journal/Volume 6/Folk-Lore of Sutherlandshire (December</span></span></span>

This second tale is less theatrical than the creel-on-fire episode, but it exposes the same anxiety. The danger moment is not deep in the wild mountains. It happens during work, in daylight, while a mother is balancing childcare and labour. The fairy threat attaches to a practical rural situation: a baby placed nearby so that a woman can keep working.

## Baptism, blessing and the vulnerable child

Dempster’s notes state directly that fairies would not steal a baptised child, and that saying “Bless you” over an unbaptised child acted as a charm against their power.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Folk-Lore_Journal/Volume_6/Folk-Lore_of_Sutherlandshire_%28December%29" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: en.wikisource.org">[Wikisource]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">en.wikisource.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Folk-Lore Journal/Volume 6/Folk-Lore of Sutherlandshire (December</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Folk-Lore Journal/Volume 6/Folk-Lore of Sutherlandshire (December</span></span></span> This detail ties Sutherland changeling belief to a wider Christian household framework. The child is at risk in the interval before formal protection, and ordinary speech can matter as much as formal ritual.

This helps explain why the time-slip story at Durcha is attached to a christening errand. The father is on his way back from arranging public recognition of the birth and buying whisky for the celebration. In a community where baptism marked a child’s safer place in the human and Christian order, the fairies are especially threatening around childbirth, naming and early infancy.

The point is not that Sutherland folklore is simply “pagan” material with a Christian layer pasted on top. The stories as recorded are already mixed. Fairies may be called the “good people”, but Dempster also says they were imagined as “spirits in prison”, and she records a Strath Oikel tale in which a fairy-like figure asks whether Scripture offers any hope of salvation for beings like her.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Folk-Lore_Journal/Volume_6/Folk-Lore_of_Sutherlandshire_%28December%29" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: en.wikisource.org">[Wikisource]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">en.wikisource.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Folk-Lore Journal/Volume 6/Folk-Lore of Sutherlandshire (December</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Folk-Lore Journal/Volume 6/Folk-Lore of Sutherlandshire (December</span></span></span>

That religious unease makes the fairies more complicated. They are not safely inside Christian society, but neither are they outside its imagination. They occupy an uncertain moral category: beautiful or merry at times, useful or civil in some encounters, yet spiritually troubling and dangerous around children.

## Music, hunting and fairy social worlds

Sutherland fairy stories are full of sound: knocking on a brae, flails beating straw, pipes from a cavern, reel-tunes under a hill, horns across the ploughland, and the cry of fairy hounds. These sounds are important because they announce an organised world just out of sight. A fairy encounter often begins when a human hears something that should not be there.

The dancing story makes music seductive. The man enters the cavern because he hears piping and dancing; once inside, he loses a year. The danger is not pain but pleasure. Fairy time is attractive enough that the victim does not understand he has been absent.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Folk-Lore_Journal/Volume_6/Folk-Lore_of_Sutherlandshire_%28December%29" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: en.wikisource.org">[Wikisource]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">en.wikisource.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Folk-Lore Journal/Volume 6/Folk-Lore of Sutherlandshire (December</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Folk-Lore Journal/Volume 6/Folk-Lore of Sutherlandshire (December</span></span></span>

The Donald Gow hunting story makes sound aristocratic. The horns and hounds create a fairy hunt that resembles the sporting life of human landowners. The fairies have leisure, hierarchy and command. Their world is not chaotic; it is socially patterned.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Folk-Lore_Journal/Volume_6/Folk-Lore_of_Sutherlandshire_%28December%29" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: en.wikisource.org">[Wikisource]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">en.wikisource.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Folk-Lore Journal/Volume 6/Folk-Lore of Sutherlandshire (December</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Folk-Lore Journal/Volume 6/Folk-Lore of Sutherlandshire (December</span></span></span>

The changeling tale makes sound domestic and labouring. Fairy power enters through the borrowed flail, a tool for threshing. The uncanny child is not merely strange because he cries; he is strange because he knows how to access a hidden labour force. The fairy world can do human work faster than humans, but the price is terror.

Together, these stories show why Sutherland’s fairies were so dangerous. They were not dangerous only because they harmed people. They were dangerous because they made an alternative society feel close: one with its own music, tools, animals, feasts, servants and rules. Humans might benefit from it for an hour, but they could not safely belong to it.

## What the Sutherland record can and cannot prove

The evidence for Sutherland’s fairy hills and changeling belief is unusually vivid, but it is still mostly mediated through nineteenth-century collecting. Dempster is the central witness. Her 1859 manuscript is real and catalogued, and most of the tales were later published in *The Folk-Lore Journal*, but the printed texts are not audio recordings or verbatim transcripts in the modern sense. They are literary presentations of oral material, sometimes with Dempster’s comparative notes attached.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://manuscripts.nls.uk/repositories/2/archival_objects/25033" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: manuscripts.nls.uk">[National Library of Scotland Manuscripts]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">manuscripts.nls.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nls.uk.</span></span></span>

John Francis Campbell’s *Popular Tales of the West Highlands* also printed Sutherland fairy material and described the county’s fairy creed as broadly similar to elsewhere in Scotland while noting a local generic term for supernatural beings. His version includes the changeling tale, showing that the story circulated beyond Dempster’s later journal publication and was recognised within the wider Highland tale-collecting world.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://sacred-texts.com/neu/celt/pt2/pt214.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sacred-texts.com">[Internet Sacred Text Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sacred-texts.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on sacred-texts.com.</span></span></span>

That means the safest reading is neither sceptical dismissal nor romantic overstatement. We can say that fairy hills, changelings, fairy labour, fairy music and fairy hunting were strongly represented in the Sutherland folklore record collected in the mid-nineteenth century. We should be more cautious about claiming exactly how widely each tale was believed, how old each version was, or whether every named location can be matched cleanly to a modern map point.

The stories matter because they show Sutherland’s fairy tradition working as a mechanism, not just a motif. Fairy belief explained why certain hills should not be cut, why babies were vulnerable before baptism, why unexplained childhood distress felt terrifying, why music and lonely caverns were risky, and why the ordinary landscape seemed to contain another people living beside the human one.

## Why these fairy hills still matter in Sutherland folklore

Sutherland’s fairy hills endure because they make the county’s landscape feel inhabited by memory. A conical hill is not only a hill. A burn is not only a burn. A cavern is not only a hollow in the ground. In these stories, each can become a threshold between the crofting household and the hidden social world of the little people.

The changeling stories are harder to enjoy because they touch real pain: infancy, disability, parental fear, women’s work, hunger, exhaustion and the question of how communities interpreted children who seemed unusually distressed or different. Their value today is not that they offer a good explanation, but that they preserve an old emotional one. They show how families once turned bewildering domestic trouble into a story with agents, rules and possible remedies.

The fairy-hunt stories, by contrast, preserve the glamour and comedy of the tradition. Donald Gow survives because the fairy huntsman recognises him. The dancer loses a year because the reel is too compelling. The fairy child gives himself away because he knows too much and works too well. These are not flat horror tales. They are lively, unsettling stories about a hidden society that mirrors Sutherland’s own world while remaining beyond human control.

Within the wider folklore of Sutherland, fairy hills and changelings form one of the county’s strongest supernatural strands. They sit naturally beside water-fairies, kelpies, witchcraft stories, saints’ legends and haunted roads, but they have their own centre of gravity: the dangerous nearness of the little people, under the hill and beside the hearth.
