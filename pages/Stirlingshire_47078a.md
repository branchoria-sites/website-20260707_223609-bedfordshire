## Introduction

For this page, “Stirlingshire” means the historic county rather than only the modern Stirling Council area. That matters because historic Stirlingshire includes places now associated with several modern authorities, including Stirling and Falkirk. The project’s mapping frame follows historic county geography: the Wikimedia Commons/Wikishire historic-counties map describes the British Isles in terms of historic counties before the major modern local-government reforms, while Wikishire places Stirlingshire between Perthshire, Clackmannanshire, West Lothian, Lanarkshire and Dunbartonshire.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File%3ABritish_Isles_map_showing_UK%2C_Republic_of_Ireland%2C_and_historic_counties.svg" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: commons.wikimedia.org">[Wikimedia Commons]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">commons.wikimedia.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wikimedia.org.</span></span></span>

## Why Stirlingshire folklore feels like borderland folklore

Stirlingshire sits at a geographical and imaginative hinge. Stirling Old Bridge was one of Scotland’s critical river crossings; Historic Environment Scotland describes Stirling as the “gateway to the Highlands” and notes that the bridge was the main crossing of the River Forth until the early nineteenth century. That practical fact helps explain why so much local tradition feels charged with passage: armies cross here, saints’ wells mark places of healing and anxiety, and fairy hills sit at the edge of villages and old routes.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.historicenvironment.scot/visit/all/stirling-old-bridge/history-and-stories/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: historicenvironment.scot">[Historic Environment Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">historicenvironment.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on historicenvironment.scot.</span></span></span>

The county’s folklore is also shaped by how history and legend overlap. Stirling Castle is not merely a scenic backdrop for ghost stories: it was a royal and military stronghold, and Historic Environment Scotland calls it one of Scotland’s greatest stone castles, with fortification on the crag reaching back into ancient times and a peak of royal importance in the sixteenth century. Stories attached to such places tend to gather around known historical tensions — monarchy, war, fire, imprisonment, betrayal — even when the supernatural details are later, uncertain or openly legendary.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.historicenvironment.scot/visit/all/stirling-castle/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: historicenvironment.scot">[Historic Environment Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">historicenvironment.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Historic Environment Scotland Stirling Castle &#124; Historic Environment Scotland</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Historic Environment Scotland Stirling Castle &#124; Historic Environment Scotland</span></span></span>

This borderland quality also explains why Stirlingshire traditions cross modern boundaries. Aberfoyle now sits within the Loch Lomond and The Trossachs National Park, Falkirk’s Kelpies belong to a modern visitor landscape, and Stirling’s old-town stories are told through heritage walks and civic interpretation. The folklore remains Stirlingshire in historic-county terms, but its present-day retelling often belongs to tourism bodies, archives, walking routes, museums and local heritage organisations.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.lochlomond-trossachs.org/things-to-do/walking/hillwalking/doon-hill-and-fairy-knowe/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: lochlomond-trossachs.org">[lochlomond-trossachs.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">lochlomond-trossachs.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Loch Lomond &amp; Trossachs NP</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Doon Hill and Fairy Knowe - Here. Now. All of us. - Loch Lomond &amp; The Trossachs National Park...</span></span></span>

## Robert Kirk and Doon Hill: Stirlingshire’s great fairy tradition

The clearest reason Stirlingshire matters in British fairy lore is Robert Kirk of Aberfoyle. Kirk was a seventeenth-century minister and Gaelic scholar whose *Secret Commonwealth of Elves, Fauns and Fairies* became one of the classic texts of Scottish fairy belief. The National Library of Scotland catalogue identifies him as later minister of Aberfoyle and author of *The Secret Commonwealth*, while the Project Gutenberg text of the 1893 edition describes the work as written in 1691 by “the Rev. Robert Kirk, minister of Aberfoyle” and concerned with the metaphysics of the fairy world.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://manuscripts.nls.uk/repositories/2/resources/21231" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: manuscripts.nls.uk">[manuscripts.nls.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">manuscripts.nls.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nls.uk.</span></span></span>

What makes Kirk especially valuable is that he was not simply inventing a fairy tale for children. The Library of Congress describes *The Secret Commonwealth* as a book about what people in Kirk’s time and community believed fairies were, where and how they lived, and how they interacted with human beings. In other words, Aberfoyle’s fairy tradition is unusually well-attested because it connects local belief, clerical curiosity, Gaelic scholarship and later antiquarian publication.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://blogs.loc.gov/law/2022/10/the-laws-of-the-good-people-fairy-lore-in-robert-kirks-secret-commonwealth/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: blogs.loc.gov">[The Library of Congress]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">blogs.loc.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on loc.gov.</span></span></span>

Doon Hill gives the text a powerful local setting. The modern National Park walking route tells visitors to look for the ancient pine at the top of Doon Hill and recounts the legend that the tree is a doorway to an underground Fairy Queen’s palace, where Kirk’s spirit was trapped after he revealed fairy secrets. Go Aberfoyle similarly presents Doon Hill as the home of the fairies and links the site to Kirk’s grave, the fairy tree, and the continuing custom of visitors tying ribbons or notes there.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.lochlomond-trossachs.org/things-to-do/walking/hillwalking/doon-hill-and-fairy-knowe/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: lochlomond-trossachs.org">[Loch Lomond &amp; Trossachs NP]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">lochlomond-trossachs.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Doon Hill and Fairy Knowe - Here. Now. All of us. - Loch Lomond &amp; The Trossachs National Park...</span></span></span>

The strongest way to read the Kirk legend is as layered tradition. The historical Kirk died in 1692; the supernatural version says he was not truly dead but held captive in Fairyland. Lizanne Henderson’s account in *The Bottle Imp* notes that Kirk set off on an evening walk on Doon Hill on 14 May 1692, was found collapsed there, and was buried at Kirkton, while the later legend claimed he had appeared with instructions for his release from Fairyland. That gap between recorded death and fairy abduction is exactly where folklore works: it turns a local minister’s sudden death into a story about forbidden knowledge, fairy secrecy and the danger of crossing too far into the otherworld.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.thebottleimp.org.uk/2016/12/the-supernatural-worlds-of-robert-kirk-fairies-beasts-landscapes-and-lychnobious-liminalities/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: thebottleimp.org.uk">[The Bottle Imp]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">thebottleimp.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on thebottleimp.org.uk.</span></span></span>

## Fairy hills, cairns and the older landscape

Doon Hill is not the only Stirlingshire place where fairy tradition attaches to a mound. The Hill of Airthrey Fairy Knowe, near Bridge of Allan, shows how prehistoric monuments often became story-sites in later belief. Historic Environment Scotland records Fairy Knowe as a scheduled prehistoric ritual and funerary cairn in the parish of Logie, while antiquarian and folklore sources preserve its local name and later fairy associations.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://portal.historicenvironment.scot/apex/f?p=1505%3A300%3A%3A%3A%3A%3AVIEWTYPE%2CVIEWREF%3Adesignation%2CSM2543" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: portal.historicenvironment.scot">[Historic Environment Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">portal.historicenvironment.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on historicenvironment.scot.</span></span></span>

This is a useful example of how to separate archaeology from folklore without stripping the place of meaning. The cairn’s legal designation tells us it is nationally important as a prehistoric ritual or funerary monument; the name “Fairy Knowe” and later stories tell us how local people explained, remembered or imaginatively inhabited a mysterious mound long after its original purpose had been forgotten.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://portal.historicenvironment.scot/apex/f?p=1505%3A300%3A%3A%3A%3A%3AVIEWTYPE%2CVIEWREF%3Adesignation%2CSM2543" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: portal.historicenvironment.scot">[Historic Environment Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">portal.historicenvironment.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on historicenvironment.scot.</span></span></span>

Stirlingshire therefore fits a wider Scottish pattern: fairy places often cluster around mounds, burial cairns, hills, wells and liminal paths. What is distinctive here is not that every story is ancient in its surviving form, but that the county gives unusually concrete anchors for the motif. At Aberfoyle there is a named minister, a dated death, a famous text and a walkable hill; at Airthrey there is a scheduled cairn with a fairy name and later literary retelling.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.thebottleimp.org.uk/2016/12/the-supernatural-worlds-of-robert-kirk-fairies-beasts-landscapes-and-lychnobious-liminalities/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: thebottleimp.org.uk">[thebottleimp.org.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">thebottleimp.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on thebottleimp.org.uk.</span></span></span>

## Witches, healers and St Ninian’s Well

Stirlingshire’s witchcraft traditions should be handled carefully. They are not cosy Halloween tales about broomsticks; they are records of accusation, fear, punishment and contested healing practices in early modern Scotland. Stirling City Heritage Trust summarises the wider Scottish witch-hunting context by noting that between 4,000 and 6,000 people were tried for witchcraft in Scotland, with execution estimates exceeding 1,500, though the precise number is uncertain.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.stirlingcityheritagetrust.org/blog/stirlings-witches" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: stirlingcityheritagetrust.org">[stirlingcityheritagetrust.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">stirlingcityheritagetrust.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Stirling’s Witches</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Stirling’s Witches</span></span></span>

The most locally vivid case is Bessie Stevenson. Your Stirling’s Women in Stirling Trail states that in 1659 Bessie Stevenson, Magdalena Blair and Isobel Bennet were tried on witchcraft-related charges at Stirling Tolbooth, in what it describes as the largest ever witch trial in Stirling, involving twelve people. It also records the key folkloric detail: Bessie’s alleged charms and cures involved St Ninian’s Well, including washing the clothes of the sick and transferring illness to the clothes.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.yourstirling.com/women-in-stirling-trail/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: yourstirling.com">[Your Stirling]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">yourstirling.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Your Stirling Women in Stirling Trail</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Your Stirling Women in Stirling Trail</span></span></span>

That detail links witchcraft history to older well customs. Stirling City Heritage Trust explains the “clootie well” pattern in Scotland: fabric belonging to an ill person could be dipped in water and tied to a tree, with the hoped-for cure taking effect as the cloth decayed. The same source identifies St Ninian’s Well in Wellgreen Place as a holy well and scheduled monument, while Trove records it as an eighteenth-century holy well in the former county of Stirlingshire, with archaeological notes describing the chambered building around the spring.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.stirlingcityheritagetrust.org/blog/stirlings-ancient-wells" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: stirlingcityheritagetrust.org">[stirlingcityheritagetrust.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">stirlingcityheritagetrust.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Stirling’s Ancient Wells</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Stirling’s Ancient Wells</span></span></span>

The result is a powerful local lesson: one person’s cure could become another authority’s superstition. Practices that may have been understood locally as healing, washing, charm-work or inherited custom could be reclassified by church and court authorities as witchcraft. St Ninian’s Well therefore belongs both to Stirlingshire’s sacred-water folklore and to its darker history of criminalising folk medicine.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.yourstirling.com/women-in-stirling-trail/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: yourstirling.com">[Your Stirling]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">yourstirling.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Your Stirling Women in Stirling Trail</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Your Stirling Women in Stirling Trail</span></span></span>

## Stirling Castle ghosts and the making of a haunted royal site

Stirling Castle’s ghost stories are among the county’s most widely retold supernatural traditions. Historic Environment Scotland’s own blog is admirably cautious: it says the organisation cannot claim such stories are true, but recognises that Stirling Castle is “notorious” for tales of what haunts its walls. That is the right frame for readers: these are traditions and reported stories, not proof of haunting.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://blog.historicenvironment.scot/2023/10/ghost-stories-from-stirling-castle/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: blog.historicenvironment.scot">[Historic Environment Scotland Blog]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">blog.historicenvironment.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Historic Environment Scotland Blog Ghost Stories from Stirling Castle</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Historic Environment Scotland Blog Ghost Stories from Stirling Castle</span></span></span>

The best-known castle ghost is the Green Lady. Historic Environment Scotland records several versions of the story, including one in which she appears near the former military base of the Argyll and Sutherland Highlanders. The origin story with the strongest historical hook links her to Mary, Queen of Scots: a young Highland servant supposedly foresaw a fire in Mary’s room on 13 September 1561, tried to keep watch, accidentally set the bed-curtains alight, saved Mary, and died in the blaze. HES notes that records show the fire took place, but that there is no written evidence for the girl or her prophecy.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://blog.historicenvironment.scot/2023/10/ghost-stories-from-stirling-castle/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: blog.historicenvironment.scot">[Historic Environment Scotland Blog]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">blog.historicenvironment.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Historic Environment Scotland Blog Ghost Stories from Stirling Castle</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Historic Environment Scotland Blog Ghost Stories from Stirling Castle</span></span></span>

That distinction matters. The Green Lady is compelling because she sits halfway between documented event and legendary embroidery. A real fire becomes a story about second sight, loyalty, accidental death and a ghostly warning. The same pattern appears in many castle hauntings: the building’s age and violence create a mood, then later storytellers attach a human figure to that mood so visitors can remember it.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://blog.historicenvironment.scot/2023/10/ghost-stories-from-stirling-castle/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: blog.historicenvironment.scot">[Historic Environment Scotland Blog]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">blog.historicenvironment.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Historic Environment Scotland Blog Ghost Stories from Stirling Castle</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Historic Environment Scotland Blog Ghost Stories from Stirling Castle</span></span></span>

Stirling Castle also has other colour-coded and atmospheric ghosts. HES mentions a Black Lady associated with the back walk and a Pink Lady or “pink aura” linked to longing around the kirkyards near the castle. These stories are less firmly tied to named historical events than the Green Lady, but they show how the castle functions as a supernatural stage where grief, danger and memory are given visible form.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://blog.historicenvironment.scot/2023/10/ghost-stories-from-stirling-castle/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: blog.historicenvironment.scot">[Historic Environment Scotland Blog]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">blog.historicenvironment.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Historic Environment Scotland Blog Ghost Stories from Stirling Castle</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Historic Environment Scotland Blog Ghost Stories from Stirling Castle</span></span></span>

## Wallace, Stirling Bridge and the line between history and legend

Not all Stirlingshire folklore is fairy or ghost lore. Some of it is heroic legend: the process by which historical figures become larger-than-life symbols. Stirling Bridge is central to that process because the 1297 battle made William Wallace and Andrew Moray into enduring figures of Scottish national memory. Historic Environment Scotland states that an earlier timber bridge near the present Stirling Old Bridge was the site where Wallace and Moray led a Scottish army to victory over Edward I’s forces.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.historicenvironment.scot/visit/all/stirling-old-bridge/history-and-stories/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: historicenvironment.scot">[Historic Environment Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">historicenvironment.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on historicenvironment.scot.</span></span></span>

The National Wallace Monument’s account shows how quickly history and legend can rub together. It explains the strategic reality clearly: medieval Stirling was the “Gateway to the Highlands”, the bridge was a narrow crossing, and horsemen could cross only two abreast. It also flags one legendary detail: the claim that the English army took three attempts to cross, including a story that John de Warenne overslept.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nationalwallacemonument.com/the-battle-of-stirling-bridge/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nationalwallacemonument.com">[National Wallace Monument]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nationalwallacemonument.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Wallace Monument The Battle of Stirling Bridge</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Wallace Monument The Battle of Stirling Bridge</span></span></span>

The same source makes an important credibility distinction about the bridge collapse. Legend says the Scots sabotaged the bridge to prevent the English retreat, but the National Wallace Monument notes that there is no evidence the bridge was deliberately destroyed. This is exactly how heroic folklore should be read: not dismissed wholesale, but sifted into documented battle, later patriotic storytelling, and dramatic details that make the episode easier to retell.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nationalwallacemonument.com/the-battle-of-stirling-bridge/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nationalwallacemonument.com">[National Wallace Monument]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nationalwallacemonument.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Wallace Monument The Battle of Stirling Bridge</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Wallace Monument The Battle of Stirling Bridge</span></span></span>

For Stirlingshire identity, Wallace lore matters because it turns a local crossing into a national symbol. The River Forth, the narrow bridge, the castle base and the surrounding carse are not just scenery; they explain why Stirling became a place where strategy, memory and legend met.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.historicenvironment.scot/visit/all/stirling-old-bridge/history-and-stories/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: historicenvironment.scot">[Historic Environment Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">historicenvironment.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on historicenvironment.scot.</span></span></span>

## Kelpies at Falkirk: old water spirit, modern landmark

At the eastern side of historic Stirlingshire, Falkirk’s Kelpies show how folklore can be remade as public art. The Helix describes The Kelpies as 30-metre-high monuments to horse-powered heritage, and its tour material presents Andy Scott’s vision as merging Scotland’s past and present. This is modern folklore in a different mode: not an old oral tale preserved unchanged, but a contemporary landmark that borrows a traditional creature-name and gives it a new civic and visual role.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.thehelix.co.uk/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: thehelix.co.uk">[Co]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">thehelix.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">website Helix Park Falkirk &#124; The Helix</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">website Helix Park Falkirk &#124; The Helix</span></span></span>

The kelpie itself is a Scottish water-spirit tradition rather than a uniquely Stirlingshire creature. Modern explainers describe kelpies as dangerous shape-shifting water beings, commonly appearing as horses and associated with rivers, lochs and pools. The Falkirk sculptures soften that older danger into spectacle, engineering and heritage: the frightening water horse becomes a monumental pair of heads beside canals, paths and visitor facilities.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.timberbush-tours.co.uk/news-offers/scottish-folklore-kelpies" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: timberbush-tours.co.uk">[Timberbush Tours]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">timberbush-tours.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Timberbush Tours Scottish Folklore: The Legend of the Kelpies &#124; Timberbush Tours</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Timberbush Tours Scottish Folklore: The Legend of the Kelpies &#124; Timberbush Tours</span></span></span>

That shift is worth noticing because it shows how folklore survives by changing form. In older tellings, kelpie stories could warn children away from dangerous water or encode anxieties about strangers, drowning and uncontrolled nature. At Falkirk, the same name helps brand a public landscape and connect horse-powered industrial heritage with mythic Scotland. It is not “fake folklore”; it is a modern retelling that should be labelled as such.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.timberbush-tours.co.uk/news-offers/scottish-folklore-kelpies" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: timberbush-tours.co.uk">[Timberbush Tours]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">timberbush-tours.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Timberbush Tours Scottish Folklore: The Legend of the Kelpies &#124; Timberbush Tours</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Timberbush Tours Scottish Folklore: The Legend of the Kelpies &#124; Timberbush Tours</span></span></span>

## How well attested are Stirlingshire’s traditions?

Stirlingshire’s folklore is unusually mixed in evidence quality, which is part of what makes it interesting. Some traditions are anchored in primary or institutional records: Robert Kirk’s writings and manuscripts, St Ninian’s Well in heritage records, Fairy Knowe’s scheduled status, Stirling Old Bridge’s historical importance, and the documented existence of witchcraft proceedings.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://manuscripts.nls.uk/repositories/2/resources/21231" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: manuscripts.nls.uk">[nls.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">manuscripts.nls.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nls.uk.</span></span></span>

Other traditions are strong as stories but weaker as history. The Green Lady has a documented fire behind one version, but not documentary proof of the servant or prophecy. The tale of Kirk being held in Fairyland grows around his sudden death rather than replacing the record of burial. The sabotage of the Stirling Bridge collapse is explicitly treated as legend without supporting evidence. These distinctions do not make the stories worthless; they tell us how memory, place and imagination have worked on the historical record.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://blog.historicenvironment.scot/2023/10/ghost-stories-from-stirling-castle/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: blog.historicenvironment.scot">[historicenvironment.scot]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">blog.historicenvironment.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Historic Environment Scotland Blog Ghost Stories from Stirling Castle</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Historic Environment Scotland Blog Ghost Stories from Stirling Castle</span></span></span>

## Stirlingshire’s folklore identity today

Today, Stirlingshire’s folklore is encountered less through fireside oral tradition and more through walks, castle interpretation, archives, monuments, local trails and tourism. That does not make it less meaningful. A visitor can climb Doon Hill, stand near Stirling Old Bridge, visit the castle, seek out St Ninian’s Well, walk around Falkirk’s Kelpies, and see how different kinds of tradition attach themselves to different kinds of place.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.lochlomond-trossachs.org/things-to-do/walking/hillwalking/doon-hill-and-fairy-knowe/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: lochlomond-trossachs.org">[lochlomond-trossachs.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">lochlomond-trossachs.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Loch Lomond &amp; Trossachs NP</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Doon Hill and Fairy Knowe - Here. Now. All of us. - Loch Lomond &amp; The Trossachs National Park...</span></span></span>

The county’s strongest theme is the charged threshold. Fairies live under hills or beyond trees; wells mediate between sickness and cure; bridges decide battles; castles turn history into haunting; kelpies lurk at dangerous waters; and heroic memory transforms a river crossing into national legend. Stirlingshire’s folklore matters because it shows Scotland’s supernatural imagination at a point of passage — between Highland and Lowland, history and story, old belief and modern retelling.
