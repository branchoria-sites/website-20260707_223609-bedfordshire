## Introduction

The Abbots Bromley Horn Dance is [Staffordshire]({{ 'why-staffordshire-feels-ancient-and/' | relative_url }})’s most famous living folk custom: a once-a-year procession in which men carry heavy reindeer antlers through the village and surrounding parish on Wakes Monday, the Monday after the first Sunday after 4 September. It is not simply a staged heritage show. The horns are collected from St Nicholas’ Church in the morning, carried on a long route of roughly 10 miles, danced at farms, pubs, village points and Blithfield Hall, then returned to the church in the evening. That mixture of church keeping, parish route, seasonal timing, old antlers, comic figures and public spectacle is why the custom feels so uncanny and so local at the same time.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://abbotsbromley.com/information/horn-dance" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: abbotsbromley.com">[abbotsbromley.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">abbotsbromley.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Horn Dance – Abbots Bromley Parish Council</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Horn Dance – Abbots Bromley Parish Council</span></span></span>



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/Staffordshire_e11f30_abbots_bromley_horn_183d43-overview.webp" | relative_url }}" alt="Overview image for Horn Dance" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

Its age is real, but also often overstated. The antlers themselves have been radio-carbon dated to the eleventh century, while the earliest clear written description of the horn-bearing dance is Robert Plot’s *Natural History of Stafford-shire* in 1686. The popular claim that the dance began at the St Bartholomew’s fair of 1226 is part of local tradition, but the secure documentary trail is later.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.search.staffspasttrack.org.uk/details.aspx?ExhibitionPage=2&amp;PageIndex=1&amp;ResourceID=24911&amp;SearchType=2&amp;ThemeID=750" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: search.staffspasttrack.org.uk">[search.staffspasttrack.org.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">search.staffspasttrack.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Exhibition Details</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Staffordshire Past Track...</span></span></span>

## What happens on Wakes Monday

On Horn Dance day, Abbots Bromley becomes a moving ritual landscape. The dancers begin by taking the horns from St Nicholas’ Church at about eight o’clock in the morning. From there, the company performs in and around the village, then follows a route through the parish, visiting surrounding farms and pubs before bringing the antlers back to the church late in the day. The parish council describes the whole circuit as about 10 miles, or 16 kilometres, which is why the event is as much a journey as a dance.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://abbotsbromley.com/information/horn-dance" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: abbotsbromley.com">[abbotsbromley.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">abbotsbromley.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Horn Dance – Abbots Bromley Parish Council</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Horn Dance – Abbots Bromley Parish Council</span></span></span>

The main figures are instantly memorable. Six “Deer-men” carry the reindeer antlers. Around them are the Fool, the Hobby Horse, the Bowman, Maid Marian and musicians, with slightly different counts in different descriptions depending on whether accompanying musicians are counted as dancers. Staffordshire Past Track, the county’s local-history archive, describes the usual historic team as six horn dancers in green, three carrying white-painted horns and three carrying darker horns, joined by the Fool, Hobby Horse, Maid Marian, a cross-bow bearer and a melodeon player.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.search.staffspasttrack.org.uk/Details.aspx?ResourceID=11736&amp;SearchType=2&amp;ThemeID=345" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: search.staffspasttrack.org.uk">[search.staffspasttrack.org.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">search.staffspasttrack.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Resource Details - Staffordshire Past Track...</span></span></span>

The route matters because it keeps the custom rooted in Abbots Bromley rather than turning it into a portable performance. Staffordshire Past Track records the dance beginning with the collection of the horns from the parish church, then continuing to places including Blithfield Hall, the Market Place and the Butter Cross before returning to the church. The parish council also warns visitors that during the middle part of the day the dancers are “some distance away from the village centre”, a practical detail that reveals the old shape of the custom: it belongs to lanes, farms and parish edges as well as the picturesque centre.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.search.staffspasttrack.org.uk/details.aspx?ExhibitionPage=2&amp;PageIndex=1&amp;ResourceID=24911&amp;SearchType=2&amp;ThemeID=750" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: search.staffspasttrack.org.uk">[search.staffspasttrack.org.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">search.staffspasttrack.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Exhibition Details</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Staffordshire Past Track...</span></span></span>

The day also has a public festival layer. Exhibitions, craft stalls, pubs and visitor traffic now surround the dance, and parking restrictions are used to keep movement safe. Yet these modern arrangements have not replaced the ritual core. The strange heart of the event is still the same repeated action: the horns leave the church, pass through the parish, and return.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://abbotsbromley.com/information/horn-dance" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: abbotsbromley.com">[abbotsbromley.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">abbotsbromley.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Horn Dance – Abbots Bromley Parish Council</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Horn Dance – Abbots Bromley Parish Council</span></span></span>

## How old is the custom really?

The honest answer is: old, but not as simple as “unchanged since 1226”. Abbots Bromley had a weekly market and an annual fair around St Bartholomew’s Day, and local accounts connect Wakes Monday or Horn Dance Day with that older fair tradition. The parish council states that the Horn Dance was performed at the Barthelmy Fair in August 1226, and Staffordshire Past Track says the tradition is believed to date back to a three-day St Bartholomew’s Day event in 1226. Those statements are important because they show how the village understands its own custom, but they are not the same as a surviving thirteenth-century description of the dance.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://abbotsbromley.com/information/horn-dance" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: abbotsbromley.com">[abbotsbromley.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">abbotsbromley.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Horn Dance – Abbots Bromley Parish Council</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Horn Dance – Abbots Bromley Parish Council</span></span></span>

The strongest early written anchor is Robert Plot’s 1686 account. Plot calls it a “Hobby-horse dance” celebrated at Christmas, New Year and Twelfth Day. He describes a horse figure, a bow and arrow making a snapping sound, and six others carrying “Rain deers heads”, three painted white and three red, decorated with the arms of local leading families. He also says they danced “the Hays, and other Country dances”, placing the custom in a recognisable social-dance world rather than in a fully recoverable pagan rite.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://quod.lib.umich.edu/e/eebo2/A55155.0001.001/1%3A9?rgn=div1&amp;view=fulltext" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: quod.lib.umich.edu">[Quod Libet]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">quod.lib.umich.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on umich.edu.</span></span></span>

Plot’s account also shows that the dance once had a charitable and parish-finance function. He says a pot was kept by local “Reeves”, who provided cakes and ale; villagers and visitors gave money, and after costs were paid the remaining funds helped repair the church and support the poor. That detail is crucial. It suggests that, whatever older meanings the antlers may once have carried, by the late seventeenth century the custom was also a community fund-raiser, church-linked festivity and social obligation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://quod.lib.umich.edu/e/eebo2/A55155.0001.001/1%3A9?rgn=div1&amp;view=fulltext" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: quod.lib.umich.edu">[Quod Libet]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">quod.lib.umich.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on umich.edu.</span></span></span>

There is another complication: Plot says the dance was remembered as a Christmas or New Year custom, while today it happens in September. That shift is one reason the Horn Dance resists easy explanation. It has been attached to more than one seasonal frame: winter festivity in the early written account, and modern Wakes Monday in September, linked locally with the old fair calendar.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://quod.lib.umich.edu/e/eebo2/A55155.0001.001/1%3A9?rgn=div1&amp;view=fulltext" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: quod.lib.umich.edu">[Quod Libet]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">quod.lib.umich.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on umich.edu.</span></span></span>

## Why the antlers are so mysterious

The antlers are the most powerful objects in the custom because they are both physically real and historically puzzling. They are reindeer antlers, not the antlers of ordinary English deer, and sources including Staffordshire Past Track and the English Folk Dance and Song Society describe them as dating to the eleventh century. Staffordshire Past Track gives a radio-carbon date of 1065–1080, while EFDSS gives the broader explanation that carbon analysis found the antlers to date from the eleventh century.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.search.staffspasttrack.org.uk/details.aspx?ExhibitionPage=2&amp;PageIndex=1&amp;ResourceID=24911&amp;SearchType=2&amp;ThemeID=750" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: search.staffspasttrack.org.uk">[staffspasttrack.org.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">search.staffspasttrack.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Exhibition Details</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Staffordshire Past Track...</span></span></span>

That does not prove that the dance itself has been performed continuously since the eleventh century. It proves that the objects used in the dance are exceptionally old. The gap between the age of the antlers and the first secure written account of the horn-bearing custom is one of the most important cautions in interpreting Abbots Bromley. Old objects can enter customs later, customs can be revived or reshaped, and living tradition is rarely a frozen survival from one original moment.

Even so, the antlers give the Horn Dance a force few English customs can match. EFDSS notes that the horns are mounted on wooden heads and poles, and that their colours have changed over time: previously red, blue and white, now brown and off-white. Robert Plot’s seventeenth-century description of three white and three red heads, bearing family arms, shows that colour and local status were already part of how the objects were seen and displayed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.efdss.org/learning/resources/beginners-guides/34-english-folk-costume/2476-efdss-section15-special-costumes1" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: efdss.org">[English Folk Dance and Song Society]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">efdss.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on efdss.org.</span></span></span>

The antlers’ storage in the church is also part of their meaning. Today’s route begins and ends at St Nicholas’ Church, and local historical accounts stress that the horns are collected from the church in the morning and returned there in the evening. This gives the event a ceremonial frame without making it a normal church service. It is a village rite held under church guardianship, with antlers moving out into the everyday world of roads, farms and public houses before being put back into safekeeping.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://abbotsbromley.com/information/horn-dance" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: abbotsbromley.com">[abbotsbromley.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">abbotsbromley.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Horn Dance – Abbots Bromley Parish Council</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Horn Dance – Abbots Bromley Parish Council</span></span></span>

## A church custom, a village custom, or a pagan survival?

The Horn Dance is often described in popular retellings as “pagan”, “ancient” or “fertility” ritual. Those interpretations are understandable: antlers, animal disguise, seasonal procession and a hobby horse all invite mythic readings. But the evidence is more interesting than a single label. The earliest written account we have does not explain the dance as pagan. Plot describes a festive hobby-horse dance with reindeer heads, music, family arms, cakes, ale, donations, church repair and help for the poor.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://quod.lib.umich.edu/e/eebo2/A55155.0001.001/1%3A9?rgn=div1&amp;view=fulltext" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: quod.lib.umich.edu">[Quod Libet]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">quod.lib.umich.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on umich.edu.</span></span></span>

That does not make the custom purely Christian either. Like many English seasonal customs, it sits between categories. It has church storage and church benefit in the record; it has fair-day and parish-route associations; it has animal masquerade and comic folk-play characters; and it has the physical drama of antlers moving through the landscape. Its power comes from that overlap, not from fitting neatly into one origin story.

The safest reading is to treat the Horn Dance as a layered parish tradition. It may preserve fragments of older performance, hunting symbolism, fair custom, winter festivity, household visiting, charity collection and local identity. But no source securely recovers a single original meaning. The dance has survived because it can carry several meanings at once: eerie spectacle for visitors, inherited duty for performers, parish emblem for Abbots Bromley, and Staffordshire folklore landmark for the wider county.

This is why the phrase “living ritual” fits better than “museum piece”. A museum piece is preserved by being separated from ordinary life. The Horn Dance is preserved by being done: by people taking the horns out, walking the old circuit, managing the crowds, playing the music, dancing at recognised points and returning the objects to the church.

## The dance as Staffordshire landscape memory

Abbots Bromley lies in historic Staffordshire, and the Horn Dance is one of the clearest cases in the county where folklore attaches to a specific parish route rather than just to a story. The event is not only “from” Abbots Bromley; it maps Abbots Bromley. St Nicholas’ Church, the Market Place, the Butter Cross, Blithfield Hall, farms, pubs and lanes are all part of how the custom is experienced.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.search.staffspasttrack.org.uk/details.aspx?ExhibitionPage=2&amp;PageIndex=1&amp;ResourceID=24911&amp;SearchType=2&amp;ThemeID=750" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: search.staffspasttrack.org.uk">[search.staffspasttrack.org.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">search.staffspasttrack.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Exhibition Details</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Staffordshire Past Track...</span></span></span>

The local landscape gives the custom extra depth. Abbots Bromley sits within a wider Needwood and east Staffordshire setting of old woodland, farms and estate connections. A local neighbourhood landscape document notes that the village lies on the Needwood Plateau and that the famous annual horn dance may still echo former forest rights. That suggestion should be treated carefully, as an interpretive possibility rather than proven origin, but it helps explain why antlers, routes and estate memory feel so at home here.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://abbotsbromley.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/04/Neighbourhood-plan-landscape-character-built-environment.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: abbotsbromley.com">[abbotsbromley.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">abbotsbromley.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Neighbourhood plan landscape character built</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Neighbourhood plan landscape character built</span></span></span>

The named families in Plot’s description also pull the dance into local landholding history. He records the arms of Paget, Bagot and Wells painted on the reindeer heads, describing them as leading families connected with the revenues of the town. That makes the horns not only wild-looking objects but also social objects: they once carried visible signs of local power, patronage and property.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://quod.lib.umich.edu/e/eebo2/A55155.0001.001/1%3A9?rgn=div1&amp;view=fulltext" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: quod.lib.umich.edu">[Quod Libet]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">quod.lib.umich.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on umich.edu.</span></span></span>

For Staffordshire folklore, this is a valuable contrast with ghost tales, black dogs or witch legends. The Horn Dance is not mainly a story told about a place; it is an action that repeatedly makes place visible. Every Wakes Monday, the village becomes a stage, the parish becomes the route, and the horns become moving signs of continuity.

## What has changed, and what has stayed recognisable

Living customs survive by changing carefully. The Horn Dance is often imagined as untouched antiquity, but the evidence shows adaptation. Its season appears to have shifted from the Christmas and New Year period described by Plot to the modern September Wakes Monday. Its costumes have also changed: EFDSS notes that dancers once wore ordinary clothes with rosettes, before special costumes were designed in the 1880s by Mrs Lowe, the local vicar’s wife, in a style that later became traditional.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://quod.lib.umich.edu/e/eebo2/A55155.0001.001/1%3A9?rgn=div1&amp;view=fulltext" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: quod.lib.umich.edu">[Quod Libet]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">quod.lib.umich.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on umich.edu.</span></span></span>

The music has changed too. The modern event is accompanied by instruments such as melodeon or accordion, while older references and later collecting history point to fiddlers and changing tune traditions. EFDSS presents the dance as a recognised English folk-dance tradition, but its continued life in Abbots Bromley depends less on a fixed “authentic” soundtrack than on the repeated public performance of the route, characters and horns.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.efdss.org/learning/resources/beginners-guides/37-english-folk-dance/2181-other-dance-traditions-2" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: efdss.org">[English Folk Dance and Song Society]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">efdss.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on efdss.org.</span></span></span>

Some changes are practical rather than symbolic. Visitor numbers require traffic management, parking controls and advice about where and when to see the dancers. The parish council’s modern visitor information makes clear that the event now has to function as both local custom and public attraction.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://abbotsbromley.com/information/horn-dance" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: abbotsbromley.com">[abbotsbromley.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">abbotsbromley.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Horn Dance – Abbots Bromley Parish Council</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Horn Dance – Abbots Bromley Parish Council</span></span></span>

What has stayed recognisable is the basic grammar of the rite: horn-bearers, accompanying figures, music, public dancing, parish movement, church safekeeping and annual return. That continuity is why the dance can change in costume, visitor management or exact musical habit without losing its identity.

## Why the Horn Dance still matters

The Horn Dance matters because it gives Staffordshire a living custom with deep local roots and national recognisability. Many folklore traditions survive only in books, antiquarian collections or retold legends. Abbots Bromley is different: the custom still takes place, still uses extraordinary old objects, and still depends on a specific village and parish. Staffordshire Past Track calls it a link between the present and the past for Abbots Bromley, and that is exactly its public value.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.search.staffspasttrack.org.uk/details.aspx?ExhibitionPage=2&amp;PageIndex=1&amp;ResourceID=24911&amp;SearchType=2&amp;ThemeID=750" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: search.staffspasttrack.org.uk">[search.staffspasttrack.org.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">search.staffspasttrack.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Exhibition Details</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Staffordshire Past Track...</span></span></span>

It also matters because it teaches a useful lesson about folklore. The most interesting traditions are not always the ones with the neatest origin story. The Horn Dance is not less valuable because scholars cannot prove a direct line back to a pagan rite or to 1226. Its value lies in the combination of evidence and mystery: seventeenth-century description, eleventh-century antlers, changing seasonal timing, church storage, village performance and continuing local care.

For visitors, the spectacle is memorable because it looks almost impossible: antlers from another age carried through an English village by dancers who are not acting in a theatre but fulfilling a yearly obligation. For Abbots Bromley, it is a marker of identity. For Staffordshire, it is a county emblem that sits alongside holy wells, moorland legends, witch stories, old stones and haunted landscapes, but with one crucial difference: on Wakes Monday, the folklore walks down the road in daylight.

      Array.prototype.slice.call(menu.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-option]')).forEach(function (button) {

          event.stopPropagation();
          applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-option'), true);
        });
      });
      document.addEventListener('click', function () { closeMenu(section); });
    }
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      button.addEventListener('click', function () {
        applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button'), true);
      });
    });
    applyMarket(section, marketId, false);
  });
