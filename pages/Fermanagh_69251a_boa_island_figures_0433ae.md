## Introduction

The Boa Island stone figures are two weathered human-shaped carvings standing in Caldragh graveyard, Dreenan townland, on Boa Island in Lower [Lough Erne]({{ 'lough-erne/' | relative_url }}), County [Fermanagh]({{ 'where-fermanaghs-water-keeps-the-stories/' | relative_url }}). The larger figure is often called the Boa Island “Janus” figure because it has two faces, but that name can mislead: it does not prove a link with the Roman god Janus, and some recent interpretation prefers the plainer name Dreenan Figure. The smaller carving, usually called the Lustymore Figure or Lustymore Man, was brought from nearby Lustymore Island in the 1930s and placed beside it for safekeeping.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://loughernepilgrimway.com/discover/caldragh/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: loughernepilgrimway.com">[Lough Erne Pilgrim Way]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">loughernepilgrimway.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Lough Erne Pilgrim Way Caldragh</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Lough Erne Pilgrim Way Caldragh</span></span></span>



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/Fermanagh_69251a_boa_island_figures_0433ae-overview.webp" | relative_url }}" alt="Overview image for Boa Island" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

Their meaning remains uncertain, which is exactly why they matter to Fermanagh folklore. They sit at the meeting point of archaeology, Christian burial ground, island place-name tradition, local memory, tourism, poetry and modern offerings. They may be early medieval rather than Iron Age; they may represent mythic, secular or Christian figures rather than named pagan gods; and the familiar “Janus” label is best treated as a visual nickname, not an identification.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.qub.ac.uk/sites/communityarchaeology/PDFFileStore/Filetoupload345610en.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: qub.ac.uk">[qub.ac.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">qub.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Queen&#x27;s University Belfast</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Queen&#x27;s University Belfast</span></span></span>

## Where the figures stand in Fermanagh

Caldragh graveyard lies on the southern side of Boa Island, a long island in the northern part of Lower Lough Erne. Today Boa Island is connected by road bridges, but its setting still feels strongly Erne-shaped: water, islands, old burial places and scattered carved stones form the local landscape in which the figures have become so memorable. The Lough Erne Pilgrim Way gives the Irish grid reference for Caldragh as H0851261978 and identifies the place as a road-accessible graveyard in Dreenan townland.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://loughernepilgrimway.com/discover/caldragh/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: loughernepilgrimway.com">[Lough Erne Pilgrim Way]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">loughernepilgrimway.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Lough Erne Pilgrim Way Caldragh</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Lough Erne Pilgrim Way Caldragh</span></span></span>

The graveyard itself is modest. That contrast is part of the attraction. Visitors do not encounter the figures in a museum case or a reconstructed ritual complex, but among later headstones in a rural cemetery. The early history of Caldragh is obscure: the name is explained as meaning “old graveyard”, no church remains are visible there now, and the 2012 excavation report notes only a tradition that a church may once have stood at the site.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.qub.ac.uk/sites/communityarchaeology/PDFFileStore/Filetoupload345610en.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: qub.ac.uk">[Queen&#x27;s University Belfast]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">qub.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Queen&#x27;s University Belfast</span></span></span>

This is important for interpreting the folklore. The figures are not attached to one surviving, fully told legend in the way a haunted house might be. Their power comes from place, survival and uncertainty. A person standing in Caldragh sees carved bodies whose original setting, date and purpose are still debated, and that uncertainty invites stories.

## The Janus and Lustymore figures at Caldragh

The larger carving is the best-known one. It is usually described as two-sided or back-to-back: two broadly similar human forms share one stone, with faces looking in opposite directions. The “Janus” label comes from that double-facing appearance, but local heritage interpretation now warns that the label is not necessarily correct, because Janus is a Roman god and the Fermanagh figure need not be Roman at all.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://loughernepilgrimway.com/discover/caldragh/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: loughernepilgrimway.com">[Lough Erne Pilgrim Way]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">loughernepilgrimway.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Lough Erne Pilgrim Way Caldragh</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Lough Erne Pilgrim Way Caldragh</span></span></span>

The figure was recorded in 1841 by the painter and antiquarian George Victor Du Noyer, which gives it a firm nineteenth-century documentary presence at Caldragh. Dorothy Lowry-Corry then brought the carvings to wider academic notice in the early 1930s. When Lowry-Corry encountered the larger figure, it was not displayed as it is today: Tuatha’s archaeological summary describes it as partly sunk into the ground and tangled in bushes before later being moved onto a plinth.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.tuatha.ie/boa-island/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: tuatha.ie">[Tuatha]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">tuatha.ie</span><span class="citation-popover-title">boa island</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">boa island</span></span></span>

The smaller Lustymore Figure has a different history. It did not originally stand at Caldragh, but came from the disused Christian graveyard on Lustymore Island, another island in Lower Lough Erne. It was eventually placed beside the Dreenan Figure at Caldragh, after a period in the care of Lady Ernestine Hunt, so the present pairing is partly a modern conservation decision rather than proof that the two figures always belonged together.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.tuatha.ie/boa-island/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: tuatha.ie">[Tuatha]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">tuatha.ie</span><span class="citation-popover-title">boa island</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">boa island</span></span></span>

That point is easy to miss. The two stones now look like a deliberate ancient pair because they stand side by side, but their shared display is not the same as shared origin. For a folklore reader, this is a useful caution: the atmosphere of a site can feel ancient and coherent even when its present arrangement is the result of later rescue, relocation and interpretation.

## What can and cannot be claimed about their origins

The safest answer is that the Boa Island figures are early stone human effigies whose exact date, makers and meaning remain disputed. Older popular accounts often called them pagan idols and placed them in the Iron Age. That interpretation is still widely encountered, helped by the figure’s strange appearance, the double face, and the island’s name. Boa Island is commonly connected with Inis Badhbha, “Badhbh’s Island”, and Badhbh is associated in Irish tradition with a war-goddess and the carrion crow.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.tuatha.ie/boa-island/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: tuatha.ie">[Tuatha]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">tuatha.ie</span><span class="citation-popover-title">boa island</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">boa island</span></span></span>

But a strong modern reading is more cautious. The Lough Erne Pilgrim Way page, drawing on Helen Lanigan Wood’s interpretation, states that the statue has often been wrongly described as a Janus figure and argues that opinion has moved towards an Early Medieval or Early Christian date. It also suggests a possible link with a frightening mythological or folkloric figure associated with Badhbh, while still presenting the early history of the site as mysterious.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://loughernepilgrimway.com/discover/caldragh/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: loughernepilgrimway.com">[Lough Erne Pilgrim Way]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">loughernepilgrimway.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Lough Erne Pilgrim Way Caldragh</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Lough Erne Pilgrim Way Caldragh</span></span></span>

Several facts make overconfidence risky. First, the larger figure is incomplete. A lower carved section with hands was found close to it in 2001 by Richard Warner, formerly of the Ulster Museum; the 2012 excavation report says this showed the sculpture had once been taller, about 1.40 metres high, and that a substantial part was still missing. The report also records that the newly found fragment was moved from its find spot without proper recording.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.qub.ac.uk/sites/communityarchaeology/PDFFileStore/Filetoupload345610en.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: qub.ac.uk">[Queen&#x27;s University Belfast]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">qub.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Queen&#x27;s University Belfast</span></span></span>

Second, the archaeological context is not straightforward. The 2012 work around the figure found many recent coins and small objects, but no pre-twentieth-century finds in that shallow excavation. The authors note that this absence might raise the possibility that the Boa Island figure was not in its original setting, though Du Noyer’s 1841 drawings show it was at Caldragh by then and there is no clear evidence that it came from elsewhere.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.qub.ac.uk/sites/communityarchaeology/PDFFileStore/Filetoupload345610en.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: qub.ac.uk">[Queen&#x27;s University Belfast]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">qub.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Queen&#x27;s University Belfast</span></span></span>

Third, comparison does not equal identification. The larger figure has been compared with other early carvings and with double-faced figures, but similarities in form do not prove the same date, religion or meaning. Tuatha’s summary notes that the figures have often been assumed to be Iron Age and pre-Christian, yet it also raises the possibility that they are closer to early medieval carvings, perhaps connected with Christian sites rather than separate pagan worship.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.tuatha.ie/boa-island/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: tuatha.ie">[Tuatha]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">tuatha.ie</span><span class="citation-popover-title">boa island</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">boa island</span></span></span>

The most honest reading is therefore layered: the figures may preserve older mythic imagery, but their surviving setting is a Christian graveyard landscape; they may evoke Badhbh, but they cannot be securely named as that goddess; they may look “pagan” to modern eyes, but that is not the same as proof of a pre-Christian cult statue.

## Why the “Janus” name is useful but risky

“Janus figure” is a convenient phrase because it tells a visitor what to look for: a stone that faces two ways. It also makes the figure easy to remember. In a graveyard, a two-faced carving naturally suggests thresholds, watching, before-and-after, death-and-life, past-and-future. Those associations are powerful, and they help explain why the figure has become one of Fermanagh’s most haunting images.

The risk is that the nickname smuggles in a false certainty. Janus belongs to Roman religion, and there is no need to imagine a Roman god standing in a Fermanagh graveyard to explain the carving. The Lough Erne Pilgrim Way account explicitly says the figure has often been wrongly described as Janus, while Tuatha likewise treats the Janus idea as one possible comparison rather than a secure identification.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://loughernepilgrimway.com/discover/caldragh/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: loughernepilgrimway.com">[Lough Erne Pilgrim Way]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">loughernepilgrimway.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Lough Erne Pilgrim Way Caldragh</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Lough Erne Pilgrim Way Caldragh</span></span></span>

A better public-facing description is “the two-sided Dreenan Figure, often nicknamed the Janus figure”. That keeps the familiar name without letting it dominate the evidence. It also makes room for local meaning. The figure’s Fermanagh identity is not dependent on Rome; it comes from Caldragh, Boa Island, Lough Erne, antiquarian recording, conservation arguments, visitor offerings and the continuing uncertainty around what the stone was meant to be.

## Modern offerings and living folklore

One of the most striking details from the 2012 excavation is not ancient at all. Archaeologists found 301 coins around the figures and nearby stones, along with objects such as shells, beads, artificial flowers, hair clips, quartz and the stub of a candle. The coins were mostly recent, with recoverable dates from 1959 to 2010 and a heavy concentration in the late twentieth and early twenty-first centuries.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.qub.ac.uk/sites/communityarchaeology/PDFFileStore/Filetoupload345610en.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: qub.ac.uk">[Queen&#x27;s University Belfast]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">qub.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Queen&#x27;s University Belfast</span></span></span>

That does not prove an unbroken ancient cult. In fact, the report says the pattern may suggest that the habit of leaving coins and objects is relatively recent, or that earlier material was cleared away when the figure was set on its concrete plinth. This is a good example of how modern folklore forms around an old object: visitors see a mysterious stone, sense that others have treated it as special, and add their own small token.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.qub.ac.uk/sites/communityarchaeology/PDFFileStore/Filetoupload345610en.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: qub.ac.uk">[Queen&#x27;s University Belfast]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">qub.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Queen&#x27;s University Belfast</span></span></span>

This matters because folklore is not only old stories collected from the fireside. It is also repeated behaviour, atmosphere and shared interpretation. A coin left in the socket at the top of the figure, a shell placed near the stone, or a visitor calling it a pagan god online can all become part of the figure’s modern life. The archaeological evidence says those practices should not be mistaken for proof of ancient ritual continuity. The folklore evidence says they are still meaningful as present-day responses to a place people experience as charged.

## Conservation, weathering and local attachment

The figures are not just symbolic objects; they are vulnerable sandstone carvings exposed in a working graveyard. The 2012 excavation was carried out before a proposed shelter, because the larger figure was deteriorating through weathering. A temporary awning had been erected by the Department in 1994 and remained until 2010, but it was vulnerable to wind and rain and was often removed by visitors taking photographs.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.qub.ac.uk/sites/communityarchaeology/PDFFileStore/Filetoupload345610en.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: qub.ac.uk">[Queen&#x27;s University Belfast]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">qub.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Queen&#x27;s University Belfast</span></span></span>

The same report records an important local tension: the owner and local people wished to keep the figure in situ in the graveyard, even though exposure was damaging it. That tension is familiar in folklore landscapes. Moving a carved stone indoors may protect it physically, but it can also strip away the setting that gives it emotional force. At Caldragh, the sense of encounter depends heavily on the graveyard, the lough island, the ordinary headstones and the feeling that the stone still belongs to the place.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.qub.ac.uk/sites/communityarchaeology/PDFFileStore/Filetoupload345610en.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: qub.ac.uk">[Queen&#x27;s University Belfast]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">qub.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Queen&#x27;s University Belfast</span></span></span>

The conservation question therefore is not simply “museum or outdoors”. It is about how to preserve both the material object and the local meaning. The Boa Island figures have become powerful partly because they are not polished museum pieces. Their worn surfaces, missing sections and awkward survival in the open are part of the story readers remember.

## Why uncertain stones become powerful folklore

The Boa Island figures show how uncertainty can strengthen folklore rather than weaken it. If there were an inscription saying exactly who carved them, when and why, they would still be important archaeological objects. But they might be less haunting. Their present power comes from a series of unanswered questions: pagan or Christian, Iron Age or early medieval, deity or human, local mythic being or misunderstood graveyard sculpture?

Fermanagh is especially suited to this kind of ambiguity. Around Lower Lough Erne, islands carry monastic ruins, burial grounds, carved stones and place-names that blur the line between history and legend. Caldragh does not need an invented ghost story to feel folkloric. The material facts are strange enough: a two-sided human stone at an old graveyard, a second island figure brought to stand beside it, a name linked with a war-goddess, a missing base rediscovered, and modern visitors leaving coins and shells.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://loughernepilgrimway.com/discover/caldragh/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: loughernepilgrimway.com">[loughernepilgrimway.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">loughernepilgrimway.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Lough Erne Pilgrim Way Caldragh</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Lough Erne Pilgrim Way Caldragh</span></span></span>

The most valuable way to retell the Boa Island figures is not to flatten them into “ancient Celtic gods” or dismiss them as mere tourist curiosities. They should be presented as rare, enigmatic carvings embedded in Fermanagh’s lake-and-island memory. Their uncertainty is not a gap to be filled with fantasy, but the very feature that keeps them alive in local imagination.

## How to read the figures today

A fair reading starts with restraint. The figures are old, but their exact age is disputed. They are evocative, but not securely identified. They stand in a Christian graveyard, but may carry older mythic echoes. The name Boa Island may invite association with Badhbh, but that association is interpretive rather than a settled label on the stone.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://loughernepilgrimway.com/discover/caldragh/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: loughernepilgrimway.com">[Lough Erne Pilgrim Way]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">loughernepilgrimway.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Lough Erne Pilgrim Way Caldragh</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Lough Erne Pilgrim Way Caldragh</span></span></span>

For visitors and readers, the best questions are not only “Who is this?” but “Why has this place made people keep asking?” The Dreenan Figure faces two ways; the Lustymore Figure has travelled from another island; the graveyard name points to an old burial place; modern offerings show continuing response; and conservation debates show that local people value the figures in their landscape, not merely as detached artefacts.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.qub.ac.uk/sites/communityarchaeology/PDFFileStore/Filetoupload345610en.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: qub.ac.uk">[Queen&#x27;s University Belfast]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">qub.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Queen&#x27;s University Belfast</span></span></span>

That is why Boa Island belongs at the centre of Fermanagh’s folklore map. The figures do not give up a simple legend, but they gather several kinds of tradition into one small graveyard: island place-name lore, possible mythic imagery, antiquarian discovery, early Christian landscape, modern visitor ritual and the enduring pull of carved stones whose makers have left no explanation.
