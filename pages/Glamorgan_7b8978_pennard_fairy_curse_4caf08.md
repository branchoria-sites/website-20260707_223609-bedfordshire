## Introduction

Pennard Castle is one of [Glamorgan]({{ 'where-glamorgans-legends-still-meet-the/' | relative_url }})’s most memorable fairy sites because the story grows out of something visitors can still see: a ruined medieval castle, a dune landscape, and the wide view over Three Cliffs Bay on Gower. The legend says that the Verry Volk, Gower’s local fairy folk, cursed the castle after its lord violently interrupted their night-time dancing and music. A storm of sand then overwhelmed the fortress, leaving it ruined and half-buried.



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/Glamorgan_7b8978_pennard_fairy_curse_4caf08-overview.webp" | relative_url }}" alt="Overview image for Pennard Fairies" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

The tradition is not good evidence that fairies literally destroyed the castle, but it is excellent folklore. It turns a real historical problem — sand encroachment around Pennard, its church and settlement — into a warning about arrogance, trespass and disrespect for unseen powers. Official heritage records describe Pennard as a medieval castle and church site, beginning as a 12th-century ringwork and later rebuilt in stone; the documented history points to gradual abandonment under pressure from drifting sand rather than one magical catastrophe.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cadwpublic-api.azurewebsites.net/reports/sam/FullReport?id=3757&amp;lang=en" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cadwpublic-api.azurewebsites.net">[cadwpublic-api.azurewebsites.net]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cadwpublic-api.azurewebsites.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Scheduled Monument</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Scheduled Monument</span></span></span>

## The Verry Folk and the cursed castle story

The fairies in the Pennard legend are usually called the Verry Volk or Verry Folk. This name is especially important because it gives the story a distinct Gower identity rather than making it just another generic Welsh fairy tale. In W. Y. Evans-Wentz’s 1911 collection *The Fairy-Faith in Celtic Countries*, the Rev. John David Davis of Llanmadoc and Cheriton told him that in that part of Gower the name commonly used for fairies was not the Welsh “Tylwyth Teg” but “Verry Volk”. Davis described them as little people dressed in scarlet and green, seen dancing on moonlit nights, generally benevolent but dangerous to offend.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://sacred-texts.com/neu/celt/ffcc/ffcc124.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sacred-texts.com">[Internet Sacred Text Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sacred-texts.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on sacred-texts.com.</span></span></span>

That description matters because it shows that Pennard’s legend belongs to a wider Gower pattern. The Verry Volk are not simply decorative winged fairies. They are the kind of beings who can help, mislead, feast, dance, punish insults and defend their own space. Davis also recorded the familiar protective trick of turning a coat inside out to break a pixy spell, placing Gower’s fairy lore within a broader British and Celtic tradition of being led astray by supernatural beings.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://sacred-texts.com/neu/celt/ffcc/ffcc124.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sacred-texts.com">[Internet Sacred Text Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sacred-texts.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on sacred-texts.com.</span></span></span>

In the Pennard story, the castle’s lord is celebrating a wedding feast. Late at night, music is heard from the courtyard. Servants or guards investigate and find the Verry Volk dancing to tiny harps. Instead of leaving them alone, the lord storms out with a sword and threatens or attacks them. The fairies answer with a curse: because he has broken in upon their innocent sport, he shall be left without castle or feast. The wind rises, sand sweeps in, and the castle is ruined. Modern retellings vary in detail, but the central pattern remains stable: a proud ruler violates fairy revelry, and the land itself seems to rise against him.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://faerietrails.blogspot.com/2011/02/gower-faeries-and-wedding-feast.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: faerietrails.blogspot.com">[faerietrails.blogspot.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">faerietrails.blogspot.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">gower faeries and wedding feast</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">gower faeries and wedding feast</span></span></span>

The legend’s force comes from its moral neatness. Pennard Castle was a symbol of lordship and control on a headland above the bay, while the Verry Volk represent an older, less controllable claim on the landscape. The curse says that power has limits: a man may own walls, halls and weapons, but he cannot safely bully the hidden life of Gower.

## What the dunes and ruins show

Pennard Castle stands above Pennard Pill and Three Cliffs Bay on the south coast of Gower, now within Swansea’s modern local-government area but historically part of Glamorgan. Heritage records identify it as the remains of a medieval castle and church, with the first defensive work on the site being a 12th-century ringwork. A stone hall was added in the 13th century, and the curtain wall and gatehouse were rebuilt in stone in the late 13th or early 14th century.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cadwpublic-api.azurewebsites.net/reports/sam/FullReport?id=3757&amp;lang=en" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cadwpublic-api.azurewebsites.net">[cadwpublic-api.azurewebsites.net]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cadwpublic-api.azurewebsites.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Scheduled Monument</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Scheduled Monument</span></span></span>

The real castle history already sounds like the beginning of a legend. The site began as a Norman lordship centre, later gained stone defences, and then became increasingly impractical as sand moved inland. Cadw-linked listing information states that the settlement and church suffered from sand-dune encroachment from the 14th century, with the settlement and church rebuilt further inland. Pennard Community Council also describes the medieval village as lying buried beneath the dunes after abandonment in the 16th century.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://britishlistedbuildings.co.uk/300011539-pennard-castle-pennard" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: britishlistedbuildings.co.uk">[British Listed Buildings]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">britishlistedbuildings.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">British Listed Buildings Pennard Castle</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">British Listed Buildings Pennard Castle</span></span></span>

That is the key point: the fairy curse is not floating free from the evidence. It explains a visible and remembered landscape change. The castle did not need to be destroyed by an army for local people to wonder why a lordly structure ended up ruinous among the sands. A 1650 survey described the fortification as “desolate and ruinous” and surrounded by sand; later accounts note that by the 18th and 19th centuries the ruin had become a favourite subject for artists and antiquarian views.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://castellogy.com/sites/sites-wales/pennard-castle" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: castellogy.com">[Castellogy]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">castellogy.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Pennard Castle</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Pennard Castle</span></span></span>

The physical remains also complicate any simple “destroyed overnight” reading. Pennard’s gatehouse and sections of curtain wall still survive, and the site is protected as a Grade II* listed building and scheduled monument. That points to a long process of decay, repair, collapse and conservation, including 20th-century concern over the masonry and later stabilisation work, rather than a single dramatic medieval disaster.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cadwpublic-api.azurewebsites.net/reports/listedbuilding/FullReport?id=11539" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cadwpublic-api.azurewebsites.net">[cadwpublic-api.azurewebsites.net]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cadwpublic-api.azurewebsites.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Listed Buildings</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Listed Buildings</span></span></span>

Yet folklore does not have to behave like a structural survey. Its job here is different. The dunes supply the evidence of environmental pressure; the tale supplies a memorable cause, a moral and a local voice. The sand becomes not merely a geological fact, but a judgement.

## How old and well-attested is the tradition?

The safest answer is that the Verry Volk are well attested as a Gower fairy tradition by the early 20th century, while the precise Pennard Castle curse is harder to pin to a single early source. Evans-Wentz’s 1911 book preserves valuable testimony from John David Davis, who had studied Gower antiquities and folklore and remembered fairy belief from earlier in the 19th century. His testimony confirms the local name, appearance and behaviour of the Verry Volk, including their moonlit dancing and the danger of offending them.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://sacred-texts.com/neu/celt/ffcc/ffcc124.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sacred-texts.com">[Internet Sacred Text Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sacred-texts.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on sacred-texts.com.</span></span></span>

For the Pennard curse itself, many modern versions circulate through local-history sites, tourism pages, storytelling blogs and Welsh legend projects. These retellings agree on the main ingredients: a castle lord or warrior, a wedding or feast, fairy music and dancing, a violent interruption, and a sandstorm curse. Land of Legends Wales presents the castle as a 12th-century ruin associated with several fables, including the Verry Folk engulfing it in sand after the lord threatened them with swords.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.landoflegends.wales/location/pennard-castle" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: landoflegends.wales">[Land of Legends]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">landoflegends.wales</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on landoflegends.wales.</span></span></span>

That mixture should be read carefully. The story is traditional in the sense that it is rooted in local Gower fairy belief and repeatedly attached to Pennard’s landscape. It is not as securely documented as a dated medieval chronicle or a court record. Some modern versions are clearly polished for visitors, children, podcasts, newsletters or tourism campaigns. That does not make them worthless; it shows how folklore survives by being retold in forms that each generation will actually hear.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://swanseabaynews.com/gower-huw-stephens-brings-the-legend-of-pennard-castles-faeries-to-life-in-gwrs-trackanory-storytime-series/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: swanseabaynews.com">[Swansea Bay News]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">swanseabaynews.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Swansea Bay News GOWER: Huw Stephens brings the legend of Pennard</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Swansea Bay News GOWER: Huw Stephens brings the legend of Pennard</span></span></span>

The strongest evidence, then, is not a single ancient manuscript saying “fairies buried Pennard Castle”. It is the convergence of three things: a distinctive Gower fairy vocabulary, a real dune-buried medieval landscape, and a persistent explanatory legend that makes local sense of the ruins.

## Why Pennard matters within Glamorgan folklore

Pennard Castle gives Glamorgan a fairy legend with an unusually strong sense of place. Many counties have fairy hills, fairy rings or fairy roads, but Pennard combines a named ruin, a dramatic coastline, a known local fairy term and a plausible historical trigger. The story is not just “fairies were here”; it is “this castle fell because its lord offended the fairies”.

That makes it different from Glamorgan traditions centred on saints, wells, ghosts or seasonal customs. Pennard belongs to Gower’s coastal and liminal folklore: cliffs, dunes, buried settlement, night music and the sense that ordinary human control weakens at the edge of land and sea. The castle looks commanding by day, but the story imagines it becoming vulnerable at night, when the Verry Volk reclaim the courtyard with music.

It also reflects Gower’s particular cultural position inside historic Glamorgan. Gower has long had its own strong local identity, shaped by seaways, farming communities, Norman lordship, English and Welsh speech zones, and a coastline that feels distinct from both Swansea town and the industrial valleys. The use of “Verry Volk” rather than the more widely recognised Welsh fairy name helps preserve that local texture.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://sacred-texts.com/neu/celt/ffcc/ffcc124.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sacred-texts.com">[Internet Sacred Text Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sacred-texts.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on sacred-texts.com.</span></span></span>

For readers exploring Glamorgan folklore more widely, Pennard sits naturally beside other place-bound legends: Arthurian associations around Cefn Bryn, haunted coastal houses, saintly wells, winter visiting customs and local ghost traditions. Its special contribution is that it turns environmental memory into fairy justice.

## How Gower retells the legend today

Today, Pennard’s fairy curse is part heritage interpretation, part visitor storytelling and part living local myth. Tourism and local-interest sites regularly present the ruin as a place where landscape and legend meet. Gower Holidays summarises the castle’s Norman and de Braose history, then connects the sandstorm tradition to the Verry Volk and the wedding-feast story. Modern versions often soften or reshape the tale. Some make the lord a greedy or controlling figure; some emphasise the wedding feast; some bring in a daughter or a more explicit environmental message; others keep closer to the older fairy-warning pattern. The changes are worth noticing because they reveal what each audience wants from the story. Antiquarian and folklore versions ask what people once believed; tourism versions ask what makes a place memorable; modern family storytelling asks what moral the tale can carry now.

What remains constant is the landscape. The reader or visitor does not need to believe in fairies to understand why the story stuck. A ruined castle above a shining bay, dunes that once swallowed settlement, and a local fairy people known for dancing in moonlight make a powerful combination. Pennard’s curse endures because it feels fitted to the place.

## The honest answer to the fairy question

So, did fairies bury Pennard Castle in sand? As history, no: the available evidence points to medieval construction, later sand encroachment, abandonment, collapse and conservation. Cadw and listing records describe a real castle and church site affected by dunes from the medieval period onward, not a proven supernatural event.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cadwpublic-api.azurewebsites.net/reports/sam/FullReport?id=3757&amp;lang=en" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cadwpublic-api.azurewebsites.net">[cadwpublic-api.azurewebsites.net]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cadwpublic-api.azurewebsites.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Scheduled Monument</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Scheduled Monument</span></span></span>

As folklore, however, the answer is more interesting. The Verry Volk story explains why the ruin looks the way it does and why a lordly building might fail before a force as ordinary and unstoppable as blown sand. It gives Gower a fairy tale that is not airy or harmless, but rooted in place: offend the hidden people, disrespect the land, and even stone walls may not protect you.

That is why Pennard Castle remains Glamorgan’s great fairy warning. It is not merely a picturesque ruin with a story attached. It is a place where history, dune movement, local fairy belief and modern retelling all point to the same lesson: the landscape remembers what power forgets.
