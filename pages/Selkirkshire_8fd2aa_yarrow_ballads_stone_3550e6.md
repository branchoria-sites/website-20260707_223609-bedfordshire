## Introduction

Yarrow is remembered as one of [Selkirkshire]({{ 'where-selkirkshire-turns-story-into/' | relative_url }})’s most sorrowful valleys because three kinds of evidence meet there: tragic Border ballad, visible stones, and old burial memory. The song usually called “The Dowie Dens of Yarrow” turns the valley into a place of ambush, loyalty and grief; the Yarrow Stone preserves an early medieval Latin memorial to two high-status dead; and nearby Annan Street, the Glebe Stone and Warrior’s Rest tie the same small landscape to cists, cairns and earlier burial ground. The result is not a simple case of “the ballad explains the stone” or “the stone proves the ballad”. It is more interesting than that. Yarrow became a landscape where people repeatedly placed sorrow: in song, in graves, in antiquarian writing, and later in literary pilgrimage. Historic Selkirkshire’s folklore here is not a monster story, but a way of reading grief in the ground.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://sacred-texts.com/neu/eng/child/ch214.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sacred-texts.com">[sacred-texts.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sacred-texts.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on sacred-texts.com.</span></span></span>

## The Dowie Dens of Yarrow in song

“The Dowie Dens of Yarrow”, also known as “The Braes o Yarrow”, is one of the great Scottish Border tragic ballads. In Francis James Child’s numbering it is Child Ballad 214, while the Vaughan Williams Memorial Library indexes versions under Roud 13 and Child 214. The basic pattern is stark: a woman’s lover or husband goes to Yarrow despite warning, faces a group of armed men, fights bravely, and is finally killed; the woman then finds him dead or learns of his death and is overwhelmed by grief.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archives.vwml.org/songs/c70ce4eb-9f3a-409b-a06b-36988cd6a342" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.vwml.org">[archives.vwml.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.vwml.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Dowie Dens o Yarrow</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Dowie Dens o Yarrow</span></span></span>

What gives the ballad its power is not simply that someone dies. It is the repeated association of Yarrow with foreboding. In Child’s version 214A, the woman dreams of pulling heather on the braes of Yarrow and of her lover coming home headless; the doomed man then meets “nine well-wight men” in the den, kills or wounds many of them, and is struck down from behind. When the woman goes down into the den, “sorrow went her before”, and she finds her true love lying slain. Child’s collected text therefore makes Yarrow not just a setting, but a place where dream, warning, violence and mourning fold into one another.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://sacred-texts.com/neu/eng/child/ch214.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sacred-texts.com">[Internet Sacred Text Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sacred-texts.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on sacred-texts.com.</span></span></span>

The ballad also belongs to Selkirkshire because Yarrow is not a vague romantic name. It is the valley of the Yarrow Water, running through the old Border country towards Selkirk. Later retellings and commentaries often connect the song with a supposed real fight among Scotts, and Walter Scott presented it as a Border tradition in the orbit of Ettrick Forest. Scott’s own educational site notes that he first published the ballad in the 1803 edition of *Minstrelsy of the Scottish Border*, where the song was framed as part of the region’s remembered violence and family feud culture.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://walterscott.eu/education/ballads-of-love-and-loss/the-dowie-dens-of-yarrow/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: walterscott.eu">[Walter Scott]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">walterscott.eu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">the dowie dens of yarrow</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">the dowie dens of yarrow</span></span></span>

That does not mean the ballad can be treated as a court record. Its details vary widely across versions: the number and identity of the attackers shift; the slain man may be a husband, lover, lord, ploughman or outsider; the woman’s father, brothers or rival suitors may be implicated in different ways. The important folklore point is that the song preserves emotional truth and localised memory more firmly than a single verifiable incident. Yarrow becomes the named valley where betrayal is imagined as something the ground itself remembers.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://sacred-texts.com/neu/eng/child/ch214.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sacred-texts.com">[Internet Sacred Text Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sacred-texts.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on sacred-texts.com.</span></span></span>

## The Yarrow Stone and Annan Street remains

The most important archaeological anchor for Yarrow’s remembered sorrow is the Yarrow Stone, also known as the Liberalis Stone, near Whitefield in the parish of Yarrow. Historic Environment Scotland records it as a scheduled monument, classed as an inscribed stone, with the designation added in 1962. Trove, drawing on the National Record of the Historic Environment, places it in the former county of Selkirkshire and links it to nearby Annan Street, the Glebe Stone and Warrior’s Rest.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://portal.historicenvironment.scot/apex/f?p=1505%3A300%3A%3A%3A%3A%3AVIEWTYPE%2CVIEWREF%3Adesignation%2CSM1727" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: portal.historicenvironment.scot">[Historic Environment Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">portal.historicenvironment.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on historicenvironment.scot.</span></span></span>

The stone matters because it is not just another unmarked standing stone. Trove records that it was turned up by the plough in the early nineteenth century when moorland known as Annan Street was first brought under cultivation. It was found lying just below the surface with human bones underneath, at a time when about twenty “large cairns” were said to have stood on the moor. After being taken to Bowhill for examination, it was returned to Annan Street and re-erected at or near the place of discovery.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.trove.scot/place/53078" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: trove.scot">[Trove Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">trove.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Scotland Whitefield, &#x27;yarrow Stone&#x27; &#124; Place &#124; trove.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">landWhitefield, &#x27;yarrow Stone&#x27; &#124; Place &#124; trove.scot...</span></span></span>

Its inscription is badly worn and has troubled specialists, but the usual reading treats it as an early medieval Latin memorial to two high-status men. Tourist and heritage summaries commonly render the inscription as a memorial to the princes Nudoss and Dumnogenus, the sons of Liberalis, while Scotland Starts Here describes the stone as commemorating two princes of the British kingdom of Strathclyde. The safest way to put this for a general reader is that the stone preserves an early Christian or post-Roman British memorial tradition in a valley now usually thought of through later Scottish Border ballad.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.undiscoveredscotland.co.uk/selkirk/yarrowstone/index.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: undiscoveredscotland.co.uk">[undiscoveredscotland.co.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">undiscoveredscotland.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on undiscoveredscotland.co.uk.</span></span></span>

Annan Street widens the picture from one carved stone to a burial landscape. Trove’s record for Annan Street notes a cist discovered around December 1882 at the eastern end of Warrior’s Rest or Annan Field, and explicitly points readers to the wider group of Yarrow records: cists, a legendary burial site and the inscribed Yarrow Stone. This is why Yarrow’s stones are so resonant in folklore terms. They do not “prove” the ballad, but they do confirm that the valley has long been treated as ground where the dead mattered.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.trove.scot/place/53056" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: trove.scot">[Trove Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">trove.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on trove.scot.</span></span></span>

## The nearby stones: Glebe Stone and Warrior’s Rest

The Yarrow Stone sits within a small cluster of monuments rather than standing alone. Trove’s Yarrow Stone record says it should be associated with two uninscribed stones nearby: the Glebe Stone, 530 yards to the east-north-east, and the stone at Warrior’s Rest, 800 yards to the north-east. Historic Environment Scotland also separately designates the Glebe Stone and Warrior’s Rest as scheduled monuments in the same local group.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.trove.scot/place/53078" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: trove.scot">[Trove Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">trove.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Scotland Whitefield, &#x27;yarrow Stone&#x27; &#124; Place &#124; trove.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">landWhitefield, &#x27;yarrow Stone&#x27; &#124; Place &#124; trove.scot...</span></span></span>

The Glebe Stone is recorded in the parish of Yarrow, in the former county of Selkirkshire, at OS grid reference NT 35260 27600. Its modern records include material from Scotland’s Rock Art Project, which is a reminder that these stones belong not only to later legend but also to the wider prehistoric and early historic study of marked and unmarked stone monuments. For a folklore reader, the crucial point is not to overstate its meaning. A standing stone in a field is tempting to turn into a tidy story, but the official record is more cautious: it gives location, classification and archive traces, not a neat legend.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.trove.scot/place/53061" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: trove.scot">[Trove Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">trove.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Scotland The Glebe Stone, Whitefield &#124; Place &#124; trove.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Scotland The Glebe Stone, Whitefield &#124; Place &#124; trove.scot</span></span></span>

Warrior’s Rest carries the more evocative name, and the record explains why that name has stuck. Trove notes that the wider site likely saw successive use as Bronze Age and Early Christian cemetery ground. The record also says that graves aligned east-west and lacking grave-goods are characteristic of Christian rather than pagan burials, while earlier objects in later graves may reflect disturbance of older interments. That is a compact archaeological explanation for the valley’s layered atmosphere: one burial horizon may have overwritten, disturbed or reinterpreted another.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.trove.scot/place/53054" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: trove.scot">[Trove Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">trove.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Scotland Warrior&#x27;s Rest &#124; Place &#124; trove.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Scotland Warrior&#x27;s Rest &#124; Place &#124; trove.scot</span></span></span>

This layering is exactly where folklore often grows. A field with a stone, bones, old names and uncertain memory invites explanation. “Warrior’s Rest” sounds like a story even before one is told, and the name turns archaeological ambiguity into a human image: someone fought, someone fell, someone was laid there. Responsible retelling should keep the uncertainty visible, but it need not strip the place of its force. Yarrow’s stones are compelling precisely because they sit between evidence and imagination.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.trove.scot/place/53054" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: trove.scot">[Trove Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">trove.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Scotland Warrior&#x27;s Rest &#124; Place &#124; trove.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Scotland Warrior&#x27;s Rest &#124; Place &#124; trove.scot</span></span></span>

## How archaeology and ballad memory overlap

The strongest reading of Yarrow is not that the Dowie Dens ballad remembers the Yarrow Stone’s princes, or that the stone marks the ballad lover’s grave. The chronology makes such a direct link very unlikely. The Yarrow Stone belongs to an early medieval memorial world, probably centuries before the later Border ballad tradition was collected in print; the ballad as preserved by Scott and Child belongs to oral, manuscript and printed transmission shaped by eighteenth- and nineteenth-century collectors.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.trove.scot/place/53078" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: trove.scot">[trove.scot]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">trove.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Scotland Whitefield, &#x27;yarrow Stone&#x27; &#124; Place &#124; trove.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">landWhitefield, &#x27;yarrow Stone&#x27; &#124; Place &#124; trove.scot...</span></span></span>

The overlap is instead thematic and local. Both the ballad and the stones treat Yarrow as a place where death is not anonymous. The ballad names the valley again and again as the place of ambush, warning and lament. The Yarrow Stone’s Latin inscription tries to preserve status, kinship and memory against time. Annan Street’s cists and cairns suggest a district where burial was visible, disturbed, recorded and reinterpreted. These are different kinds of evidence, but they point in the same emotional direction: Yarrow is a valley where the dead are made memorable.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://sacred-texts.com/neu/eng/child/ch214.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sacred-texts.com">[sacred-texts.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sacred-texts.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on sacred-texts.com.</span></span></span>

This matters for Selkirkshire’s county identity because the area’s folklore is often literary without being merely literary. Scott’s *Minstrelsy* helped fix the ballad in print, but it did not create the valley’s older monuments or the local habit of attaching story to place. Later readers, singers and travellers inherited a Yarrow already thick with associations: Border feud, women’s lament, ancient burial, early Christian inscription, and the melancholy beauty of the Southern Uplands.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://mainlynorfolk.info/folk/songs/thedowiedensofyarrow.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: mainlynorfolk.info">[Mainly Norfolk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">mainlynorfolk.info</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on mainlynorfolk.info.</span></span></span>

William Wordsworth’s Yarrow poems show how quickly this reputation became part of literary tourism. “Yarrow Unvisited” imagines the river before seeing it, and the University of Toronto’s Representative Poetry Online notes that Wordsworth had in mind earlier Yarrow ballads including *The Braes of Yarrow* and *Willie’s Drowned in Yarrow*. Poetry Foundation’s text of “Yarrow Unvisited” even turns the decision not to visit into a meditation on whether an imagined Yarrow might be more powerful than the real valley.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://rpo.library.utoronto.ca/content/yarrow-unvisited" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: rpo.library.utoronto.ca">[Representative Poetry Online]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">rpo.library.utoronto.ca</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Representative Poetry Online Yarrow Unvisited &#124; RPO</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Representative Poetry Online Yarrow Unvisited &#124; RPO</span></span></span>

## Why Yarrow still feels sorrowful today

Yarrow’s modern appeal comes from the fact that it offers no single tidy legend. A visitor can stand near the Yarrow Stone and see a protected monument, not a theatrical ruin. They can follow the ballad and encounter not a precise battlefield, but a valley repeatedly named as the scene of grief. They can read the archaeological records and find careful uncertainty rather than romantic certainty. That combination is unusually strong: the place is documented enough to be grounded, but open enough to remain haunting.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://portal.historicenvironment.scot/apex/f?p=1505%3A300%3A%3A%3A%3A%3AVIEWTYPE%2CVIEWREF%3Adesignation%2CSM1727" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: portal.historicenvironment.scot">[historicenvironment.scot]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">portal.historicenvironment.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on historicenvironment.scot.</span></span></span>

For folklore, this is more valuable than a sensational ghost tale. Yarrow shows how landscape memory works when song, stone and story keep reinforcing one another. The ballad gives the valley a voice of lament. The Yarrow Stone gives it a visible memorial to named dead. Annan Street, the Glebe Stone and Warrior’s Rest give it a deeper burial setting. Later poets and local heritage retellings keep the association alive, but the core feeling is older and simpler: Yarrow is remembered as a place where love, violence and burial meet.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.trove.scot/place/53054" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: trove.scot">[trove.scot]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">trove.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Scotland Warrior&#x27;s Rest &#124; Place &#124; trove.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Scotland Warrior&#x27;s Rest &#124; Place &#124; trove.scot</span></span></span>

That is why the valley belongs so naturally inside a Selkirkshire folklore map. It links the county’s Border ballad tradition with its ancient stones and its habit of making place-names carry emotion. Carterhaugh may be Selkirkshire’s fairy landscape, but Yarrow is its sorrowful one: not because every story attached to it can be proved, but because the valley has become one of the clearest places in the Borders where remembered grief has a tune, a stone and a road through the hills.
