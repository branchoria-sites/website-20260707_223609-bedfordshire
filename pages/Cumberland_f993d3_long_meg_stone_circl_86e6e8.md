## Introduction

Long Meg and Her Daughters is [Cumberland]({{ 'where-cumberland-turns-landscape-into/' | relative_url }})’s most memorable meeting point between archaeology and folklore: a vast stone circle near Little Salkeld in the Eden Valley, watched over by a taller red sandstone outlier called Long Meg. Archaeologists treat it as a major late Neolithic or Bronze Age monument, with associated cropmark remains, rock art and a long ritual landscape around the River Eden. Local tradition turns the same stones into a coven, a forbidden dance, a punishment, and a counting puzzle nobody should solve. Historic England records 69 large stones in a flattened oval, with Long Meg standing to the south-west; Cumbria County History Trust preserves the famous tale that Michael Scott turned witches to stone and that an exact count may break the spell.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://historicengland.org.uk/listing/the-list/list-entry/1007866" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: historicengland.org.uk">[Historic England]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">historicengland.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Historic EnglandLong Meg and Her Daughters stone circle, associated...The cursus and prehistoric enclosure have been identified from cro...</span></span></span>



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/Cumberland_f993d3_long_meg_stone_circl_86e6e8-overview.webp" | relative_url }}" alt="Overview image for Long Meg" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

That double life is why Long Meg matters so much within Cumberland folklore. It is not simply an ancient monument with a story attached. It is a landscape where people have repeatedly tried to explain an older mystery in the language of their own time: sin, witchcraft, magic, antiquarian curiosity, Romantic awe, heritage tourism and modern local identity.

## The circle, Long Meg and the Eden Valley setting

Long Meg and Her Daughters stands north-east of Penrith, near Little Salkeld, on a wide terrace above the River Eden. In historic-county terms this is Cumberland’s Eden Valley, though modern visitor material usually places it in Cumbria. The older county frame still matters because the legend belongs to a Cumberland landscape of river terraces, Pennine edges, old routes and prehistoric remains rather than to a modern council boundary.

The monument itself is impressive even before the folklore begins. Historic England describes an oval stone circle of 69 large stones, some upright and some fallen, arranged roughly 109 metres east-west by 94 metres north-south. The circle is flattened on its northern side, and associated remains include a cursus or linear earthwork to the west and a prehistoric enclosure to the north, identified from aerial cropmarks rather than obvious earthworks on the ground.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://historicengland.org.uk/listing/the-list/list-entry/1007866" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: historicengland.org.uk">[Historic England]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">historicengland.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Historic EnglandLong Meg and Her Daughters stone circle, associated...The cursus and prehistoric enclosure have been identified from cro...</span></span></span>

Long Meg is the dramatic outlier. She stands to the south-west of the ring, apart from the “daughters”, and is made of red sandstone rather than the granitic glacial erratics used for the main circle. The visitor-friendly description of the site often stresses the contrast: the tall individual stone outside the ring, the great oval of smaller stones, and the sense that the outlier is facing, guarding or commanding the group. Historic England’s scheduling record also notes prehistoric rock art on Long Meg, including cup-and-ring markings and other carved designs whose exact meaning remains unknown.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://historicengland.org.uk/listing/the-list/list-entry/1007866" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: historicengland.org.uk">[Historic England]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">historicengland.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Historic EnglandLong Meg and Her Daughters stone circle, associated...The cursus and prehistoric enclosure have been identified from cro...</span></span></span>

The surrounding landscape strengthens the sense that this was not an isolated monument. Heritage Gateway places the circle within a wider prehistoric setting beside the Eden, with the flattened northern side apparently respecting an earlier enclosure ditch. Other sources also point to nearby decorated and ceremonial sites, including Little Meg and other Eden Valley monuments, making this part of Cumberland one of the county’s richest zones for prehistoric stone and rock-art traditions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.heritagegateway.org.uk/Gateway/Results_Single.aspx?resourceID=19191&amp;uid=12199" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: heritagegateway.org.uk">[Heritage Gateway]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">heritagegateway.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Results Single.aspx</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Results Single.aspx</span></span></span>

For a folklore reader, the key point is simple: the legend grew around a monument that was already strange, old and visually difficult to master. The outlier looks person-like. The circle is large enough to resist a casual glance. Stones are fallen, missing, irregular and unevenly spaced. The landscape does not explain itself. That is exactly the kind of place where “count them if you dare” becomes more than a parlour challenge.

## Witches, Michael Scott and the counting taboo

The best-known Long Meg story says that the stones were once women: witches, dancers, sisters, daughters or a coven. In one common version, they were holding a sabbat when the magician Michael Scott interrupted them and turned them into stone. Cumbria County History Trust records the sharper local form: anyone who achieves an exact count may reactivate the coven and suffer harm, while one variant says the count must come out the same clockwise and anticlockwise.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cumbriacountyhistory.org.uk/long-meg-and-her-daughters-background-little-salkeld" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cumbriacountyhistory.org.uk">[Cumbria County History Trust]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cumbriacountyhistory.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cumbria County History TrustLong Meg and Her Daughters (Background: Little Salkeld)The story is that they were a coven of witches whose s...</span></span></span>

The “uncountable stones” motif is not unique to Cumberland. Folklorists have identified it at several British megalithic monuments, including Stonehenge, the Hurlers in Cornwall and Long Meg. The basic pattern is that a person cannot successfully count the stones, or that matching counts are dangerous, magical or impossible. Long Meg’s local distinctiveness lies in how the motif locks onto the personality of the site: a named female outlier, her “daughters”, and a witchcraft story that makes a failed count feel like a failed attempt to control a living spell.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Countless_stones" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Countless stones</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Countless stones</span></span></span>

There is also a moralised version. Instead of witches in the later sensational sense, the women are sometimes young dancers or sinful women punished for profaning a holy place, dancing on the Sabbath, or encouraging unlawful love. British Folklore’s summary of the antiquarian tradition notes that Celia Fiennes recorded a version involving “Great Mag and her Sisters”, while later Cumberland historian William Hutchinson gave the site a stronger holy-place-and-witches framing.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://britishfolklore.com/long-meg-and-her-daughters/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: britishfolklore.com">[britishfolklore.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">britishfolklore.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Long Meg and her Daughters</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Long Meg and her Daughters</span></span></span>

Michael Scott, or Michael Scot, adds a border-country flavour. Historically, Michael Scot was a medieval scholar and translator who became legendary as a magician; in later folklore he appears as a wizard capable of astonishing feats. Linking him to Long Meg gives the Cumberland stones a wider northern supernatural network, connecting them to Scottish-border magical legend rather than leaving them as a purely local curiosity. The Michael Scott connection is not as early as the name Long Meg itself, and secondary folklore summaries trace it especially through later antiquarian or nineteenth-century retellings, but it has become one of the most memorable forms of the tale.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_Scot" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Michael Scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Michael Scot</span></span></span>

The counting taboo works because it turns ordinary visitor behaviour into ritual behaviour. Most people who arrive at a stone circle want to know how many stones there are. Long Meg makes that question unstable. Historic England can record 69 stones; Cumbria County History Trust can say the present established count is 68 daughters without disaster; antiquarian and popular accounts preserve numbers ranging far more widely. Folklore lives in the gap between those positions. The official count answers the archaeological question. The legend keeps alive the older feeling that some places should not be reduced too easily to a number.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://historicengland.org.uk/listing/the-list/list-entry/1007866" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: historicengland.org.uk">[historicengland.org.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">historicengland.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Historic EnglandLong Meg and Her Daughters stone circle, associated...The cursus and prehistoric enclosure have been identified from cro...</span></span></span>

## Why did people say the stones could not be counted?

The practical answer is that Long Meg is genuinely awkward to count. Stones have fallen, shifted, broken or disappeared; some are large and obvious, others less so; and older descriptions may have counted different things. A person walking the ring may include or exclude fallen stones, fragments, outliers and doubtful stones. That alone can create inconsistent totals without needing a supernatural explanation.

The deeper folklore answer is that uncountability protects wonder. Many British stone-circle legends treat exact counting as a kind of trespass: to count is to master, measure or demystify the place. The punishment varies from bad luck to the breaking of an enchantment, but the effect is the same. The story tells visitors that the circle is not just a heap of archaeological material. It is a company, a spell, a mystery and a warning.

At Long Meg, the taboo is especially vivid because the stones already have social identities. They are not “stone 1” to “stone 69”; they are Meg and her daughters. That family language makes the circle feel animated. It also explains why the outlier matters so much. Long Meg is not merely an extra stone outside the ring. In the story-world, she is the named figure around whom the rest gather.

The older records show how flexible the tradition has been. Reginald Bainbridge, an Appleby schoolmaster, informed William Camden around 1600 of monuments commonly called “Meg with her daughters”, and Cumbria County History Trust notes that counts recorded in tradition range from 57 to 200. That wild variation is not a failure of folklore; it is part of the point. The site’s legendary identity was formed through repeated attempts to describe something too old, too large and too irregular to feel settled.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cumbriacountyhistory.org.uk/long-meg-and-her-daughters-background-little-salkeld" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cumbriacountyhistory.org.uk">[Cumbria County History Trust]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cumbriacountyhistory.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cumbria County History TrustLong Meg and Her Daughters (Background: Little Salkeld)The story is that they were a coven of witches whose s...</span></span></span>

## How old is the tradition?

The monument is prehistoric, but the surviving folklore is much later. That distinction matters. The builders of Long Meg did not leave a written explanation, and no responsible account can say that the witch story preserves a direct Neolithic belief. Archaeology can place the monument within late Neolithic and Bronze Age ceremonial landscapes; folklore can show how later communities imagined and reimagined that landscape. Those are different kinds of truth.

The name itself is early in the written record. Camden’s *Britannia*, drawing on information from Reginald Bainbridge around 1600, already uses the Meg-and-daughters naming pattern. That means the female personification of the site is at least early modern in attested form, and may be older orally.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cumbriacountyhistory.org.uk/long-meg-and-her-daughters-background-little-salkeld" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cumbriacountyhistory.org.uk">[Cumbria County History Trust]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cumbriacountyhistory.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cumbria County History TrustLong Meg and Her Daughters (Background: Little Salkeld)The story is that they were a coven of witches whose s...</span></span></span>

The narrative details develop over time. Celia Fiennes, the late seventeenth- and early eighteenth-century traveller, is associated with a version in which “Great Mag and her Sisters” were turned to stone for sexual or moral transgression. William Hutchinson’s eighteenth-century Cumberland history helps preserve a version in which the women are witches profaning a holy place. Later retellings bring in Michael Scott, the named wizard, and modern heritage writing often combines these elements into the familiar tale of witches petrified by magic.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://britishfolklore.com/long-meg-and-her-daughters/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: britishfolklore.com">[britishfolklore.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">britishfolklore.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Long Meg and her Daughters</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Long Meg and her Daughters</span></span></span>

This layered history is useful because it prevents two common mistakes. One mistake is to treat the witch legend as a prehistoric survival. The other is to dismiss it as merely fake because it is not prehistoric. Long Meg’s folklore is historically valuable precisely because it shows how Cumberland people, travellers and antiquaries made sense of a prehistoric monument after its original meanings had long been lost.

## How archaeology and folklore tell different truths

Archaeology asks what the site is, when it was built, how it relates to other monuments and what physical evidence survives. Folklore asks what later people believed, feared, repeated and enjoyed about it. Long Meg needs both approaches because each answers a different reader question.

Archaeologically, the circle belongs to a long-lived prehistoric landscape. Historic England’s record places the scheduled monument within late Neolithic and Bronze Age traditions and identifies associated features such as the cursus and prehistoric enclosure. The rock art on Long Meg is especially important because cup-and-ring marks and related motifs are among the most evocative but least fully understood forms of prehistoric visual culture in Britain. Historic England is careful here: the carvings may be sacred or religious symbols, but their exact meaning remains unknown.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://historicengland.org.uk/listing/the-list/list-entry/1007866" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: historicengland.org.uk">[Historic England]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">historicengland.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Historic EnglandLong Meg and Her Daughters stone circle, associated...The cursus and prehistoric enclosure have been identified from cro...</span></span></span>

Folklore fills that silence with people. It turns stones into bodies, the circle into a gathering, and the outlier into a named woman. This does not tell us what Neolithic Cumberland believed, but it tells us a great deal about later Cumberland imagination. In Christianised and early modern retellings, unexplained ancient stones often became petrified sinners, Sabbath-breakers or witches. The legend therefore records a later moral and supernatural reading of the landscape, not the monument’s original purpose.

The two truths can sit together without cancelling each other. An official count does not destroy the story. A witch legend does not replace excavation, aerial photography or scheduling records. Instead, Long Meg becomes a lesson in how old places accumulate meanings. The prehistoric builders created a monument. Later communities created a narrative powerful enough to make that monument memorable to people who had no access to its original language.

## Long Meg among Cumberland’s other unquiet stones

Long Meg is the flagship, but it also belongs to a wider Cumberland and Cumbrian pattern of prehistoric stones becoming story-places. The Eden Valley and nearby Lake District include other circles, henges, cairns and decorated stones. Some are better known archaeologically than folklorically; others survive in visitor memory because they have acquired names, legends or striking settings.

Castlerigg, near Keswick in historic Cumberland, offers a useful comparison. It is another major stone circle where landscape setting is central to the experience: the mountains frame the stones, and the place invites both archaeological interpretation and imaginative response. Long Meg, by contrast, is more strongly personified. Its folklore has a named female figure, a group of daughters and a taboo about counting. That gives it a story-shape many stone circles lack.

Little Meg, nearby, is also important because it reminds us that Long Meg is not an isolated marvel but part of a local cluster. Heritage summaries link the Long Meg complex with Little Meg, Glassonby and other decorated Eden Valley monuments. For readers following Cumberland folklore, this matters because the county’s prehistoric sites are not just scattered curiosities. They form a mythic landscape in which river, terrace, route, stone and story reinforce one another.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.heritagegateway.org.uk/Gateway/Results_Single.aspx?resourceID=19191&amp;uid=12199" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: heritagegateway.org.uk">[Heritage Gateway]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">heritagegateway.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Results Single.aspx</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Results Single.aspx</span></span></span>

The “uncountable” idea also links Long Meg outward to other British stone traditions. The same motif appears at famous sites beyond Cumberland, but Long Meg’s version remains locally distinctive because it is attached to a female figure and a witchcraft narrative. In that sense, the site works well as an internal bridge to other project themes: witches and cunning folk, petrified dancers, fairy or spirit-haunted landscapes, and the uneasy way Christian moral stories were sometimes laid over older sacred places.

## What visitors and retellings keep alive today

Modern visitors usually meet Long Meg through three overlapping frames: prehistoric monument, local legend and scenic Eden Valley stop. Official and heritage sources emphasise its scale, scheduling and archaeology. Tourism sources emphasise access, atmosphere and the famous story that the stones are petrified people who may be released if counted correctly. The Lake District visitor listing, for example, describes Long Meg as the tallest of 69 stones and repeats the legend that correctly counting the petrified people could free them from their spell.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.visitlakedistrict.com/things-to-do/long-meg-and-her-daughters-p1213161" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: visitlakedistrict.com">[Visit Lake District]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">visitlakedistrict.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Visit Lake District Long Meg and her Daughters</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Visit Lake District Long Meg and her Daughters</span></span></span>

The story also travels through modern culture. Penrith and Eden Museum’s schools material notes that Long Meg has inspired artists and writers, including William Wordsworth, Jacob Thompson and Lorna Graves. Wordsworth’s poem “The Monument, Commonly Called Long Meg and Her Daughters” belongs to the Romantic afterlife of the stones: not folklore in the oral-tradition sense, but part of the way the site became a literary object of awe.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.eden.gov.uk/leisure-culture-and-events/penrith-and-eden-museum/museums-school-workshops/landmarks-of-eden-schools-project/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: eden.gov.uk">[eden.gov.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">eden.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">landmarks of eden schools project</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">landmarks of eden schools project</span></span></span>

That modern afterlife can blur the evidence. Some retellings confidently merge separate strands: Meg as a witch, Meg of Meldon, Michael Scott, Sabbath dancing, bleeding stone, whispering stone and the uncountable curse. A careful Cumberland folklore page should keep those strands visible without flattening them into one “true” legend. The pleasure of Long Meg is that it has never had just one explanation.

The most durable modern version is still wonderfully simple. There is a great circle of stones in a Cumberland field. One stone stands apart, taller and redder than the rest. People call her Long Meg, and the others her daughters. They were once women, says the tale, until magic or judgement fixed them in place. Count them if you like, but do not be surprised if the number changes, the story changes, or the stones seem to have kept something back.

## Why Long Meg still defines Cumberland’s mythic landscape

Long Meg endures because it gives Cumberland folklore a perfect emblem: ancient, local, eerie, half-explained and firmly attached to a real place. It is not a floating legend that could happen anywhere. It belongs to a particular terrace above the Eden, near Little Salkeld, in a landscape where prehistoric monuments and later story-making remain visible together.

It also captures a recurring Cumberland pattern. The county’s folklore often lives at thresholds: border lines, fell roads, old religious sites, haunted houses, coastal edges, Roman remains and prehistoric stones. Long Meg is a threshold between measured history and felt mystery. One can map it, schedule it, count it and photograph it; yet the old question still lingers because the folklore has trained generations of visitors to doubt the neatness of a final number.

The best way to understand Long Meg is therefore not to ask whether archaeology or folklore is “right”. Archaeology tells us that this is a major prehistoric monument with associated features and significant carved stone. Folklore tells us that later Cumberland communities saw in it a company of women, a magical punishment and a warning against overconfident counting. Together, they make Long Meg one of the clearest examples of how Cumberland turns ancient stone into living story.
