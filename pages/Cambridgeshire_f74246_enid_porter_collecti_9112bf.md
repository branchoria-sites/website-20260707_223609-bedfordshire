## Introduction

Enid Porter is the reason much of [Cambridgeshire]({{ 'cambridgeshire/' | relative_url }})’s everyday folklore can still be read, studied and retold. As curator of the Cambridge & County Folk Museum, now the Museum of Cambridge, from 1947 to 1976, she collected the customs, beliefs, songs, stories, charms and memories of ordinary people at a moment when older rural and fenland ways of life were rapidly changing. Her work matters because it gives Cambridgeshire folklore a rare evidence base: not just famous legends, but the spoken, domestic and seasonal traditions that lived in kitchens, farmyards, pubs, village lanes, railway journeys and museum conversations.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://capturingcambridge.org/online-exhibitions-of-prior-years/herstory/enidporter/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: capturingcambridge.org">[Capturing Cambridge]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">capturingcambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on capturingcambridge.org.</span></span></span>



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/Cambridgeshire_f74246_enid_porter_collecti_9112bf-overview.webp" | relative_url }}" alt="Overview image for Enid Porter" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

Porter’s best-known book, *Cambridgeshire Customs and Folklore*, first published in 1969 and later reissued by Routledge, shows the breadth of what she preserved. Its subjects range from courtship, birth, death and burial to weather lore, plant beliefs, healing customs, calendar rituals, witchcraft, ghosts, local legends, games, university customs, civic customs, charity traditions and food and drink. That range is the key to Porter’s importance: she treated folklore not as a handful of spooky tales, but as the texture of daily Cambridgeshire life.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.routledge.com/Cambridgeshire-Customs-and-Folklore-Pbdirect/Porter/p/book/9781138845435" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: routledge.com">[Routledge]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">routledge.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Cambridgeshire Customs and Folklore Pbdirect</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cambridgeshire Customs and Folklore Pbdirect</span></span></span>

## The Folk Museum Became a Listening Post

Porter’s collecting work was inseparable from the small, informal museum she ran. The Museum of Cambridge describes itself as a social-history museum devoted to “the extraordinary stories of everyday people from Cambridge and Cambridgeshire”, housed in the former White Horse Inn on Castle Street. Its collections focus on everyday life, customs and traditions, and the museum states that Porter’s work still characterises the collection today.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.museumofcambridge.org.uk/about/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: museumofcambridge.org.uk">[The Museum of Cambridge]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">museumofcambridge.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Museum of Cambridge About – The Museum of Cambridge</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Museum of Cambridge About – The Museum of Cambridge</span></span></span>

That setting shaped the folklore she gathered. Porter was not only a curator hidden behind catalogues. She took entrance money, arranged exhibits, cleaned displays, organised school visits and gave talks, while also listening to visitors who came in to reminisce. The Museum of Cambridge’s account of her career says she regarded this flow of talk as one of the advantages of a small informal museum, because visitors brought memories of Cambridge and Cambridgeshire with them.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.museumofcambridge.org.uk/projects/tracing-traditions/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: museumofcambridge.org.uk">[The Museum of Cambridge]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">museumofcambridge.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Museum of Cambridge Tracing Traditions – The Museum of Cambridge</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Museum of Cambridge Tracing Traditions – The Museum of Cambridge</span></span></span>

This helps explain why her collections feel different from a purely antiquarian survey. A grand county history might preserve charters, buildings and notable families. Porter preserved what people remembered: the charm used against illness, the story attached to an object, the custom observed at a seasonal feast, the ghost remembered by a Women’s Institute group, or the local saying that had never seemed important enough to write down. Her museum was not just a storehouse of things. It became a listening post for vanishing memory.

The approach also fitted the museum’s founding purpose. The Museum of Cambridge traces its origins to the 1930s, when local organisers wanted a “Folk” Museum that would preserve common objects and reflect everyday life in the town and county. Porter inherited that mission and deepened it, linking objects to the customs, beliefs and spoken traditions that gave them meaning.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.museumofcambridge.org.uk/about/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: museumofcambridge.org.uk">[The Museum of Cambridge]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">museumofcambridge.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Museum of Cambridge About – The Museum of Cambridge</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Museum of Cambridge About – The Museum of Cambridge</span></span></span>

## How Porter Collected Stories Before They Vanished

Porter’s method was energetic, informal and unusually alert to where folklore actually lived. She did not wait for polished stories to arrive by post. She listened in carpenters’ sheds, farmyards, public houses, fields, trains, buses, streets and even hospital wards, adding material to her notebooks from chance conversations as well as deliberate interviews. The Museum of Cambridge quotes her own account of gathering material in exactly those places, including fellow patients and visitors who supplied more Cambridgeshire memories while she was in hospital.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.museumofcambridge.org.uk/projects/tracing-traditions/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: museumofcambridge.org.uk">[The Museum of Cambridge]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">museumofcambridge.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Museum of Cambridge Tracing Traditions – The Museum of Cambridge</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Museum of Cambridge Tracing Traditions – The Museum of Cambridge</span></span></span>

This was not modern oral history in the formal sense of carefully recorded interviews with standard permissions, equipment and transcripts. It was earlier, more personal and more opportunistic. Capturing Cambridge describes Porter as a pioneer of oral history and stresses that she believed in going out to collect information rather than waiting for it to be brought to the museum. It also notes her view that folklore, social history and community life should not be kept in separate academic boxes.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://capturingcambridge.org/online-exhibitions-of-prior-years/herstory/enidporter/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: capturingcambridge.org">[Capturing Cambridge]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">capturingcambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on capturingcambridge.org.</span></span></span>

For readers today, that creates both value and caution. The value is obvious: Porter recorded material that might otherwise have disappeared with the people who knew it. The caution is that her notebooks and published accounts often preserve remembered tradition rather than verifiable event. A ghost story in Porter is evidence that a ghost story was told in Cambridgeshire; it is not evidence that a ghost appeared. A cure, charm or witchcraft belief is evidence of local belief culture; it is not medical or supernatural proof.

That distinction makes her work stronger, not weaker. Porter’s collections are most useful when read as a record of what people feared, hoped, repeated, doubted, laughed about and passed on. In a county split between university Cambridge and the wider fenland and rural communities, that is invaluable. It shows a Cambridgeshire where knowledge did not only come from colleges, courts and archives, but also from neighbours, elders, workers, storytellers and household practice.

## What She Collected From Daily Life

The range of *Cambridgeshire Customs and Folklore* is one of its most important features. Routledge’s table of contents shows that Porter organised the county’s folklore around the whole course of life and year: courtship, marriage, pregnancy, birth, child care, death and burial; birds, plants, animals, weather, trees and water; cures; calendar customs; ghosts and witchcraft; local legends and strange incidents; games, sports, university customs, civic customs, charity customs, land-letting traditions, rental customs and foodways.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.routledge.com/Cambridgeshire-Customs-and-Folklore-Pbdirect/Porter/p/book/9781138845435" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: routledge.com">[Routledge]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">routledge.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Cambridgeshire Customs and Folklore Pbdirect</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cambridgeshire Customs and Folklore Pbdirect</span></span></span>

That structure matters because it prevents Cambridgeshire folklore from being reduced to “haunted Cambridge” or [fenland witchcraft]({{ 'witches/' | relative_url }}) alone. Porter’s county includes uncanny stories, but also ordinary rituals of belonging. A charm against illness, a rule about courtship, a weather sign, a funeral custom or a village game may look modest beside a dramatic legend, yet together they reveal how people made sense of risk, luck, illness, love, death, work and place.

Several broad clusters stand out.

**Life-cycle customs** show how folklore marked vulnerable thresholds: birth, marriage and death. These are moments when families often rely on inherited rules, taboos and sayings, especially in rural communities where memory and reputation matter.

**Nature lore** ties belief to the Cambridgeshire landscape. Birds, plants, insects, reptiles, mammals, the moon, stars, trees, water and weather all appear in the book’s contents, which is especially fitting for a county shaped by fen drainage, wide skies, farming and seasonal labour.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.routledge.com/Cambridgeshire-Customs-and-Folklore-Pbdirect/Porter/p/book/9781138845435" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: routledge.com">[Routledge]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">routledge.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Cambridgeshire Customs and Folklore Pbdirect</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cambridgeshire Customs and Folklore Pbdirect</span></span></span>

**Healing and protective magic** connect the domestic world to wider English traditions of charms, wise women, household remedies and protective objects. The modern Museum of Cambridge still presents fenland traditions through themes such as witches, wise women, traditional cures, plants, potions and familiars, showing how Porter’s interests continue to shape public interpretation of the county.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.museumofcambridge.org.uk/resources/family-fun/fenland-traditions-and-folklore/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: museumofcambridge.org.uk">[The Museum of Cambridge]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">museumofcambridge.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on museumofcambridge.org.uk.</span></span></span>

**Ghosts, witchcraft and local legends** give the collection its more obviously supernatural appeal. But Porter’s treatment places them beside games, food, charity customs and civic practices, so they appear as part of everyday culture rather than isolated thrills.

## The Fenland Storytellers Behind the Books

Porter’s Cambridgeshire was not limited to Cambridge city. Her work in the Fens was especially important because the fenland north of the historic county preserved distinctive memories of water, work, isolation, dialect, seasonal rhythms and older rural practices. Two figures were central to this part of her collecting: W. H. Barrett of Brandon Creek and Arthur Randell of Magdalen, near King’s Lynn.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.museumofcambridge.org.uk/projects/tracing-traditions/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: museumofcambridge.org.uk">[The Museum of Cambridge]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">museumofcambridge.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Museum of Cambridge Tracing Traditions – The Museum of Cambridge</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Museum of Cambridge Tracing Traditions – The Museum of Cambridge</span></span></span>

The Museum of Cambridge describes Barrett and Randell as two outstanding Fenland storytellers. Porter gathered enough of Barrett’s memories to edit *Tales from the Fens* in 1963 and *More Tales from the Fens* in 1964, and the museum notes that Barrett supplied much of the source material for *Cambridgeshire Customs and Folklore*. With Randell, she gathered material that fed into several further Fenland books, including *Sixty Years a Fenman*, *Fenland Railwayman*, *Fenland Memories* and *Fenland Mole-Catcher*.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.museumofcambridge.org.uk/projects/tracing-traditions/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: museumofcambridge.org.uk">[The Museum of Cambridge]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">museumofcambridge.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Museum of Cambridge Tracing Traditions – The Museum of Cambridge</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Museum of Cambridge Tracing Traditions – The Museum of Cambridge</span></span></span>

These collaborations matter because they remind us that Porter’s “collection” was not only her own voice. It was a network of remembered speech. Barrett and Randell were not decorative informants added to an academic project; they were bearers of lived fenland knowledge. Through them, Porter preserved a world of eel-catching, railway work, mole-catching, river life, local characters and storytelling habits that would otherwise be much harder to reconstruct.

For Cambridgeshire folklore, this is a crucial point. The county’s traditions do not sit neatly within modern administrative boundaries or tourist images of Cambridge. Fenland memory crosses into Norfolk, Suffolk and the Isle of Ely’s older identity, and Porter’s work reflects that porous landscape. Her collecting keeps Cambridgeshire connected to the wider East Anglian world while still giving the county its own documentary centre.

## Why Her Evidence Still Shapes Cambridgeshire Folklore

Porter’s authority rests on more than nostalgia. Her work was recognised during her lifetime by major institutions. The Folklore Society lists Enid Porter among the 1968 recipients of the Coote Lake Medal, an award given for outstanding research and scholarship in folklore studies. Capturing Cambridge records that the University of Cambridge awarded her an honorary MA in 1972, followed by the Open University in 1981.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.folklore-society.com/awards/the-coote-lake-medal/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: folklore-society.com">[Folklore Society]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">folklore-society.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Folklore Society The Coote Lake Medal</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Folklore Society The Coote Lake Medal</span></span></span>

Modern heritage bodies still treat her notebooks as foundational. A Museum of Cambridge trustee pack describes the museum’s folklore and childhood collections as among the most important social-history collections in the region, based on Porter’s collecting and recording. It says her notebooks record stories, memories and observations from and about local people, capturing a time when older Cambridgeshire ways of life were disappearing.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.museumofcambridge.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2020/10/Trustee-Recruitment-pack-Oct-2020.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: museumofcambridge.org.uk">[The Museum of Cambridge]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">museumofcambridge.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Museum of Cambridge</span></span></span>

That is why many modern Cambridgeshire folklore accounts still lead back to her. When a local writer, museum volunteer, community project or folklore guide discusses Cambridgeshire ghosts, witchcraft, cures, seasonal customs or fenland storytelling, Porter is often somewhere in the evidence chain. Her 1969 book remains a county anchor because it gathers scattered oral material into an organised form that later readers can use.

There is also a democratic reason her work continues to matter. Cambridgeshire is often imagined through the university city: colleges, scholars, chapels, libraries and famous names. Porter’s collections pull the focus outward and downward, towards domestic rooms, village informants, museum visitors, fen workers, women’s memories, public houses and field labour. They make the county’s folklore less like a cabinet of curiosities and more like a social map.

## The Modern Afterlife of Porter’s Notebooks

Porter’s collections have not simply sat on a shelf. They have been reused in community heritage, museum interpretation and public-facing folklore projects. In 2014, the Enid Porter’s Folk project, managed by Cambridgeshire County Council and funded by the Heritage Lottery Fund, used Porter’s notebooks, owned by the Museum of Cambridge, to research and re-enact local folklore, customs and traditions. The project worked with Swaffham Prior, Haddenham, Littleport, Wisbech and Whittlesford, exploring stories, songs, dances, games and rituals associated with those communities around a century earlier.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambridgenetwork.co.uk/news/website-launched-bring-history-21st-century" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridgenetwork.co.uk">[Cambridge Network]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridgenetwork.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cambridgenetwork.co.uk.</span></span></span>

That project shows how Porter’s work functions today: not only as evidence for researchers, but as a prompt for living communities to ask what has survived, what has changed and what has been forgotten. It also shows the difference between collecting and revival. Porter recorded customs and memories from people who had inherited or remembered them. Modern projects may re-enact, reinterpret or share those traditions for education, performance and community identity. Both are valuable, but they are not the same stage of folklore.

The Museum of Cambridge’s more recent Tracing Traditions project explicitly presents itself as reviving Porter’s ethos by recording present-day stories, memories and experiences of customs and beliefs. The museum asks people to contribute their own stories and memories, continuing Porter’s idea that local tradition is not only found in old books but in what people still remember and practise.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.museumofcambridge.org.uk/projects/tracing-traditions/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: museumofcambridge.org.uk">[The Museum of Cambridge]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">museumofcambridge.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Museum of Cambridge Tracing Traditions – The Museum of Cambridge</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Museum of Cambridge Tracing Traditions – The Museum of Cambridge</span></span></span>

A 2024 Museum of Cambridge placement blog describes work on the museum’s folklore cabinet and mentions objects such as a wish bone, snakestone, witch ball and mummers’ sword. ## How to Read Porter Today

The best way to read Enid Porter is neither as unquestionable fact nor as quaint entertainment. Her collections are evidence of tradition: what people in and around Cambridgeshire said, remembered, feared, practised or believed. That makes them especially powerful for folklore, where the survival of a story often matters as much as whether the story describes a literal event.

A ghost recorded by Porter should be read as a local ghost tradition. A charm should be read as evidence of how people tried to manage illness, luck or danger. A calendar custom should be read as a social performance that tied a village, school, guild, farm or family to the turning year. A fenland anecdote should be read as memory shaped by landscape, work and identity.

Her work also asks a larger question about what counts as county history. Without Porter, Cambridgeshire folklore might look like a scattered set of dramatic legends: witches here, a black dog there, a college ghost somewhere else. With Porter, it becomes a dense record of everyday belief culture. She saved not only stories but habits of attention: the way people noticed weather, plants, animals, illness, death, luck, neighbours, roads, rivers and the unseen.

That is why Enid Porter remains central to Cambridgeshire folklore. She did not invent the county’s traditions, and she did not freeze them in time. She caught many of them at the point where living memory was beginning to slip away, then gave later readers, museums and communities enough evidence to hear those older voices again.
