## Introduction

Black Mere, also called Blake Mere, Blakemere Pond or the Mermaid’s Pool, is a small upland pool on Morridge near Leek in the [Staffordshire]({{ 'why-staffordshire-feels-ancient-and/' | relative_url }}) Moorlands. Its legend turns dark moorland water into a warning story: the pool is said to be bottomless, avoided by cattle and birds, haunted by a drowned or cursed mermaid, and dangerous to lone travellers, especially men. The tale matters because it is not just a “mermaid story” in an unlikely inland county. It is a Staffordshire way of talking about lonely roads, peat-stained water, drowning, sexual violence, witchcraft accusation, and the fear that a quiet-looking pool may have its own memory. Early antiquaries record the pool’s bad reputation before the mermaid is clearly visible in the tradition, while later retellings give the water a named supernatural inhabitant and a moral plot.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/stream/a-history-of-the-ancient-parish-of-leek/A_History_of_the_Ancient_Parish_of_Leek_djvu.txt" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Internet Archive Full text of &quot;A History Of The Ancient Parish Of Leek</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Internet Archive Full text of &quot;A History Of The Ancient Parish Of Leek</span></span></span>

## The mermaid warning story

The modern Black Mere legend is usually told as a dark, localised haunting. Somewhere on the high moor near the old Leek to Buxton route lies a black pool. In it lives a mermaid, but not the bright sea-maiden of children’s illustration. She is more often a dangerous water spirit, a drowned woman, or a wronged figure who rises from the mere to lure people into death. Local retellings commonly say she appears at midnight, combs her hair, and draws single men towards the water.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://insearchofholywellsandhealingsprings.wordpress.com/2021/04/19/mysterious-creatures-of-wells-and-springs-mermaids-of-the-peak-district/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: insearchofholywellsandhealingsprings.wordpress.com">[holyandhealingwells]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">insearchofholywellsandhealingsprings.wordpress.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wordpress.com.</span></span></span>

One widely repeated origin story makes the mermaid a woman punished after rejecting a man’s advances. In this version, a man named Joshua Linnet accuses her of witchcraft; she is drowned in the pool, curses him, and he is later found dead by the water with his face clawed. Another version makes her a pregnant woman murdered, or nearly murdered, by a lover who intends to drown her in the remote mere. A gentler but still melancholy strand says she was brought inland from the sea by a sailor from Thorncliffe, loved him, and became bitter after his death because she was stranded far from her own waters.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://insearchofholywellsandhealingsprings.wordpress.com/2021/04/19/mysterious-creatures-of-wells-and-springs-mermaids-of-the-peak-district/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: insearchofholywellsandhealingsprings.wordpress.com">[holyandhealingwells]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">insearchofholywellsandhealingsprings.wordpress.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wordpress.com.</span></span></span>

These versions do not all have equal age or evidential weight. The important point is the pattern they share: Black Mere becomes a place where human wrongdoing is absorbed into water. The pool is not simply “haunted”; it is haunted because betrayal, accusation, lust, fear and death have been attached to it. That is why the mermaid works so well as a Staffordshire Moorlands figure. She is both creature and consequence: the shape taken by a landscape where people already believed the water was uncanny.

## Plot, antiquaries and later retellings

Black Mere is unusually interesting because the place’s sinister reputation is older in print than the fully developed mermaid tale. Robert Plot, in his seventeenth-century account of Staffordshire, records the pool’s bad name but is openly sceptical of it. Later writers quote him as saying that people claimed no cattle would drink from the water, no bird would settle on it or fly over it, and that it was bottomless; Plot said he found these claims false, noting that his own horse drank there and that the depth had been measured.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://insearchofholywellsandhealingsprings.wordpress.com/2021/04/19/mysterious-creatures-of-wells-and-springs-mermaids-of-the-peak-district/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: insearchofholywellsandhealingsprings.wordpress.com">[holyandhealingwells]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">insearchofholywellsandhealingsprings.wordpress.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wordpress.com.</span></span></span>

That scepticism is useful. It shows that the pool already had a reputation for being dreadful, but it also warns against treating every element of the legend as an ancient belief preserved unchanged. Plot does not appear to give us the mermaid as the centre of the story. He gives us the mechanism that later folklore could build upon: a black, remote, mistrusted body of water that local people already talked about as unnatural.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/stream/a-history-of-the-ancient-parish-of-leek/A_History_of_the_Ancient_Parish_of_Leek_djvu.txt" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Internet Archive Full text of &quot;A History Of The Ancient Parish Of Leek</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Internet Archive Full text of &quot;A History Of The Ancient Parish Of Leek</span></span></span>

Nineteenth-century antiquarian writing adds more colour. *The Reliquary and Illustrated Archaeologist* describes “Blake Mere” or “Black Mere” as a small irregular pond in a hollow on the summit of Morridge, about three and a half miles east-north-east of Leek, pleasant enough in summer but grim in winter mist and cutting wind. It preserves an older literary tag about a lake so feared that beasts would not enter it even when chased. The same discussion repeats Plot’s debunking of the bottomless-pool and animal-avoidance claims, showing that folklore and scepticism travelled together in accounts of the place.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/stream/reliquaryandill09unkngoog/reliquaryandill09unkngoog_djvu.txt" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on archive.org.</span></span></span>

A later local history of Leek gives the pool another memorable layer. It retells the story of a poor woman lured to Black Mere on a stormy night by a “bloody ruffian” who had made her pregnant and meant to drown her. A man from Leek, having accepted a dare to visit the dreaded mere in bad weather, hears her cries and saves her by pretending other men are with him. In the same passage, the mermaid is said to have appeared to a startled rustic and warned that, if the waters burst their bounds, Leek and Leekfrith would be flooded.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/stream/a-history-of-the-ancient-parish-of-leek/A_History_of_the_Ancient_Parish_of_Leek_djvu.txt" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Internet Archive Full text of &quot;A History Of The Ancient Parish Of Leek</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Internet Archive Full text of &quot;A History Of The Ancient Parish Of Leek</span></span></span>

Charlotte Sophia Burne is important for the later folklore framing. She was a major collector of English folklore with Staffordshire connections, and her 1896 paper “What Folklore is, and how it is to be collected” is repeatedly linked with the Black Mere mermaid tradition in later discussions. Accounts drawing on Burne report origin-stories in which the mermaid is either a drowned pregnant woman or a woman killed as a witch, and they also preserve the tradition that she threatened to flood Leek when locals tried to drain the mere.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://jurn.link/spyders/2017/12/27/charlotte-s-burnes-paper-what-folkore-is-and-how-it-is-to-be-collected/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: jurn.link">[jurn.link]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">jurn.link</span><span class="citation-popover-title">charlotte s burnes paper what folkore is and how it is to be collected</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">charlotte s burnes paper what folkore is and how it is to be collected</span></span></span>

This gives Black Mere a layered documentary profile rather than a single neat “first version”. The pool’s fearful reputation is well attested in antiquarian material. The murder or attempted-murder associations have early local-history support. The mermaid, as a named supernatural figure with a tragic origin, becomes clearer in nineteenth-century and later folklore retellings. The result is not a weakness in the tradition; it is exactly how local legend often works, with older place-fear attracting newer narrative shape.

## Why dark water breeds legend

Black Mere’s folklore depends on the way the place feels. It is not a grand lake or a seaside cave, but a small moorland pool in an exposed upland setting. The surrounding Staffordshire Moorlands landscape helps explain why such a modest body of water could carry a large legend. Nearby Black Brook nature reserve, for example, is described by Staffordshire Wildlife Trust as part of a wild moorland landscape with wet, unsurfaced paths, tussocky vegetation, grazing animals, rare upland birds and protected moorland habitat within the Leek Moors conservation area.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.staffs-wildlife.org.uk/nature-reserves/black-brook" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: staffs-wildlife.org.uk">[staffs-wildlife.org.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">staffs-wildlife.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Black Brook &#124; Staffordshire Wildlife Trust</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Black Brook &#124; Staffordshire Wildlife Trust</span></span></span>

That kind of landscape makes the story believable at a human level even when the supernatural claim is treated as tradition rather than fact. A peat-dark pool can look deeper than it is. Mist and wind alter distance and sound. A lonely road makes ordinary danger feel moral and supernatural. Water that livestock supposedly avoid becomes a sign of hidden corruption. Birds not flying over it become a sign that even nature knows to keep away. Plot’s horse and the reported geese undercut the literal claim, but they do not erase the emotional truth: people were reading the pool as a warning place.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://insearchofholywellsandhealingsprings.wordpress.com/2021/04/19/mysterious-creatures-of-wells-and-springs-mermaids-of-the-peak-district/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: insearchofholywellsandhealingsprings.wordpress.com">[holyandhealingwells]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">insearchofholywellsandhealingsprings.wordpress.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wordpress.com.</span></span></span>

The “bottomless” motif is especially common in dangerous-water lore. It does not have to be hydrologically true to matter. In stories, bottomlessness means that the place cannot be easily measured, mastered or made safe. Later accounts report that modern investigation found the pool to be shallow rather than abyssal, while older retellings say attempts to drain or test the mere provoked the mermaid’s anger. The legend therefore turns practical curiosity into trespass: measure the water, and the water answers back.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://moathouse.co.uk/blog/post/5-beautiful-wild-swimming-spots-to-discover-in-the-peak-district" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: moathouse.co.uk">[The Moat House]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">moathouse.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on moathouse.co.uk.</span></span></span>

The flood-warning strand is one of the tale’s most revealing details. The mermaid is not only a killer or temptress; she is also the voice of the water’s boundary. When she threatens to flood Leek and Leekfrith if people continue draining the pool, the story makes the mere seem small but powerful, as though the upland hollow secretly commands the valley below. That is a classic folklore reversal: the little, overlooked place is imagined as having the power to overwhelm the town.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/stream/a-history-of-the-ancient-parish-of-leek/A_History_of_the_Ancient_Parish_of_Leek_djvu.txt" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Internet Archive Full text of &quot;A History Of The Ancient Parish Of Leek</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Internet Archive Full text of &quot;A History Of The Ancient Parish Of Leek</span></span></span>

## A Staffordshire mermaid, not a seaside one

Black Mere’s mermaid is striking because Staffordshire is landlocked. That apparent contradiction is part of the appeal. British mermaid traditions are not confined to coasts; inland pools, wells and springs can also produce water-women, nymphs and drowning spirits. A holy-wells and healing-springs survey of Peak District mermaid traditions notes freshwater mermaid accounts elsewhere in England and treats Black or Blake Mere as one of the Peak-edge examples, alongside the better-known Mermaid’s Pool near Kinder Scout in Derbyshire.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://insearchofholywellsandhealingsprings.wordpress.com/2021/04/19/mysterious-creatures-of-wells-and-springs-mermaids-of-the-peak-district/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: insearchofholywellsandhealingsprings.wordpress.com">[holyandhealingwells]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">insearchofholywellsandhealingsprings.wordpress.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wordpress.com.</span></span></span>

The comparison with Derbyshire helps but should not swallow the Staffordshire story. The Kinder Scout mermaid is often associated with reward, immortality, Easter-eve sighting, or wish-granting as well as danger. Black Mere’s mermaid is darker. Her tale is rooted less in magical blessing than in threat: men are lured, towns may flood, a woman is drowned, an accuser dies, and a lonely moorland road becomes a test of nerve.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://insearchofholywellsandhealingsprings.wordpress.com/2021/04/19/mysterious-creatures-of-wells-and-springs-mermaids-of-the-peak-district/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: insearchofholywellsandhealingsprings.wordpress.com">[holyandhealingwells]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">insearchofholywellsandhealingsprings.wordpress.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wordpress.com.</span></span></span>

There is also a close Staffordshire neighbour in Doxey Pool on the Roaches, often linked with Jenny Greenteeth or a malicious water-woman. Some modern walking and folklore accounts connect Doxey Pool and Black Mere through rumours of underground water links or shared Peak-edge mermaid lore. Those links are best treated as motif-kinship rather than proven geography. What matters for this page is that the north Staffordshire edge of the Peak District has a cluster of dangerous-water stories, with Black Mere as the county’s most distinctive mermaid case.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gopeakwalking.co.uk/the-doxey-pool-mermaid-the-legend-of-jenny-greenteeth/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gopeakwalking.co.uk">[gopeakwalking.co.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gopeakwalking.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Doxey Pool Mermaid</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Doxey Pool Mermaid</span></span></span>

The inland setting also changes the meaning of the mermaid herself. At the coast, a mermaid may stand for the lure and danger of the sea. At Black Mere, she stands for hidden water in a working upland landscape: a place passed by travellers, farmers, drinkers, drovers, walkers and local storytellers. Her “otherness” is sharpened because she does not belong where she is. The Thorncliffe sailor version even makes that displacement explicit, imagining a sea-being trapped in Staffordshire and turned hostile by exile.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://talesofbritainandireland.com/episode-56-peak-mermaid/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: talesofbritainandireland.com">[Tales of Britain and Ireland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">talesofbritainandireland.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">episode 56 peak mermaid</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">episode 56 peak mermaid</span></span></span>

## What the legend preserves

The Black Mere tradition preserves several kinds of memory at once. It preserves a place-name memory: Black Mere, Blake Mere and Blakemere all keep attention on the dark quality of the water. It preserves a road memory: Leek, Leekfrith, Morridge and the Buxton direction sit behind the stories as real local geography. It preserves social memory: the fear of women being harmed, accused, silenced or disposed of in remote places runs through the murder and witchcraft variants.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/stream/a-history-of-the-ancient-parish-of-leek/A_History_of_the_Ancient_Parish_of_Leek_djvu.txt" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Internet Archive Full text of &quot;A History Of The Ancient Parish Of Leek</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Internet Archive Full text of &quot;A History Of The Ancient Parish Of Leek</span></span></span>

It also preserves a way of managing risk. A story that says “do not go near the pool at midnight” is not only supernatural entertainment. It is a memorable safety rule for a bleak upland place where weather, darkness, boggy ground and water could genuinely endanger people. Folklore often gives practical caution a face. At Black Mere, that face is the mermaid.

At the same time, the legend is not just a disguised warning sign. Its staying power comes from its moral ambiguity. Is the mermaid evil, wronged, protective of her home, or all three? Is she a murdered woman transformed into a spirit of revenge, a fairy-like water being, a stranded sea creature, or the story-shape of the mere itself? Different retellings answer differently, which is why the tradition remains alive rather than fixed.

## How Black Mere is retold today

Today, Black Mere circulates through local-history writing, walking blogs, folklore podcasts, newspaper features, outdoor-interest sites and social media posts about the Peak District and Staffordshire Moorlands. Modern accounts tend to emphasise the same memorable elements: the landlocked mermaid, the supposedly bottomless pool, the cattle and birds that avoid it, the drowned woman or witchcraft story, the threat to flood Leek, and the eerie moorland setting.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://lichfieldlore.co.uk/2016/05/31/dark-water/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: lichfieldlore.co.uk">[lichfieldlore.co.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">lichfieldlore.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">dark water</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">dark water</span></span></span>

These modern retellings sometimes blur evidence and atmosphere. Claims such as “only inland mermaid legend in England” or “last recorded sighting” are attractive but should be handled carefully, because freshwater mermaid and water-woman traditions occur elsewhere, and the documentary trail is uneven. The stronger claim is narrower and more defensible: Black Mere is one of Staffordshire’s most vivid water legends and one of the best-known inland mermaid traditions on the English Peak-edge.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://insearchofholywellsandhealingsprings.wordpress.com/2021/04/19/mysterious-creatures-of-wells-and-springs-mermaids-of-the-peak-district/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: insearchofholywellsandhealingsprings.wordpress.com">[holyandhealingwells]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">insearchofholywellsandhealingsprings.wordpress.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wordpress.com.</span></span></span>

Its value within Staffordshire folklore is not that it proves belief in mermaids. Its value is that it shows how the county’s north-eastern moorlands generate a different kind of legend from the Potteries, Cannock Chase, Lichfield’s saints or Abbots Bromley’s ritual calendar. Black Mere belongs to the Staffordshire of dark roads, high wind, peat water, old inns, attempted journeys and stories told against the danger of going too close.
