## Introduction

Midlothian’s folklore is less a single tidy body of fairy tales than a landscape of charged places: [Roslin Glen]({{ 'roslin-glen/' | relative_url }}) with its cave and chapel legends, the witch-rock tradition at [Carlops]({{ 'carlops/' | relative_url }}), healing water at St Catherine’s Balm Well, prehistoric stones surviving in modern streets, and Edinburgh’s darker memories of prison, execution and witchcraft. The county’s strongest traditions are attached to named, visitable sites rather than to one dominant creature or hero. That makes Midlothian especially good for readers who like folklore rooted in roads, wells, ruins and old local names.



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/Midlothian_ff4e39-overview.webp" | relative_url }}" alt="Overview image for Where Midlothian&#x27;s Legends Meet the..." loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

For this page, Midlothian is treated as the historic county, not only the modern council area. That matters because historic Midlothian, long also known as Edinburghshire, included Edinburgh itself, while the present-day Midlothian council area is smaller and excludes places that still belong to the older county story. The Association of British Counties describes Midlothian as a historic county on the southern shore of the Firth of Forth, dominated by Edinburgh but extending well beyond it into glens, hills and former mining and farming districts.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://abcounties.com/counties/county-profiles/midlothian/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: abcounties.com">[Association of British Counties]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">abcounties.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Association of British Counties Midlothian</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Association of British Counties Midlothian</span></span></span>

## What counts as Midlothian folklore?

Midlothian folklore sits at the meeting point of Lowland Scottish tradition, medieval church culture, antiquarian tourism, and modern Gothic storytelling. Some traditions are old and local, such as names attached to rocks, wells and caves. Others are better understood as literary or tourist folklore: stories that may not be ancient oral tradition, but which have become part of how people now experience a place.

The county’s most famous example is [Rosslyn]({{ 'rosslyn/' | relative_url }}) Chapel at Roslin. The chapel was founded by Sir William St Clair in 1446, and its setting, ornate stonework and later role in *The Da Vinci Code* have made it one of Scotland’s best-known “mystery” sites. Rosslyn Chapel Trust itself frames the building as a place whose beauty, rural Midlothian setting and mysterious symbolism have attracted writers, artists and visitors for generations.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.rosslynchapel.com/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: rosslynchapel.com">[The Official Rosslyn Chapel Website]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">rosslynchapel.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on rosslynchapel.com.</span></span></span>

That fame can distort the folklore. Some Rosslyn stories are genuinely long-lived legends; others are modern speculative overlays involving the Holy Grail, Knights Templar or secret societies. A useful rule for Midlothian is to ask: is this story preserved in a place-name, parish memory, antiquarian record or local custom, or is it mainly a recent interpretation promoted through novels, tours and screen culture?

## The county boundary problem

Historic Midlothian and modern Midlothian are not identical. The historic county emerged in the Middle Ages around Edinburgh and was formally called the shire of Edinburgh or Edinburghshire until the 20th century. Modern local government changes in 1975 and 1996 produced a smaller Midlothian council area, while parts of the old county went to Edinburgh, East Lothian, West Lothian and the Borders.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Midlothian_%28historic%29" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Midlothian (historic</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Midlothian (historic</span></span></span>

For folklore, the historic county frame is the more useful one because legends do not obey modern council lines. Edinburgh Castle, the Heart of Midlothian mosaic, St Catherine’s Balm Well at Liberton, Roslin Glen, Penicuik and the Pentland edge all belong naturally to the older Midlothian story. At the same time, some traditions near the southern edge, such as Carlops and the Pentland routes, sit close to or across modern Borders boundaries. That is not a mistake; it is how oral tradition and old roads work.

The wider Lothian region also matters. Witchcraft records, holy wells, saints’ dedications, castle ghost stories and fairy traditions often move between Midlothian, East Lothian, West Lothian and the Borders. Midlothian’s folklore identity is therefore both county-based and route-based: it follows the Esk valleys, the Pentland passes, the old Edinburgh approaches and the paths around Roslin.

## Roslin and Rosslyn Chapel: Midlothian’s great legend-machine

Rosslyn Chapel is the county’s best-known folklore magnet. Its legends cluster around carvings, family memory, hidden chambers, ghosts, Green Men, dragons and the famous Apprentice Pillar. The chapel’s official visitor material says there are over 100 Green Man carvings inside and outside the building; these foliage-faced figures are widespread in medieval churches, but their sheer number at Rosslyn helps explain why visitors often read the chapel as a meeting place of Christianity, nature symbolism and older seasonal imagery.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.rosslynchapel.com/visit/things-to-do/count-the-green-men/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: rosslynchapel.com">[The Official Rosslyn Chapel Website]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">rosslynchapel.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Official Rosslyn Chapel Website Count The Green Men</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Official Rosslyn Chapel Website Count The Green Men</span></span></span>

The Apprentice Pillar is the clearest example of a chapel legend with deep staying power. The tale, told since at least the mid-17th century according to Rosslyn Chapel’s own account, says that a master mason went abroad for inspiration, while his apprentice dreamed the design and carved the pillar in his absence. When the master returned and saw the beautiful finished work, he killed the apprentice in jealous rage. The pillar itself is real; the story turns it into a moral drama about craft, envy and punishment.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.rosslynchapel.com/highlights/the-apprentice-pillar/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: rosslynchapel.com">[The Official Rosslyn Chapel Website]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">rosslynchapel.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Official Rosslyn Chapel Website The Apprentice Pillar</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Official Rosslyn Chapel Website The Apprentice Pillar</span></span></span>

Rosslyn’s dragons add another layer. The chapel identifies eight dragons at the base of the Apprentice Pillar and links them with the St Clair family’s Norse heritage, noting a possible association with dragons of Niflheim and the world tree Yggdrasil. Whether or not medieval worshippers read them in exactly that way, the carvings show how Rosslyn encourages mythic interpretation: stone details invite stories, and stories then guide how later visitors look at the stone.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.rosslynchapel.com/highlights/the-apprentice-pillar/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: rosslynchapel.com">[The Official Rosslyn Chapel Website]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">rosslynchapel.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Official Rosslyn Chapel Website The Apprentice Pillar</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Official Rosslyn Chapel Website The Apprentice Pillar</span></span></span>

The modern Rosslyn mythos changed dramatically after Dan Brown’s *The Da Vinci Code*. Rosslyn Chapel Trust says visitor numbers rose from 38,141 to 79,916 after the novel was published and to more than 176,000 after the film was released; a 2024 visitor-centre display explicitly marked the continuing “Da Vinci Code” effect.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.rosslynchapel.com/news/research-reveals-da-vinci-code-impact-21-years-after-publication/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: rosslynchapel.com">[The Official Rosslyn Chapel Website]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">rosslynchapel.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Official Rosslyn Chapel Website Research reveals Da Vinci Code impact</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Official Rosslyn Chapel Website Research reveals Da Vinci Code impact</span></span></span>

That does not make the Grail and Templar claims old Midlothian folklore in the same sense as a named rock or healing well. The better reading is that Rosslyn now has several folklore layers: medieval religious imagery, early modern and antiquarian legend, Romantic tourism, esoteric speculation, and 21st-century film-and-novel tourism. The modern layer is still folklore, but it is modern folklore rather than proof of medieval secrets.

## Roslin Glen, Wallace’s Cave and heroic landscape memory

Roslin Glen turns Midlothian folklore from architecture into landscape. Visit Midlothian describes Roslin Glen as an atmospheric place for a wander, with romantic ruins, woodland, Rosslyn Chapel and Roslin Castle linked by paths along the River North Esk.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://visitmidlothian.co.uk/places/roslin-glen/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: visitmidlothian.co.uk">[Visit Midlothian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">visitmidlothian.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on visitmidlothian.co.uk.</span></span></span>

One of the glen’s most memorable traditions is Wallace’s Cave, also known as Hawthornden Castle Cave. Historic Environment Scotland records it as a cave and rock-carving site, noting that it is traditionally thought to have been a hiding place of Sir William Wallace, though its actual date and function are unknown and no finds have been recorded. The official record adds that the cave could have held up to about 70 men.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://portal.historicenvironment.scot/apex/f?p=1505%3A300%3A%3A%3A%3A%3AVIEWTYPE%2CVIEWREF%3Adesignation%2CSM6825" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: portal.historicenvironment.scot">[Historic Environment Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">portal.historicenvironment.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on historicenvironment.scot.</span></span></span>

This is classic heroic folklore. The cave does not need documentary proof of Wallace’s stay to matter culturally. It matters because it fixes national resistance memory into a physical ledge above the River Esk, near the Battle of Roslin of 1303. A visitor can stand in a real wooded gorge and see how a difficult-to-reach cave could become attached to a fugitive hero story.

Roslin Glen also contains carved and marked stone traditions. Records for Roslin Glen carved stone describe a fish carving, possibly of Iron Age date, on a stone locally known as Lover’s Leap, while other records connect the glen with cup-and-ring style rock art and antiquarian interest.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.themodernantiquarian.com/site/842/roslin-glen" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: themodernantiquarian.com">[The Modern Antiquarian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">themodernantiquarian.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on themodernantiquarian.com.</span></span></span> The folklore here is quieter than the chapel’s, but perhaps older in feeling: a rock face, an unusual mark, a dangerous drop, and a name that invites explanation.

## Witches, “carlins” and the Pentland edge

The most vivid witch-place near Midlothian’s southern edge is Carlops, associated with the Carlin’s Loup or Witches’ Leap. Local walking and heritage sources explain the traditional derivation of the name as “the carlin’s loups” or “witches’ leaps”, referring to two prominent rock outcrops that once straddled the road near the village. One local account notes that only one outcrop survives prominently today.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pentlandfriends.org.uk/carlops/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pentlandfriends.org.uk">[pentlandfriends.org.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pentlandfriends.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on pentlandfriends.org.uk.</span></span></span>

The usual story says witches, or “carlins”, leapt between the two rock faces for entertainment. This is not a court record of witchcraft; it is a place-name legend, the kind of tale that explains a dramatic landscape feature and gives a dangerous gap a supernatural personality. Wikishire gives the same core account: two exposed rock faces, about 65 feet high, facing each other, with folklore saying witches leapt from one to the other across the chasm.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://wikishire.co.uk/wiki/Carlops" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wikishire.co.uk">[Wikishire]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wikishire.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wikishire.co.uk.</span></span></span>

The Carlops tradition also shows how folklore can drift into darker modern retelling. Some recent versions turn the leap into a trial-by-ordeal story in which accused women were thrown from the cliff. That version is memorable but much less securely grounded than the older “witches leaping” place-name explanation. For a county folklore page, the safer reading is that Carlops preserves a supernatural landscape-name tradition, not firm evidence of a particular execution practice.

This matters because Midlothian really does sit close to Scotland’s documented witchcraft history. The University of Edinburgh’s Survey of Scottish Witchcraft contains nearly 4,000 people known to have been accused of witchcraft in early modern Scotland, and its FAQ says 32% of named accused witches came from the Lothians.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://witches.hca.ed.ac.uk/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: witches.hca.ed.ac.uk">[Scottish Witchcraft Survey]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">witches.hca.ed.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on ed.ac.uk.</span></span></span> That hard historical background gives Midlothian’s witch-place names extra weight, but it also means modern retellings should be careful not to turn folklore into unsupported atrocity tales.

## Edinburgh’s dark customs inside historic Midlothian

Because Edinburgh belonged to historic Midlothian, the city’s traditions are part of the wider county folklore map, even though Edinburgh is now its own council area. Two examples are especially important: the Heart of Midlothian and the Witches’ Well.

The Heart of Midlothian is the heart-shaped mosaic outside St Giles’ Cathedral on the Royal Mile. It marks the site of the entrance to the Old Tolbooth, the prison demolished in 1817 and made famous by Sir Walter Scott’s 1818 novel *The Heart of Midlothian*. The public custom is to spit on the heart. Today this is often explained as a good-luck gesture, though many accounts trace it to contempt for the old prison and the executions associated with it.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://edinburghtourist.co.uk/questions/why-spit-heart-midlothian/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: edinburghtourist.co.uk">[Edinburgh Tourist]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">edinburghtourist.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Edinburgh Tourist Why spit on the Heart of Midlothian?</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Edinburgh Tourist Why spit on the Heart of Midlothian?</span></span></span>

This is a rare example of a living urban custom: not a tale told about a place, but a repeated action performed at a place. It is also a neat symbol of Midlothian’s folklore problem. Modern visitors may see it as a quirky tourist habit; older meanings point to punishment, resentment and civic memory.

The Witches’ Well near Edinburgh Castle is more solemn. Historic Edinburgh sources describe it as a memorial on Castlehill to people accused as witches and executed nearby. Historic Environment Scotland’s Edinburgh Castle blog says the well was installed on the Esplanade in 1894 as a modest memorial to those who suffered and died there, and notes that during the North Berwick witch panic at least 60 women and men were brought to Edinburgh Castle, tortured, and many were hanged or burned on the open ground now occupied by the Esplanade.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://blog.edinburghcastle.scot/the-scourge-of-evil/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: blog.edinburghcastle.scot">[Edinburgh Castle Blog]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">blog.edinburghcastle.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Edinburgh Castle Blog The scourge of evil</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Edinburgh Castle Blog The scourge of evil</span></span></span>

This is not folklore in the sense of “old fairy tale”, but it is part of belief culture: fear of witchcraft, state violence, public execution, later memorialisation, and modern reassessment. Recent Edinburgh tourism material also notes that Scotland’s witch persecutions ran from the 16th to 18th centuries and that Edinburgh’s Castle Esplanade became one of the country’s most notorious execution places for accused witches.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://edinburgh.org/blog/edinburghs-witches-wizards/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: edinburgh.org">[Forever Edinburgh]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">edinburgh.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on edinburgh.org.</span></span></span>

## Holy wells and healing water

Midlothian’s holy-well tradition is best represented by St Catherine’s Balm Well at Liberton, within historic Midlothian. The site is also known as St Catherine’s Well or the Oily Well because of the black oily substance associated with its water. Canmore records the well at Howdenhall Road and notes the historical claim that leprosy was treated there, while cautioning that this leprosy claim should be discounted because it relies on an erroneous derivation of Liberton as “leper-town”.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://canmore.org.uk/site/152718/edinburgh-howden-hall-road-st-catherines-house-balm-well" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: canmore.org.uk">[Canmore]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">canmore.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on canmore.org.uk.</span></span></span>

That caution is useful. Folklore often preserves genuine belief without preserving accurate medical or linguistic history. In this case, the well’s healing reputation is real as a tradition, but some explanations attached to it are doubtful.

The legend says St Catherine’s oil came to Liberton through Queen Margaret: a holy oil associated with St Catherine was spilled, and the spring appeared. The British Pilgrimage Trust summarises this tradition as a story in which Queen Margaret possessed a phial of oil from St Catherine’s shrine and, when it was spilt at Liberton, the holy well miraculously appeared.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.britishpilgrimage.org/places/st-catherines-well-liberton-edinburgh" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: britishpilgrimage.org">[British Pilgrimage Trust]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">britishpilgrimage.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on britishpilgrimage.org.</span></span></span>

There is also a geological explanation. Edinburgh Geological Society identifies Balm Well as a natural oil seepage linked to the nearby Pentland Fault and says the well was considered to have healing powers up until the early 20th century, particularly for eczema.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://edinburghgeolsoc.org/geological-site/balm-well/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: edinburghgeolsoc.org">[Edinburgh Geological Society]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">edinburghgeolsoc.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">balm well</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">balm well</span></span></span> The result is a perfect folklore pairing: an unusual natural phenomenon, a saintly origin story, royal associations and remembered healing practice.

## Standing stones, cup marks and prehistoric mystery

Midlothian’s prehistoric sites are not always wrapped in named supernatural tales, but they form the older mythic texture of the county. Standing stones and cup-marked rocks invite explanation because they outlast the communities that made them.

The Caiy Stane at Fairmilehead, now within Edinburgh but historically in Midlothian, is a striking example. Historic Environment Scotland lists the Caiy Stane as a scheduled standing stone, while local megalithic records describe it as a broad red sandstone monolith about 2.75 to 2.8 metres high. Its survival in a residential street makes it especially memorable: a prehistoric stone absorbed into suburban life.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://portal.historicenvironment.scot/apex/f?p=1505%3A300%3A%3A%3A%3A%3AVIEWTYPE%2CVIEWREF%3Adesignation%2CSM1155" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: portal.historicenvironment.scot">[Historic Environment Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">portal.historicenvironment.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on historicenvironment.scot.</span></span></span>

Later tradition says the stone may mark the site of an ancient battle, possibly involving Romans and Picts, and that other stones and cairns once stood nearby.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://wikishire.co.uk/wiki/Caiy_Stane" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wikishire.co.uk">[Wikishire]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wikishire.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Caiy Stane</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Caiy Stane</span></span></span> Such battle explanations are common around ancient stones. They may preserve a local memory of old burials or discoveries, but they can also be retrospective storytelling: people see bones, stones and mounds, then create a battle to explain them.

Cup-and-ring marked stones add another layer. Historic Environment Scotland explains, in its general account of Scottish rock art, that cup-and-ring marks probably belong to the Neolithic or early Bronze Age and occur across Scotland, with recurring motifs such as cup marks, rings, spirals and grooves.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.historicenvironment.scot/visit-a-place/places/drumtroddan-cup-and-ring-marked-rocks/history/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: historicenvironment.scot">[Historic Environment Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">historicenvironment.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on historicenvironment.scot.</span></span></span> Midlothian examples at Glencorse and Roslin Glen connect the county to that wider prehistoric symbolic world, even when no surviving local tale tells us exactly what the markings meant.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.themodernantiquarian.com/site/761/glencorse" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: themodernantiquarian.com">[The Modern Antiquarian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">themodernantiquarian.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on themodernantiquarian.com.</span></span></span>

## Ghosts, castles and modern haunting stories

Midlothian has many ghostly retellings, but they are unevenly attested. Some belong to long-running castle lore; others are modern hotel, tour or paranormal storytelling. That does not make them worthless, but it changes how they should be read.

Rosslyn Chapel and Roslin Castle are often retold as haunted places. Rosslyn Chapel Trust’s educational material discusses ghost stories around the chapel and castle as stories to be questioned rather than treated as fact, asking readers to consider what other explanations there might be for reported ghostly experiences.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.rosslynchapel.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/12/L2-Discussion-Topic-6-Ghosts.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: rosslynchapel.com">[The Official Rosslyn Chapel Website]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">rosslynchapel.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Official Rosslyn Chapel Website Ghosts</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Official Rosslyn Chapel Website Ghosts</span></span></span> That is a healthy approach: the ghost story is part of the place’s cultural atmosphere, but the evidence is anecdotal.

Dalhousie Castle near Bonnyrigg has a well-known Grey Lady story. Castle-hotel and heritage retellings usually identify her as Lady Catherine, said to have died after being imprisoned in a tower.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.celticcastles.com/articles/dalhousie-castle-ghost-story/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: celticcastles.com">[Celtic Castles]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">celticcastles.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Celtic Castles Dalhousie Castle</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Celtic Castles Dalhousie Castle</span></span></span> Borthwick Castle also has ghostlore attached to it, including stories involving Mary, Queen of Scots, though the strongest sources for the building itself are architectural and historical rather than folkloric.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Borthwick_Castle" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Borthwick Castle</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Borthwick Castle</span></span></span>

Midlothian is not especially known for a single county-defining black dog legend in the way East Anglia is known for Black Shuck. Reports of ghostly dogs appear more often in broader Scottish or Edinburgh ghost-tour material, including modern tales of a black dog at Edinburgh Castle’s pet cemetery.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.mercattours.com/blog-post/Edinburgh-Castle-Ghosts" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: mercattours.com">[Mercat Tours Ltd, Edinburgh, Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">mercattours.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Edinburgh Castle Ghosts</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Edinburgh Castle Ghosts</span></span></span> For this county, ghosts are more strongly attached to castles, closes, chapels and execution sites than to one famous spectral hound.

## Folklore in local identity today

Midlothian’s folklore matters because it gives the county a distinct identity beyond commuter towns and proximity to Edinburgh. Roslin Glen, Rosslyn Chapel, the Esk valleys, Dalkeith, Penicuik, the Pentlands and historic Edinburgh all carry stories that connect local landscape with memory.

Tourism has amplified this identity. Visit Midlothian promotes Roslin Glen through its atmosphere, ruins, chapel, castle and riverside setting, while Rosslyn Chapel’s own figures show how dramatically modern fiction reshaped visitor interest.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://visitmidlothian.co.uk/places/roslin-glen/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: visitmidlothian.co.uk">[Visit Midlothian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">visitmidlothian.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on visitmidlothian.co.uk.</span></span></span> Dalkeith Museum, meanwhile, presents a more grounded local-history model, focusing on the people, work, worship and everyday life of Dalkeith across the centuries.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://dalkeithhistorysociety.co.uk/dalkeith-museum/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: dalkeithhistorysociety.co.uk">[dalkeithhistorysociety.co.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">dalkeithhistorysociety.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on dalkeithhistorysociety.co.uk.</span></span></span>

The best modern retellings make room for both enchantment and evidence. Rosslyn can be mysterious without every carving proving a secret code. St Catherine’s Balm Well can be a holy healing place in tradition while also being an oil seep. Carlops can preserve a witch-name legend without needing an invented execution story. The Heart of Midlothian can be both a tourist curiosity and a survival of older civic resentment.

That balance is what makes Midlothian rewarding. Its folklore is not only about supernatural beings; it is about how people have read meaning into stone, water, caves, prisons, chapels, roads and ruins. The county’s legends survive because they are anchored in places that still ask questions when you stand in front of them.
