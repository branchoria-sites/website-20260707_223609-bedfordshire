## Introduction

[Lanarkshire]({{ 'what-makes-lanarkshires-folklore-so/' | relative_url }})’s witch records do not reveal a hidden coven so much as a dangerous meeting point between folk belief, church discipline and local fear. In 1649–50, during one of Scotland’s fiercest witch-hunting peaks, women accused in the Presbytery of Lanark were brought before ministers, imprisoned in Lanark Tolbooth and examined by the professional witch-pricker George Cathie. The surviving evidence is unusually valuable because it shows how quickly accusation could travel: a case that began with the “confessing witch” Janet Coutts in Peeblesshire spread into Lanarkshire through denunciation, official questioning and the search for the supposed Devil’s mark.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.futurelearn.com/info/courses/ems-palaeography/0/steps/48445" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: futurelearn.com">[FutureLearn]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">futurelearn.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Future Learn Witch-hunting in Lanarkshire: part 2</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Future Learn Witch-hunting in Lanarkshire: part 2</span></span></span>



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/Lanarkshire_60db75_witch_trials_cunning_c68e58-overview.webp" | relative_url }}" alt="Overview image for Witch Trials" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

For folklore, the importance lies in the fragments trapped inside hostile records. These minutes preserve hints of ordinary belief in charms, harmful speech, bewitched people and bewitched animals, but they were written by authorities trying to prove a capital crime. That means Lanarkshire’s witch-lore has to be read carefully: not as a reliable record of what accused people really did, but as evidence of how neighbourly suspicion, healing practice and religious fear could become legal danger.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.futurelearn.com/info/courses/ems-palaeography/0/steps/48444" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: futurelearn.com">[FutureLearn]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">futurelearn.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Future Learn Witch-hunting in Lanarkshire: part 1</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Future Learn Witch-hunting in Lanarkshire: part 1</span></span></span>

## Why Lanarkshire’s Witch Records Matter

Historic Lanarkshire was the county of Clydesdale, centred on the Clyde valley and including Lanark, Douglas, Lesmahagow, Carluke, Hamilton, Rutherglen and Glasgow within the older county frame. Wikishire describes Lanarkshire as embracing the valley of the Clyde, while modern local government boundaries now divide the old county story across South Lanarkshire, North Lanarkshire, Glasgow and neighbouring council areas.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://wikishire.co.uk/wiki/Lanarkshire" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wikishire.co.uk">[Wikishire]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wikishire.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wikishire.co.uk.</span></span></span>

That matters because the main Lanarkshire witch-hunt evidence belongs not to a vague “Scottish witch” legend but to a specific church-court landscape. The Presbytery of Lanark included parishes such as Carluke, Carmichael, Carnwath, Carstairs, Crawford, Crawfordjohn, Douglas, Lanark, Lesmahagow, Pettinain and Wiston. University of Glasgow teaching material notes that the presbytery records survive in full from 1623 to 1717, giving historians a rare continuous window into how accusations were handled locally.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.futurelearn.com/info/courses/ems-palaeography/0/steps/48444" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: futurelearn.com">[FutureLearn]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">futurelearn.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Future Learn Witch-hunting in Lanarkshire: part 1</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Future Learn Witch-hunting in Lanarkshire: part 1</span></span></span>

The county’s witch-lore is therefore less about later spooky storytelling than about paperwork: presbytery minutes, kirk discipline, court procedure, imprisonment, examinations and the grim machinery by which supernatural suspicion became official business. For a folklore reader, that is precisely what makes the material powerful. It shows belief culture at the moment it passed from rumour into record.

## The 1649–50 Witch-Hunt in Lanarkshire

The Lanarkshire cases sit inside the national witch-hunt of 1649–50, one of the major peaks of Scottish witch prosecution. Scotland’s Witchcraft Act of 1563 had made witchcraft, sorcery and necromancy capital offences, and the Covenanting regime of 1649 renewed the legal and religious pressure by ratifying the earlier act and widening concern to those thought to consult devils or familiar spirits.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.futurelearn.com/info/courses/ems-palaeography/0/steps/48444" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: futurelearn.com">[FutureLearn]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">futurelearn.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Future Learn Witch-hunting in Lanarkshire: part 1</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Future Learn Witch-hunting in Lanarkshire: part 1</span></span></span>

University of Glasgow’s account of the Lanarkshire material places the case in the wider panic of 1649–50, when more than 500 people were investigated for alleged witchcraft. The same source explains that kirk sessions and presbyteries were central to the process: local complaints might first reach a kirk session, then be sent upwards to the presbytery for examination and further action.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.futurelearn.com/info/courses/ems-palaeography/0/steps/48444" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: futurelearn.com">[FutureLearn]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">futurelearn.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Future Learn Witch-hunting in Lanarkshire: part 1</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Future Learn Witch-hunting in Lanarkshire: part 1</span></span></span>

In Lanark, the records become vivid on 8 November 1649. The presbytery minutes list a group sent from the Marquess of Douglas out of the parish of Crawford to appear before the presbytery: Jonet McBirnie, Lilias Moffet, Helene Acheson, Marion Moffet, Male Laidlaw, Bessie Gibsone, Jonet Acheson, Marion Hunter, Margaret Kilpatricke, Bessie Williamson and another Marion Moffet. They were said to have been “delated” for witchcraft by Jonet Cowts, a confessing witch then imprisoned in Peebles.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.futurelearn.com/info/courses/ems-palaeography/0/steps/48445" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: futurelearn.com">[FutureLearn]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">futurelearn.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Future Learn Witch-hunting in Lanarkshire: part 2</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Future Learn Witch-hunting in Lanarkshire: part 2</span></span></span>

Two weeks later, on 22 November 1649, servants of the Marquess of Douglas presented the accused before the presbytery. The minute says they were “obstinate”, meaning they did not satisfy their examiners, and the presbytery asked the magistrates to imprison them. Robert Birnie, minister of Lanark and moderator of the presbytery, was ordered to write for George Cathie, the witch-pricker, “for helping discover the marke”.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.futurelearn.com/info/courses/ems-palaeography/0/steps/48445" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: futurelearn.com">[FutureLearn]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">futurelearn.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Future Learn Witch-hunting in Lanarkshire: part 2</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Future Learn Witch-hunting in Lanarkshire: part 2</span></span></span>

This is one of the most important Lanarkshire moments. It places the accused women not in a fireside legend, but in Lanark Tolbooth, under the authority of ministers, magistrates and a professional specialist brought in to search their bodies for supposed proof of a demonic pact. A later University of Strathclyde course description summarises the same episode: women accused as witches were held in Lanark Tolbooth, while Cathie was brought there to find the mark of the Devil on those incarcerated.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://mycll.strath.ac.uk/View-Class/rdid/41/cr/5033" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: mycll.strath.ac.uk">[mycll.strath.ac.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">mycll.strath.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on strath.ac.uk.</span></span></span>

## What Happened at Lanark Tolbooth?

Lanark Tolbooth becomes the key folklore-and-records site for this subtopic. It was not simply a backdrop. It was the place where accused women were confined, examined and subjected to the ritualised search for hidden evidence on their bodies. According to the Lanark presbytery material transcribed by the University of Glasgow, Cathie’s pricking was reported on 6 December 1649: he “did prik pinnes in everie one of them”, and in several cases the pin was said to enter without pain, with witnesses able to testify.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.futurelearn.com/info/courses/ems-palaeography/0/steps/48445" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: futurelearn.com">[FutureLearn]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">futurelearn.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Future Learn Witch-hunting in Lanarkshire: part 2</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Future Learn Witch-hunting in Lanarkshire: part 2</span></span></span>

To modern readers, witch-pricking can sound like a grotesque superstition, but in the trial culture of the time it could function as evidence. The supposed Devil’s mark was believed to be an insensitive or bloodless spot on the body, given by the Devil to seal a pact. George Cathie was a professional witch-pricker from Tranent in East Lothian, one of several such men hired in seventeenth-century Scotland to locate these alleged marks.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.futurelearn.com/info/courses/ems-palaeography/0/steps/48445" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: futurelearn.com">[FutureLearn]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">futurelearn.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Future Learn Witch-hunting in Lanarkshire: part 2</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Future Learn Witch-hunting in Lanarkshire: part 2</span></span></span>

The Survey of Scottish Witchcraft entries for several Lanarkshire accused women confirm the pattern. Margaret Kilpatrick was arrested on 22 November 1649, imprisoned in Lanark Tolbooth, pricked on 2 December, and later had the action dropped in February 1650 after the investigators failed to find enough evidence. The Survey notes that Janet Coutts denounced her, that Cathie examined the suspects, and that Coutts and Cathie appeared to operate together in a way the record describes as “a bit of a double act”.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://witches.hca.ed.ac.uk/trial/T/JO/1121" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: witches.hca.ed.ac.uk">[witches.hca.ed.ac.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">witches.hca.ed.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on ed.ac.uk.</span></span></span>

Marion Moffat’s record is similar. She was arrested on 22 November, imprisoned in Lanark Tolbooth, pricked on 2 December, and the action against her was dropped after no witnesses were found. Another Marion Moffat, distinguished in the Survey as “Marion Moffat (second)”, was also imprisoned and pricked; her case was dismissed because, apart from Coutts’s accusation and Cathie’s evidence, there appears to have been no other evidence or witnesses.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://witches.hca.ed.ac.uk/trial/T/JO/1116" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: witches.hca.ed.ac.uk">[witches.hca.ed.ac.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">witches.hca.ed.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on ed.ac.uk.</span></span></span>

Bessie Gibson and Jonet Acheson show the same machinery at work. Gibson was denounced by Janet Coutts, linked with Cathie’s pricking, and apparently left without sufficient evidence for the case to proceed. Jonet Acheson’s case was dismissed after the record found little beyond Coutts and Cathie. These entries are important because they resist the common assumption that accusation automatically led to execution. In Lanarkshire, the presbytery’s process was dangerous and damaging, but several cases stalled when supporting evidence could not be produced.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://witches.hca.ed.ac.uk/trial/T/JO/1120" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: witches.hca.ed.ac.uk">[witches.hca.ed.ac.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">witches.hca.ed.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on ed.ac.uk.</span></span></span>

## Janet Coutts and the Spread of Accusation

Janet Coutts is central to the Lanarkshire episode, even though she belonged first to the Peeblesshire side of the story. The Survey of Scottish Witchcraft describes her case as a major hunt in which she seems to have put herself forward as someone who could identify witches. She was moved around the presbyteries of Peebles and Biggar to confront and name suspects, and probably named around 88 people. The Survey adds a crucial warning: the religious authorities appeared sceptical.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://witches.hca.ed.ac.uk/case/C/EGD/1817" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: witches.hca.ed.ac.uk">[witches.hca.ed.ac.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">witches.hca.ed.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">the case of Jonet Coutts</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">She seems to have put herself forward as someone who could identify witches in some way. That scepticism matters. Witch-hunts often expanded when one accused person named others, especially under pressure, imprisonment or torture. In this case, Coutts’s accusations reached Lanarkshire, where women were brought before the Presbytery of Lanark after being “delated” by her. The University of Glasgow transcription makes that chain visible in the first Lanark entry: people from the parish of Crawford were summoned because Coutts, then a confessing witch in Peebles, had named them.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.futurelearn.com/info/courses/ems-palaeography/0/steps/48445" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: futurelearn.com">[FutureLearn]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">futurelearn.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Future Learn Witch-hunting in Lanarkshire: part 2</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Future Learn Witch-hunting in Lanarkshire: part 2</span></span></span>

The Lanarkshire cases also show how fragile the evidence could be. Lillias Moffat’s Survey entry says she was named by Coutts, arrested and investigated by the presbytery, and pricked after a commission was granted, but “nothing found against” her led to her release. The note adds that ministers had wanted Coutts brought to confront those she named, but this seems not to have happened, making the case another example of Coutts naming people “without any cause”.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://witches.hca.ed.ac.uk/trial/T/JO/1113" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: witches.hca.ed.ac.uk">[witches.hca.ed.ac.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">witches.hca.ed.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on ed.ac.uk.</span></span></span>

Jonet McBirnie’s entry is especially telling because it records some witnesses who accused her of causing harm, yet the case was still dismissed after further investigation. McBirnie wanted Coutts brought to confront the suspects, but the record says this appears not to have happened. This is a rare glimpse of an accused woman pushing back against the logic of denunciation: if the accusation depended on Coutts, then Coutts should face the people she had named.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://witches.hca.ed.ac.uk/trial/T/JO/1114" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: witches.hca.ed.ac.uk">[witches.hca.ed.ac.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">witches.hca.ed.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on ed.ac.uk.</span></span></span>

For folklore, Coutts’s role exposes the danger of “knowing” who the witch was. A person believed to identify witches could become a terrifying authority, especially when paired with an examiner like Cathie. Yet the same records show doubt, contradiction and collapse. The Lanarkshire hunt did not simply move from belief to conviction; it also shows the limits of belief when officials could not make the evidence hold.

## Kirk Sessions, Presbyteries and Accusations of Malefice

The word “malefice” means harmful magic: the belief that someone caused illness, injury, death, spoiled milk, sick animals, damaged crops or other misfortune by supernatural means. In Lanarkshire’s records, the accusation process was shaped by church courts rather than by modern police or professional prosecutors. Kirk sessions monitored parish behaviour, while presbyteries supervised multiple parishes and could investigate serious cases.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.futurelearn.com/info/courses/ems-palaeography/0/steps/48444" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: futurelearn.com">[FutureLearn]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">futurelearn.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Future Learn Witch-hunting in Lanarkshire: part 1</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Future Learn Witch-hunting in Lanarkshire: part 1</span></span></span>

This matters because early modern witchcraft accusation often grew out of ordinary tensions. A quarrel, a refusal of help, an illness after an argument, an animal’s sudden decline, or a neighbour’s angry words could be reinterpreted as supernatural harm. The Survey of Scottish Witchcraft was designed partly to let researchers trace such themes across nearly 4,000 accused people, including cases involving neighbourhood quarrels, demonic possession, fairies and other belief categories.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://witches.hca.ed.ac.uk/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: witches.hca.ed.ac.uk">[witches.hca.ed.ac.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">witches.hca.ed.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on ed.ac.uk.</span></span></span>

Lanarkshire’s surviving material catches the institutional part of that process. By late December 1649, the Presbytery of Lanark was worried enough to authorise John Hume, minister of Lesmahagow, to request a commission from the Committee of Estates for “trying, confronting, imprisoning, entertaining, watching” and doing whatever else was necessary concerning people delated and suspected of witchcraft.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.futurelearn.com/info/courses/ems-palaeography/0/steps/48445" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: futurelearn.com">[FutureLearn]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">futurelearn.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Future Learn Witch-hunting in Lanarkshire: part 2</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Future Learn Witch-hunting in Lanarkshire: part 2</span></span></span>

Those verbs are chilling because they show how accusation expanded into a full administrative project. “Trying” meant examining the case; “confronting” meant bringing accuser and accused together; “imprisoning” meant holding suspects; “watching” could mean keeping them under observation, often with the possibility of sleep deprivation in Scottish witch-hunting contexts. Even when a Lanarkshire case ended in dismissal, the accused had already endured public suspicion, confinement and bodily examination.

The kirk records also show why local witch-lore cannot be treated as a neat list of beliefs. The surviving voices are filtered through ministers, clerks and legal categories. A neighbour’s fear of harm, a woman’s reputation, a healer’s charm, or a rumour about an animal may appear only when it becomes useful to an accusation. The folklore is there, but it is caught in a trap.

## Where Cunning Folk Fit In

“Cunning folk” is a broad modern label for people who were believed to use practical magic: healing, counter-witchcraft, charms, finding lost goods, identifying thieves, protecting animals or diagnosing supernatural harm. In Scotland, the language in records often centres on charmers, healers, wise people or people using specific rites, rather than always using the English phrase “cunning folk”. Historian Owen Davies’s comparative work notes that cunning folk and charmers were not prosecuted in the same numbers as witches, even though church and secular authorities could regard their practices as dangerous or diabolical.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1057/9780230591400_9" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: link.springer.com">[Springer]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">link.springer.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on springer.com.</span></span></span>

This is where Lanarkshire’s witch records become especially useful for folklore. They do not simply show people fearing evil witches. They point to a wider culture in which charms, counter-charms, healing knowledge and supernatural diagnosis could be part of everyday problem-solving. The danger was that a helpful charm in one setting could be reinterpreted as suspect magic in another.

A useful distinction is this: charmers were not automatically witches, but they lived close to the line that witch-hunters cared about. One study of charming in early modern Scotland puts the issue plainly: charming was one feature of witchcraft practice and belief, but not all charmers practised witchcraft and not all witches practised charming. In some cases, one person’s charmer could become another person’s witch. e g r e g o r e s<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://egregores.wordpress.com/2011/07/25/charming-and-witchcraft-in-early-modern-scotland/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: egregores.wordpress.com">[egregores.wordpress.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">egregores.wordpress.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">charming and witchcraft in early modern scotland</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">charming and witchcraft in early modern scotland</span></span></span>

The wider Scottish evidence supports that caution. A 2024 study of healers and midwives accused of witchcraft, based on the Survey of Scottish Witchcraft, notes that almost 4,000 people were accused in Scotland between 1563 and 1736 and that some were healers, midwives and nurses. This does not mean most accused witches were medical practitioners, nor that midwives were routinely targeted as a group. It means that healing and care could become legally dangerous when framed through witchcraft accusation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38029694/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nih.gov.</span></span></span>

For Lanarkshire, the phrase “witch trials and cunning folk” should therefore be handled carefully. The best evidence is not a romantic image of secret village magicians. It is the harsher fact that a community’s need for healing, explanation and protection existed alongside a legal-religious system that could turn magical reputation into prosecution.

## Why Hostile Records Distort Ordinary Folk Belief

The most important rule for reading Lanarkshire’s witch material is that the records are not neutral folklore notebooks. They were made by authorities investigating an alleged crime punishable by death. They preserve belief, but they preserve it under pressure.

Three distortions are especially important.

**First, the records privilege accusation over ordinary life.** We usually hear about charms, quarrels or strange events only because someone thought they helped prove witchcraft. A charm used to heal an animal, stop bleeding or protect a household might never be recorded unless it became suspicious.

**Second, the records translate local fear into official language.** A neighbour may have spoken in terms of grudges, illness or uncanny timing; the presbytery and court system reframed such stories through sin, confession, malefice, the Devil’s mark and legal proof.

**Third, the records often silence the accused.** Some entries say there was no confession, no defence or no surviving trial note. Even when an accused person’s words appear, they may have been shaped by interrogation, imprisonment, fear, exhaustion or the expectation that confession was the only way to make the questioning stop. The Survey’s Lanarkshire entries repeatedly record no confession, no defence and no completed central trial, which leaves the reader with procedure rather than personal testimony.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://witches.hca.ed.ac.uk/trial/T/JO/1121" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: witches.hca.ed.ac.uk">[witches.hca.ed.ac.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">witches.hca.ed.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on ed.ac.uk.</span></span></span>

This is why Lanarkshire’s records should not be read as proof that the accused practised harmful magic. They are evidence that many people in the county’s church-court world believed harmful magic was possible, that authorities took such claims seriously, and that certain kinds of reputation could become deadly.

## What the Records Reveal About Fear, Power and Place

The Lanarkshire witch-hunt reveals a county where belief moved along social routes as well as roads. The accusation came from Peebles; the suspects were sent from the lands of the Marquess of Douglas; the presbytery gathered them; Lanark Tolbooth held them; a pricker from Tranent was summoned; and a minister from Lesmahagow was authorised to seek a commission from the national Committee of Estates. This was not isolated village panic. It was a network.

The role of powerful men is visible throughout. William Douglas, 1st Marquess of Douglas, appears in the background of the Lanark records because accused people were sent from his parish of Crawford. Robert Birnie, minister of Lanark, acted within the presbytery structure. Gideon Jack, a representative of Lanark burgh in the Scottish Parliament, is noted in modern course material as present in the wider setting of the Tolbooth examinations.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.futurelearn.com/info/courses/ems-palaeography/0/steps/48445" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: futurelearn.com">[FutureLearn]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">futurelearn.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Future Learn Witch-hunting in Lanarkshire: part 2</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Future Learn Witch-hunting in Lanarkshire: part 2</span></span></span>

At the same time, the named accused were mostly women of comparatively local standing: Jonet McBirnie, Lilias Moffat, Helen Acheson, Marion Moffat, Male Laidlaw, Bessie Gibson, Jonet Acheson, Marion Hunter, Margaret Kilpatrick and others. Their names survive because suspicion settled on them, not because the records were designed to honour their lives.

This is part of the county’s darker folk memory. Lanarkshire is often remembered through saints’ wells, old customs, Covenanting history and industrial communities, but the witch records add another layer: the memory of how fragile ordinary safety could be when misfortune demanded a culprit.

## How Lanarkshire’s Witch-Lore Is Retold Today

Modern retellings increasingly treat Scotland’s witch trials as history of injustice rather than as entertainment. The University of Edinburgh’s Survey of Scottish Witchcraft remains the key public database, bringing together information on people known to have been accused between 1563 and 1736. The Heritage Fund has also highlighted later mapping work based on the Survey, designed to make the data more accessible to the public.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://witches.hca.ed.ac.uk/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: witches.hca.ed.ac.uk">[witches.hca.ed.ac.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">witches.hca.ed.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on ed.ac.uk.</span></span></span>

Lanarkshire’s cases are also used in teaching because the presbytery minutes are excellent examples of early modern Scottish handwriting, vocabulary and legal-religious procedure. The University of Glasgow’s palaeography course uses the Lanarkshire witch-hunt to teach readers how to work with presbytery records, while keeping the human stakes of the material visible.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.futurelearn.com/info/courses/ems-palaeography/0/steps/48444" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: futurelearn.com">[FutureLearn]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">futurelearn.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Future Learn Witch-hunting in Lanarkshire: part 1</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Future Learn Witch-hunting in Lanarkshire: part 1</span></span></span>

This shift in retelling matters. Older local lore could turn “witches” into spooky figures; modern evidence-led interpretation asks who was accused, who accused them, what institutions were involved, what evidence was considered, and why so many cases were built on fear rather than reliable proof. In Lanarkshire, that approach changes the story from “there were witches in the Tolbooth” to “people were imprisoned in the Tolbooth because a culture of fear made witchcraft accusations credible”.

## What Do Lanarkshire’s Witch Records Reveal?

Lanarkshire’s witch records reveal a county where folklore, law and religion overlapped in dangerous ways. They show that belief in harmful magic was not merely a fireside tale; it could mobilise ministers, magistrates, noble households, prisons, professional witch-prickers and national commissions. They also show that evidence could fail: several accused Lanarkshire women were released or had cases dropped when the accusations could not be substantiated beyond Janet Coutts’s denunciations and George Cathie’s bodily examinations.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://witches.hca.ed.ac.uk/trial/T/JO/1121" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: witches.hca.ed.ac.uk">[witches.hca.ed.ac.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">witches.hca.ed.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on ed.ac.uk.</span></span></span>

For readers interested in folklore, the value of the Lanarkshire material lies in its discomfort. It preserves traces of charms, witch-fear, malefice, cunning practice and supernatural diagnosis, but it does so through documents created by people trying to discipline, expose and punish. The records are therefore both rich and unreliable: rich because they show what people feared; unreliable because they distort the lives and voices of the accused.

The most grounded conclusion is not that Lanarkshire had a uniquely strange witch tradition. It is that Lanarkshire has unusually revealing evidence for how Lowland Scottish witch-belief worked in practice. In the winter of 1649–50, a rumour network became a presbytery case, a Peeblesshire accusation entered Clydesdale, women were held in Lanark Tolbooth, and the old boundary between folk healing, neighbour suspicion and capital crime became frighteningly thin.
