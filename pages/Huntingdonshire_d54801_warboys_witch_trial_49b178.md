## Introduction

The Warboys witch trial became [Huntingdonshire]({{ 'where-huntingdonshire-keeps-its-strange/' | relative_url }})’s best-known witch legend because it joined three powerful things in one story: a village quarrel, a formal assize trial at Huntingdon, and a printed warning tale that kept the case alive long after the executions. Between 1589 and 1593, the daughters of Robert Throckmorton of Warboys accused Alice Samuel, an elderly neighbour, of bewitching them. The accusations widened until Alice, her husband John and their daughter Agnes were tried for the death of Lady Cromwell and hanged in 1593. The case matters because it is not simply a spooky local tale: it is one of the most heavily discussed English witchcraft cases of the sixteenth century, preserved through a long 1593 pamphlet, later sermons, antiquarian interest, local memory and modern retellings.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://api.pageplace.de/preview/DT0400.9780857717924_A23726021/preview-9780857717924_A23726021.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: api.pageplace.de">[PagePlace]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">api.pageplace.de</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Page Place Witches of Warboys new</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Page Place Witches of Warboys new</span></span></span>

Warboys itself sits on the fen edge between Ramsey and St Ives, within historic Huntingdonshire. That geography is part of the afterlife of the story. The case belongs to a small county whose folklore is often tied to parish reputation, market-town justice, fenland suspicion and church memory rather than to grand mythic beasts or heroic romance. ## Why Warboys Became the County’s Witch Legend

Warboys became famous because the story moved quickly from household accusation to public example. The first drama unfolded inside the Throckmorton household, where children’s fits and accusations were interpreted through the religious and medical assumptions of the time. The second drama took place in the legal setting of Huntingdon assizes, where the Samuel family were turned from suspicious neighbours into condemned felons. The third drama came in print, where the case was reshaped as a moral warning with devils, familiars, torments and providential judgement.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cambridgeblog.org/2015/10/a-warning-to-witches-this-halloween/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridgeblog.org">[Fifteen Eighty Four]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridgeblog.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Fifteen Eighty Four A Warning to Witches</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Fifteen Eighty Four A Warning to Witches</span></span></span>

The case also had unusually high social reach for a village quarrel. Robert Throckmorton was a local gentleman; Lady Cromwell was connected to the powerful Cromwell family; Judge Edward Fenner presided over the trial; and the trial took place at Huntingdon rather than remaining a purely village matter. Cambridge Core identifies Anne Reiber DeWindt’s study of Warboys as a research article in the Journal of British Studies, and that framing is important: modern historians have treated the case not only as witchcraft folklore but as evidence for competing ideas of what a “good” village community should be.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-british-studies/article/witchcraft-and-conflicting-visions-of-the-ideal-village-community/710965E8AA13316937308871F45EAAAD" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridge.org">[Cambridge University Press &amp; Assessment]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cambridge.org.</span></span></span>

For folklore readers, this is the heart of Warboys. The legend is not just “three witches were hanged”. It is a story about how a community explains misfortune, how the words of children can become public evidence, how elite households shape local belief, and how a parish can remember a terrible case for centuries.

## The Throckmorton Accusations

The surviving story begins with illness and interpretation. In November 1589, Jane Throckmorton, one of Robert Throckmorton’s daughters, was said to suffer violent fits. Alice Samuel, an elderly local woman, was accused of causing them. The allegations spread to other Throckmorton daughters and household servants, creating the impression of a besieged household. The later printed account described the girls’ torments in vivid physical terms, the kind of detail designed to convince readers that something more than ordinary sickness was happening.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Witches_of_Warboys" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Witches of Warboys</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Witches of Warboys</span></span></span>

One reason the case has lasted so strongly in local memory is that it began in a recognisable village setting. Alice Samuel was not a distant monster from legend. She was a neighbour. That makes Warboys different from a fairy hill or a black-dog road. Its fear comes from social closeness: the old woman next door, the child in the sickroom, the household servant repeating what she has heard, the parish minister called in to make sense of it.

Lady Cromwell’s visit gave the accusations a fatal turn. The accounts say she confronted Alice Samuel, cut off a lock of her hair, and later suffered dreams or illness before dying in 1592. Cutting or burning a suspected witch’s hair belonged to a wider world of anti-witchcraft practice: not official medicine, but a practical folk response by which frightened people tried to break a witch’s supposed power. In the Warboys story, that small act becomes part of the chain by which suspicion hardens into a capital charge.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Witches_of_Warboys" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Witches of Warboys</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Witches of Warboys</span></span></span>

The Throckmorton accusations therefore survive in two ways at once. As history, they show how early modern people connected sickness, household disorder and witchcraft. As folklore, they show how a story attaches itself to a place: Warboys becomes the village where fear entered the home and would not leave.

## The Trial at Huntingdon

The Samuel family were tried not at Warboys but at Huntingdon, the county town. That matters. It turned a village panic into a county event. The charge centred on the alleged bewitching to death of Lady Cromwell, and the punishment followed the severe witchcraft law then in force. Philip Almond’s account notes that the Samuels were tried under a section of the statute by which witchcraft, enchantment, charm or sorcery causing death could be punished as felony.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://api.pageplace.de/preview/DT0400.9780857717924_A23726021/preview-9780857717924_A23726021.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: api.pageplace.de">[PagePlace]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">api.pageplace.de</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Page Place Witches of Warboys new</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Page Place Witches of Warboys new</span></span></span>

The legal evidence available to us is frustratingly narrow. Almond stresses that the only full account of the Warboys story is the printed work usually known by its long title, The Most Strange and Admirable Discoverie of the Three Witches of Warboys. It was published in 1593, under the patronage of Judge Edward Fenner, who had presided over the trial. No separate trial record has survived, which means modern readers must treat the pamphlet as both indispensable and partial.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://api.pageplace.de/preview/DT0400.9780857717924_A23726021/preview-9780857717924_A23726021.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: api.pageplace.de">[PagePlace]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">api.pageplace.de</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Page Place Witches of Warboys new</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Page Place Witches of Warboys new</span></span></span>

That is why the case should be read carefully. The pamphlet is not a neutral transcript. It is a printed, moralised account shaped for readers who already believed witchcraft was real and dangerous. At the same time, it is not simply a late ghost story invented from nothing. It names people, places and institutions, and it entered the Stationers’ Register in 1593, very close to the event itself. Almond notes that the work appears to have entered public circulation within months of the executions, and that copies survive in major collections including the British Library, the Bodleian, the Norris Museum Library at St Ives, the Folger Shakespeare Library and the University of Glasgow Library.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://api.pageplace.de/preview/DT0400.9780857717924_A23726021/preview-9780857717924_A23726021.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: api.pageplace.de">[PagePlace]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">api.pageplace.de</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Page Place Witches of Warboys new</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Page Place Witches of Warboys new</span></span></span>

The trial’s local geography is still important for understanding its afterlife. Warboys supplied the accusation; Huntingdon supplied the court, the gallows and later the sermon tradition. That two-place structure helped the legend spread across the county. It was not just “something that happened in one village”. It became a Huntingdonshire warning, located between village household, county justice and parish preaching.

## Pamphlet, Sermon and Local Memory

The 1593 pamphlet made Warboys portable. Its full title promised readers the bewitching of five Throckmorton daughters, the tormenting of other people, and the bewitching to death of Lady Cromwell. Internet Archive’s catalogue identifies the 1593 printed item and describes it as a 111-page digitised work, while Almond’s preview describes the Warboys account as unusually long for a witchcraft and possession text, about 50,000 words across more than 110 pages.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/details/bim_early-english-books-1475-1640_the-most-strange-and-adm_warboys_1593" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">bim early english books 1475 1640 the most strange and adm warboys 1593</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Internet ArchiveThe most strange and admirable discoverie of the three witches of Warboys... 1593: Warboys.: Free Download, Borrow, a...</span></span></span>

That length matters. A short news-sheet might have faded quickly. A long pamphlet could preserve scenes, speeches, accusations, confessions, denials, household episodes and moral interpretations. It gave later writers a script. It also fixed the story’s tone: Warboys was remembered not as a doubtful tragedy, at least at first, but as a providential discovery of hidden evil.

The sermon tradition gave the case an even longer local afterlife. Cambridge University Press’s blog recounts that Sir Henry Cromwell used property confiscated from the Samuels to fund an annual sermon at All Saints’ Church in Huntingdon, intended to discourage witchcraft. The same account notes that these sermons continued into the early nineteenth century, by which time attitudes to witchcraft had changed so much that the message increasingly became a warning against superstition rather than against practising witchcraft.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cambridgeblog.org/2015/10/a-warning-to-witches-this-halloween/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridgeblog.org">[Fifteen Eighty Four]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridgeblog.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Fifteen Eighty Four A Warning to Witches</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Fifteen Eighty Four A Warning to Witches</span></span></span>

This is one of the most striking features of the Warboys afterlife. The county did not simply remember the case through rumour. It ritualised it. An annual sermon meant that the story could be renewed in public, year after year, in a church setting. Over time, the meaning shifted. What began as a warning that witches were real became, in later Enlightenment-inflected preaching, a warning that belief in witches was itself dangerous.

The case also entered popular culture quickly. Almond notes that a ballad called A Lamentable Songe of Three Wytches of Warbos was entered in the Stationers’ Registers in December 1593, although the ballad itself has not survived. That lost song is important because it shows that Warboys was not confined to legal or clerical culture. ## What the Evidence Can and Cannot Prove

The strongest evidence for Warboys is also the main difficulty: nearly everything runs through the 1593 pamphlet. It is close to the events, but it was produced in a culture that expected witchcraft narratives to demonstrate guilt, divine judgement and the danger of Satanic influence. The pamphlet preserved the case, but it also shaped how the case could be remembered.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://api.pageplace.de/preview/DT0400.9780857717924_A23726021/preview-9780857717924_A23726021.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: api.pageplace.de">[PagePlace]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">api.pageplace.de</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Page Place Witches of Warboys new</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Page Place Witches of Warboys new</span></span></span>

This means a good reading of Warboys has to separate several layers:

**The historical core:** Alice, John and Agnes Samuel were accused, tried at Huntingdon and executed in 1593. The case was connected to the Throckmorton household and to Lady Cromwell’s death.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Witches_of_Warboys" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Witches of Warboys</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Witches of Warboys</span></span></span>

**The printed witchcraft story:** The pamphlet presented the events as a dramatic discovery of witches, devils, torments and guilt. It is the source that made the case famous, but it is not a detached court record.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://api.pageplace.de/preview/DT0400.9780857717924_A23726021/preview-9780857717924_A23726021.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: api.pageplace.de">[PagePlace]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">api.pageplace.de</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Page Place Witches of Warboys new</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Page Place Witches of Warboys new</span></span></span>

**The local memorial practice:** Sir Henry Cromwell’s funded sermon at All Saints’ Church in Huntingdon helped keep the case alive as a public warning for generations.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cambridgeblog.org/2015/10/a-warning-to-witches-this-halloween/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridgeblog.org">[Fifteen Eighty Four]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridgeblog.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Fifteen Eighty Four A Warning to Witches</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Fifteen Eighty Four A Warning to Witches</span></span></span>

**The later folklore afterlife:** Local retellings, museum interpretation, county histories and modern media have turned Warboys into Huntingdonshire’s emblematic witch story. The details are often simplified, and some popular versions move too quickly from “accused witches” to “witches” without making clear that the supernatural claims are historical allegations, not facts.

This evidence pattern makes Warboys powerful but not simple. We know far more about how the case was narrated than about what the Throckmorton children were medically experiencing, how testimony was shaped inside the household, or what pressures were placed on the Samuels. That uncertainty is not a weakness of the story. It is part of why the case still matters.

## Why the Story Still Clings to Warboys

Warboys remains memorable because it has a rare combination of place, people and paper trail. Many English villages had witchcraft rumours. Far fewer produced a case that linked a fen-edge parish, a gentry household, a Cromwell family death, a county assize, a long printed pamphlet, a lost ballad and an annual sermon. That is why Warboys stands out within Huntingdonshire folklore.

The village afterlife also includes localised legends that do not always match the legal history. Some modern local storytelling connects the witches with the Weir, a pond in the centre of Warboys at the fork of High Street and Mill Green, and with ideas about trial by water. Such stories show how folklore relocates memory into visible landscape. The historical trial, however, was at Huntingdon, and the Samuels were hanged after conviction; claims about village drowning or trial at the pond should be treated as later local legend rather than the core documented event.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://fenlandphil.com/2021/02/28/the-warboys-witches/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: fenlandphil.com">[fenlandphil.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">fenlandphil.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">s Blog The Warboys Witches &#124; Fenlandphil&#x27;s Blog</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">s Blog The Warboys Witches &#124; Fenlandphil&#x27;s Blog</span></span></span>

This distinction is useful for visitors and readers. The historically grounded Warboys story belongs to the road between Warboys and Huntingdon: accusation in the village, judgement in the county town, remembrance in church and print. The later folklore belongs to the ways people try to pin a disturbing memory to a pond, a crossroads, a parish name or a Halloween retelling.

## Warboys in Huntingdonshire Folklore Today

Today, the Warboys case is often described as one of England’s most famous witch trials, and within Huntingdonshire it is the county’s central witchcraft memory. Modern attention comes through local history talks, museum material, digitised early printed books, academic studies and popular history podcasts or videos. The Cromwell connection gives it another route into public memory, because Lady Cromwell was linked to the family from which Oliver Cromwell later emerged.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=FpwI_QdVfk0" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: youtube.com">[youtube.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">youtube.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on youtube.com.</span></span></span>

Its modern value is not that readers should believe the accusations. Its value is that Warboys shows how folklore can become law, and how law can become folklore. A child’s accusation, a neighbour’s reputation, a minister’s interpretation and a printed pamphlet worked together to produce a story that outlived everyone involved. The Samuels died as convicted witches; later generations inherited a warning tale, then a superstition sermon, then a local legend, then a case study in fear, power and memory.

Within the wider Huntingdonshire branch, Warboys sits naturally beside later witchcraft accusations, cunning-folk beliefs, fenland supernatural warnings and local ghost traditions. But it remains distinct because it is unusually well-attested in print and unusually rooted in county institutions. It is not just a tale told beside the fire. It is a Huntingdonshire event that became a pamphlet, a pulpit tradition and a lasting name in England’s witchcraft history.
