## Introduction

Brington is the clearest place-name in [Northamptonshire]({{ 'where-northamptonshires-strange-stories/' | relative_url }}) fairy lore. In Thomas Sternberg’s *The Dialect and Folk-lore of Northamptonshire* of 1851, the parish appears twice in a compact but memorable cluster of traditions: a “fairy pool” near the village where fairies were said to sport among the water-plants, and a locally famous “fairies’ ring” in pasture, believed to mark an underground dwelling and to resist the plough. The stories matter because they give Northamptonshire’s fairies a definite landscape: not a vague enchanted kingdom, but damp edges, lonely wells, moonlit fields and old grassland around a real parish west of Northampton.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/stream/dialectfolkloreo00steruoft/dialectfolkloreo00steruoft_djvu.txt" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Internet Archive Full text of &quot;The dialect and folk-lore of Northamptonshire</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Internet Archive Full text of &quot;The dialect and folk-lore of Northamptonshire</span></span></span>



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/Northamptonshire_ad0dee_brington_fairy_place_710345-overview.webp" | relative_url }}" alt="Overview image for Fairy Places" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

The evidence is not abundant, and that is part of the story. Brington’s fairy places are known above all because Sternberg preserved them at a moment when he thought rural fairy belief was already fading. They are best read as nineteenth-century records of older local belief, rather than as fully mapped tourist sites. Their power lies in the way they connect ordinary Northamptonshire countryside with older ideas about hidden people below the ground, dangerous circles in pasture, and water places that became uncanny after dusk.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/stream/dialectfolkloreo00steruoft/dialectfolkloreo00steruoft_djvu.txt" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Internet Archive Full text of &quot;The dialect and folk-lore of Northamptonshire</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Internet Archive Full text of &quot;The dialect and folk-lore of Northamptonshire</span></span></span>

## Brington’s famous fairy ring

Sternberg describes the “green sour ringlets” found on pasture land as marks made by fairies. In the belief he records, the dark circle was not merely a surface pattern: the fairies’ dwelling was imagined to lie beneath the enclosed area. Brington supplied his strongest local example. In the parish, he says, there was one ring so celebrated locally that it was known above all others as “the fairies’ ring”.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/stream/dialectfolkloreo00steruoft/dialectfolkloreo00steruoft_djvu.txt" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Internet Archive Full text of &quot;The dialect and folk-lore of Northamptonshire</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Internet Archive Full text of &quot;The dialect and folk-lore of Northamptonshire</span></span></span>

The details are unusually rich for a short county folklore notice. Sternberg says the Brington ring was believed to have existed “from the earliest times” and to have resisted attempts by the plough to erase it. The story also carried a warning: efforts to destroy the ring were associated with “awful calamities”, turning a patch of pasture into a protected, tabooed place. In practical terms, the legend told people that this was not ordinary ground. It should not be ploughed, mocked, or treated carelessly.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/stream/dialectfolkloreo00steruoft/dialectfolkloreo00steruoft_djvu.txt" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Internet Archive Full text of &quot;The dialect and folk-lore of Northamptonshire</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Internet Archive Full text of &quot;The dialect and folk-lore of Northamptonshire</span></span></span>

The full-moon tradition is the most vivid part of the account. According to village tradition, anyone who ran round the ring nine times on the first night of the full moon might hear “mirth and revelry” rising from the subterranean fairy dwelling. That small ritual gives the story its shape: the ring was a threshold, the moon supplied the right time, the number nine gave the act a ritual pattern, and the reward was not the sight of fairies but the sound of their hidden life below the field.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/stream/dialectfolkloreo00steruoft/dialectfolkloreo00steruoft_djvu.txt" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Internet Archive Full text of &quot;The dialect and folk-lore of Northamptonshire</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Internet Archive Full text of &quot;The dialect and folk-lore of Northamptonshire</span></span></span>

Modern natural history gives a different explanation for rings in grass. The Woodland Trust explains that fairy rings are caused by an individual fungus spreading underground in a circular pattern, with mushrooms or changes in grass growth appearing around the edge as the mycelium expands. The Royal Horticultural Society likewise describes fairy rings as fungal colonies that may create circles of dead grass, dark green grass, or toadstools in lawns and grassland. This does not make the folklore less interesting; it explains why such circles were memorable enough to attract stories.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.woodlandtrust.org.uk/blog/2019/08/what-is-a-fairy-ring/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: woodlandtrust.org.uk">[Woodland Trust]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">woodlandtrust.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">what is a fairy ring</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The fungus sprouts lots of small threads, called mycelium, in a circular...Read more...</span></span></span>

The Brington tale therefore sits at the meeting point between observation and belief. People saw a ring that seemed to endure, return, or resist disturbance. Folklore supplied a social and imaginative explanation: the fairies lived beneath it, and the ploughman who violated it risked trouble. The tale also makes sense in a rural county where pasture, old field boundaries and estate landscapes shaped everyday experience more than spectacular mountains or ruins.

## Pools, wells and moonlit fields

Brington’s fairy lore is not only about rings. Just before the ring passage, Sternberg says Northamptonshire fairies showed a marked liking for water. They were imagined bathing and playing in lonely ponds or sedgy bends of brooks on summer evenings, and in some parts of the county such ponds were known as “fairy pools”. Near Brington, he records one such pool where he had been assured that, only a few years earlier, fairies could be seen on the surface and among the water-plants at the edges.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/stream/dialectfolkloreo00steruoft/dialectfolkloreo00steruoft_djvu.txt" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Internet Archive Full text of &quot;The dialect and folk-lore of Northamptonshire</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Internet Archive Full text of &quot;The dialect and folk-lore of Northamptonshire</span></span></span>

This is important because it gives Northamptonshire fairy belief two linked settings: the ring and the pool. The ring belongs to pasture and the hidden underground dwelling; the pool belongs to twilight, still water and the living edges of reeds or sedges. Together they show that Brington’s fairies were not imagined as distant courtly figures. They were attached to places villagers might pass, work near, or avoid after dark.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/stream/dialectfolkloreo00steruoft/dialectfolkloreo00steruoft_djvu.txt" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Internet Archive Full text of &quot;The dialect and folk-lore of Northamptonshire</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Internet Archive Full text of &quot;The dialect and folk-lore of Northamptonshire</span></span></span>

Sternberg also extends the pattern to wells. He says wells were favourite fairy resorts and notes a “vague” apprehension in the rural mind about even passing a lonely well after nightfall. That phrase is revealing. The belief did not need a long narrative every time; sometimes the folklore survived as a feeling about place — a sense that a well, pool, or dark water-edge was not quite neutral after dusk.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/stream/dialectfolkloreo00steruoft/dialectfolkloreo00steruoft_djvu.txt" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Internet Archive Full text of &quot;The dialect and folk-lore of Northamptonshire</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Internet Archive Full text of &quot;The dialect and folk-lore of Northamptonshire</span></span></span>

This water-setting fits wider Northamptonshire material in Sternberg’s book, where wells and springs receive their own section and are treated as places with lingering supernatural associations. He later writes of older belief around wells and fountains, including the idea that their surroundings were favoured by elves and that dark waters might house a well-spirit. Brington’s fairy pool is therefore not an isolated oddity, but one of the clearest local examples of a wider county pattern.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/stream/dialectfolkloreo00steruoft/dialectfolkloreo00steruoft_djvu.txt" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Internet Archive Full text of &quot;The dialect and folk-lore of Northamptonshire</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Internet Archive Full text of &quot;The dialect and folk-lore of Northamptonshire</span></span></span>

The landscape around Brington helps explain why such stories could attach there. The modern civil parish includes Great Brington, Little Brington and Nobottle, and the parish history places it within a long local story of medieval estates, church life, Althorp connections and older settlement names. Great Brington’s parish church of St Mary the Virgin is described by the parish council as the oldest and largest building in the village, while Althorp remained part of Brington parish until the seventeenth century.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://bringtons-pc.gov.uk/history-of-the-parish/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: bringtons-pc.gov.uk">[bringtons-pc.gov.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">bringtons-pc.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">history of the parish</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">history of the parish</span></span></span>

That does not prove the exact location of the fairy pool or ring today. It does, however, place the tradition in a plausible rural setting of pasture, water, lanes, church-centred village life and large estate land. Historic England describes nearby Althorp as a landscape park with features from the sixteenth century onwards, while Northamptonshire’s Historic Environment Record notes park development from the early sixteenth century and later surviving earthworks. Brington’s fairy places belong to the humbler, oral side of that same countryside: the remembered field, the pond-edge, the avoided well.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://historicengland.org.uk/listing/the-list/list-entry/1001023" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: historicengland.org.uk">[Historic England]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">historicengland.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Historic England Althorp, Althorp</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Historic England Althorp, Althorp</span></span></span>

## How Sternberg preserved the stories

Thomas Sternberg is the key witness for Brington’s fairy places. His 1851 book was explicitly designed to collect Northamptonshire dialect words, popular superstitions, fairy lore and other survivals among the county’s rural population. In his preface, he says the fairy material was worth preserving because it brought to light details of a “fast disappearing” branch of popular mythology in Northamptonshire and surrounding counties.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/stream/dialectfolkloreo00steruoft/dialectfolkloreo00steruoft_djvu.txt" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Internet Archive Full text of &quot;The dialect and folk-lore of Northamptonshire</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Internet Archive Full text of &quot;The dialect and folk-lore of Northamptonshire</span></span></span>

That statement matters for how the Brington evidence should be weighed. Sternberg was not writing a modern archaeological survey, and he did not give a grid reference, landowner, witness list, or date for the first appearance of the ring. He was recording oral tradition: what people said about a place, what they believed could happen there, and what warnings or tests were attached to it. The Brington ring is therefore well-attested as a nineteenth-century folklore tradition, but not as a precisely locatable monument with continuous documentation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/stream/dialectfolkloreo00steruoft/dialectfolkloreo00steruoft_djvu.txt" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Internet Archive Full text of &quot;The dialect and folk-lore of Northamptonshire</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Internet Archive Full text of &quot;The dialect and folk-lore of Northamptonshire</span></span></span>

His wording also shows the layered nature of the material. For the fairy pool, he says he had been assured that a few years earlier the fairies “might be seen” there. For the ring, he reports what was believed and what village traditions related. This is careful enough to preserve distance: Sternberg does not ask the reader to accept the fairies as fact, but he does treat the beliefs as real parts of rural culture.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/stream/dialectfolkloreo00steruoft/dialectfolkloreo00steruoft_djvu.txt" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Internet Archive Full text of &quot;The dialect and folk-lore of Northamptonshire</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Internet Archive Full text of &quot;The dialect and folk-lore of Northamptonshire</span></span></span>

The book’s publication history also gives the material weight. The Internet Archive catalogue identifies *The Dialect and Folk-lore of Northamptonshire* as an 1851 work by Thomas Sternberg, published in London, with Northamptonshire folklore as one of its subject categories. Later bibliographic listings and folklore indexes continue to cite Sternberg as a source for English and Northamptonshire tradition, which is why Brington’s fairy pool and ring still circulate in modern retellings.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/details/dialectfolkloreo00steruoft" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on archive.org.</span></span></span>

Modern folklore websites have repeated the Brington material, often trying to connect Sternberg’s brief description with present-day maps or possible surviving landscape features. Such retellings are useful for keeping the tradition visible, but they should be treated as secondary unless they add fresh local evidence. The secure core remains Sternberg’s nineteenth-century account: a famous Brington ring, a Brington fairy pool, and a wider Northamptonshire belief that fairies gathered around water, wells and pasture circles.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.fairyist.com/fairy-places/midland-fairies/brington-fairy-pool-northamptonshire/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: fairyist.com">[Fairyist]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">fairyist.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Brington Fairy Pool (Northamptonshire</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Brington Fairy Pool (Northamptonshire</span></span></span>

## Why Brington matters in Northamptonshire folklore

Brington matters because it gives Northamptonshire fairy lore a named centre of gravity. Many counties have general talk of fairy rings, but Sternberg’s account singles out Brington’s ring as locally famous. That makes it one of the county’s most memorable fairy places: a site where the general British motif of the fairy ring is pinned to a particular Northamptonshire parish.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/stream/dialectfolkloreo00steruoft/dialectfolkloreo00steruoft_djvu.txt" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Internet Archive Full text of &quot;The dialect and folk-lore of Northamptonshire</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Internet Archive Full text of &quot;The dialect and folk-lore of Northamptonshire</span></span></span>

It also shows how modest landscapes can carry strong folklore. There is no need for a castle ruin, dramatic cliff, or ancient stone circle. The Brington material works through ordinary rural features: a pond, a well, pasture land, a dark ring in grass, a full moon. This is exactly the kind of place-based belief that can disappear if nobody writes it down, because it often lives in local speech rather than in formal records. Sternberg’s collection is valuable because it caught these small traditions before they became only fragments.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/stream/dialectfolkloreo00steruoft/dialectfolkloreo00steruoft_djvu.txt" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Internet Archive Full text of &quot;The dialect and folk-lore of Northamptonshire</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Internet Archive Full text of &quot;The dialect and folk-lore of Northamptonshire</span></span></span>

The stories also reveal the rules of fairy contact. The fairies are near, but not safely domestic. Their ring can be approached only through a ritualised act. Their dwelling is hidden below the ground. Their pool is a place of evening play, not daytime certainty. Their wells produce apprehension after nightfall. The tone is not horror, but caution: the countryside is shared with unseen neighbours, and certain places require respect.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/stream/dialectfolkloreo00steruoft/dialectfolkloreo00steruoft_djvu.txt" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Internet Archive Full text of &quot;The dialect and folk-lore of Northamptonshire</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Internet Archive Full text of &quot;The dialect and folk-lore of Northamptonshire</span></span></span>

Within the wider Northamptonshire branch, Brington connects naturally with other county traditions of hidden or uncanny places: haunted woods and avenues, wells with healing or dangerous associations, witchcraft stories, and seasonal rural customs. What makes Brington distinct is the concentration of fairy evidence. It is not merely “a place where fairies were once mentioned”, but the parish where Sternberg records both a named fairy pool and the county’s standout fairy ring.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/stream/dialectfolkloreo00steruoft/dialectfolkloreo00steruoft_djvu.txt" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Internet Archive Full text of &quot;The dialect and folk-lore of Northamptonshire</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Internet Archive Full text of &quot;The dialect and folk-lore of Northamptonshire</span></span></span>

## What can still be said today

The honest answer is that Brington’s fairy places survive best as recorded tradition, not as clearly signposted heritage attractions. The parish is real, the nineteenth-century source is real, and the motifs are clearly described. What is uncertain is the exact modern identification of the pool and ring. Later writers have speculated about possible locations, but Sternberg’s text itself does not provide enough detail to fix them confidently on a present-day map.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.fairyist.com/fairy-places/midland-fairies/brington-fairy-pool-northamptonshire/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: fairyist.com">[Fairyist]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">fairyist.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Brington Fairy Pool (Northamptonshire</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Brington Fairy Pool (Northamptonshire</span></span></span>

That uncertainty should not be treated as failure. Folklore often preserves relationships with landscape rather than survey data. The Brington traditions tell us that local people once understood certain rings, pools and wells as charged places. They tell us that pasture circles could be protected by fear of fairy punishment, that moonlit movement around a ring could be imagined as a way to hear hidden revelry, and that water places became more uncanny after dusk.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/stream/dialectfolkloreo00steruoft/dialectfolkloreo00steruoft_djvu.txt" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Internet Archive Full text of &quot;The dialect and folk-lore of Northamptonshire</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Internet Archive Full text of &quot;The dialect and folk-lore of Northamptonshire</span></span></span>

They also show why Northamptonshire’s folklore is best read through historic county identity rather than only modern administrative boundaries. For readers today, the value of the Brington fairy stories lies in that combination of specificity and mystery. The tradition is specific enough to name a parish, a pool, a ring, a moonlit test and a collector. It is mysterious because the exact sites have slipped back into the ordinary landscape. That is why Brington remains one of Northamptonshire’s strongest fairy places: not because it offers a neatly packaged attraction, but because it preserves the older sense that a field-edge or pond could be a doorway into hidden life.

    });
    section.setAttribute('data-ebay-selected-market', marketId);
    if (persist) {
      try { if (window.localStorage) localStorage.setItem('phoenix-ebay-market', marketId); } catch (err) {}
    }
    closeMenu(section);
  }
  sections.forEach(function (section) {
    if (section.getAttribute('data-ebay-localized-init') === '1') return;
    section.setAttribute('data-ebay-localized-init', '1');
    var available = availableMarkets(section);
    var marketId = defaultMarket;
    if (window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation && window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation.inferMarket) {
      marketId = window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation.inferMarket(available, {
        storageKey: 'phoenix-ebay-market',
        defaultMarket: defaultMarket
      });
    } else if (available.indexOf(defaultMarket)< 0) {
      marketId = available[0] || defaultMarket;
    }
    var select = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-select]');
    if (select) {
      select.addEventListener('change', function () {
        applyMarket(section, select.value, true);
      });
    }
    var trigger = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-trigger]');
    var menu = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-menu]');
    if (trigger && menu) {
      trigger.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
        event.stopPropagation();
        var expanded = trigger.getAttribute('aria-expanded') === 'true';
        menu.hidden = expanded;
        trigger.setAttribute('aria-expanded', expanded ? 'false' : 'true');
        var picker = trigger.closest('.fr-ebay-market-picker');
        if (picker) picker.classList.toggle('fr-ebay-market-picker--open', !expanded);
      });
      Array.prototype.slice.call(menu.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-option]')).forEach(function (button) {
        button.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
          event.stopPropagation();
          applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-option'), true);
        });
      });
      document.addEventListener('click', function () { closeMenu(section); });
    }
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      button.addEventListener('click', function () {
        applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button'), true);
      });
    });
    applyMarket(section, marketId, false);
  });
})();
