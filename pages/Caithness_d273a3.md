## Where Caithness folklore belongs on the map

For this project, Caithness is best treated as the historic county at the north-eastern tip of mainland Scotland. Wikishire describes it as bounded by the sea on two sides and by Sutherland on the landward side, while the Gazetteer of British Place Names calls it the northernmost county of mainland Great Britain. That matters for folklore because many stories sit exactly on the county’s margins: the Pentland Firth links Caithness to Orkney; the east coast links it to North Sea routes; and the southern boundary towards Sutherland shares prehistoric and Gaelic-Norse cultural patterns.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://wikishire.co.uk/wiki/Caithness" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wikishire.co.uk">[Wikishire]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wikishire.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wikishire.co.uk.</span></span></span>



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/Caithness_d273a3-overview.webp" | relative_url }}" alt="Overview image for Where Caithness Folklore Meets the Edge" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

Modern administration can blur this older identity. Caithness is now within the Highland council area, but its older county geography remains useful for folklore because stories were often collected by parish, estate, coastline, ferry route or historic county rather than by modern council boundary. Caithness also differs in texture from the mountain Highlands imagined by many visitors: it is a county of open farming land, moor, blanket bog and dramatic coast, rather than a landscape dominated by high peaks.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://gazetteer.org.uk/search?place=Caithness&amp;type=em" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gazetteer.org.uk">[Gazetteer]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gazetteer.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on gazetteer.org.uk.</span></span></span>

That openness helps explain the folklore. Caithness legends often feel exposed rather than enclosed: the mermaid is seen in the bay, the seal-fairy is met near the shore, the fairy music comes from a broch mound, and the [ancient stones]({{ 'ancient-stones-64e71d/' | relative_url }}) stand low on a hillside under a wide sky.

## Sea stories: mermaids, seals and the dangerous shore

The most famous Caithness supernatural tradition is probably the mermaid material from Reay and Dunnet. In 1809, Elizabeth Mackay of Reay reported seeing a mermaid-like creature in the sea; Katie Garner’s peer-reviewed study notes that the account caused a national sensation when it appeared in the press and later influenced literary responses by Christian Isobel Johnstone, Thomas Love Peacock and Walter Scott. Garner treats the account carefully: not as proof of a mermaid, but as a coastal encounter that sat between folklore, natural history, women’s testimony and the imaginative power of the northern shoreline.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://research-repository.st-andrews.ac.uk/handle/10023/29522" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: research-repository.st-andrews.ac.uk">[St Andrews Research Repository]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">research-repository.st-andrews.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on st-andrews.ac.uk.</span></span></span>

The Reay mermaid was not simply a throwaway curiosity. Simon Young’s study of the “Reay mermaids” argues that the sightings were debated in newspapers and among educated readers, and that belief in a possible unknown marine creature remained more socially acceptable in the early nineteenth century than belief in fairies or ghosts. The story later came to be regarded by many as a hoax, but it continued to circulate locally and nationally.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.shimajournal.org/issues/v12n2/e.-Young-Shima-v12n2.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: shimajournal.org">[Shima Journal]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">shimajournal.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">e. Young Shima v12n2</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">e. Young Shima v12n2</span></span></span>

James T. Calder’s *Sketch of the Civil and Traditional History of Caithness*, first published in 1861 and later reissued with notes by Thomas Sinclair, preserved another mermaid tale attached to the coast between Murkle, Castlehill and Dwarwick Head. In Calder’s version, a young man meets a mermaid, receives riches from her, becomes greedy, and is finally taken to a cave at Dwarwick Head where he is bound by a chain of gold. The tale is typical of mermaid folklore in one sense — a sea-woman gives gifts, then punishes betrayal — but it is strongly localised by its Caithness setting: Dunnet sands, the Pentland Firth and the cave in the headland.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/stream/sketchcivilandt00sincgoog/sketchcivilandt00sincgoog_djvu.txt" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on archive.org.</span></span></span>

Caithness also belongs to the wider northern tradition of seal-people. “The Seal-Catcher’s Adventure”, printed from William Grant Stewart’s Highland folklore and later included in George Douglas’s *Scottish Fairy and Folk Tales*, is set near John o’ Groats. It tells of a seal-killer taken beneath the sea, changed into seal form, and made to heal the seal he had wounded before swearing never to kill seals again. The tale calls seals “downright fairies”, which shows how close the boundary could be between animal lore, fairy belief and moral warning.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://sacred-texts.com/neu/celt/sfft/sfft58.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sacred-texts.com">[Internet Sacred Text Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sacred-texts.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on sacred-texts.com.</span></span></span>

What makes these Caithness sea stories memorable is that they are not just romantic inventions about mermaids. They speak to a real coastal economy of fishing, seal-hunting, wreck, weather and risk. The supernatural figure often acts as the sea’s moral voice: rewarding, warning, luring or punishing humans who treat the shore only as a source of profit.

## Fairy hills and brochs: when archaeology becomes story

Caithness is one of Britain’s great broch landscapes. The Highland Archaeological Research Framework notes that Caithness has the largest density of known brochs in the Highlands, though many were excavated in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries under methods that now make interpretation difficult. Modern projects by groups such as Caithness Archaeological Trust and AOC Archaeology have helped re-examine some of these sites.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://scarf.scot/regional/higharf/highland-archaeological-research-framework-case-studies/nybster-broch/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: scarf.scot">[Scarf]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">scarf.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-title">nybster broch</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">nybster broch</span></span></span>

Folklore turns these Iron Age ruins into something more than archaeological remains. Bruan Broch, in Latheron parish, is remembered in modern retellings as the “fairy mound of Bruan”. A version recorded by the Rev. George Sutherland in 1937 tells of two men carrying whisky at Hogmanay who hear music from the broch, find fairies dancing inside, and discover the dangerous time-slip quality familiar from fairy-hill stories.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.themodernantiquarian.com/site/14070/bruan" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: themodernantiquarian.com">[The Modern Antiquarian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">themodernantiquarian.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on themodernantiquarian.com.</span></span></span>

The Caithness Broch Project has embraced this overlap between ancient buildings and storytelling. Its own writing on “brochs and stories” reflects on how prehistoric places continue to inspire new tales, illustrations and public imagination. That does not make every fairy tale ancient, but it does show how modern Caithness heritage work keeps folklore alive as part of how people approach ruins.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.thebrochproject.co.uk/blog/on-brochs-and-stories" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: thebrochproject.co.uk">[Caithness Broch Project]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">thebrochproject.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">on brochs and stories</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">on brochs and stories</span></span></span>

The useful distinction is this: the brochs themselves are archaeological monuments, not proven “fairy sites” in any literal sense; the fairy stories are later traditional or semi-traditional narratives attached to those monuments. For readers, that distinction makes the stories more interesting, not less. It shows how people explained strange old buildings before modern excavation and how a ruin could become a doorway into the otherworld.

## Stones, cairns and the older sacred landscape

Caithness has prehistoric monuments that invite legend even when no single famous folktale is securely attached to them. The Hill o’ Many Stanes, near Mid Clyth, consists of about 200 small stones arranged in rows down a low hill. Historic Environment Scotland dates the monument to about 4,000 years ago and suggests possible uses including gatherings, religious ceremonies, astronomy or the organisation of the farming cycle. It also stresses that the monument’s purpose remains unknown.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.historicenvironment.scot/visit/all/hill-omany-stanes/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: historicenvironment.scot">[Historic Environment Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">historicenvironment.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on historicenvironment.scot.</span></span></span>

That uncertainty is exactly why such places become folklore magnets. The Hill o’ Many Stanes is not a towering stone circle; its stones are low, numerous and puzzling. The mystery lies in pattern rather than size. Modern visitors often read it as a place of ancient knowledge, while archaeologists are more cautious: the site was carefully planned, but its exact meaning cannot be recovered with confidence.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.historicenvironment.scot/visit/all/hill-omany-stanes/history-and-stories/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: historicenvironment.scot">[Historic Environment Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">historicenvironment.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on historicenvironment.scot.</span></span></span>

The Grey Cairns of Camster offer another kind of atmosphere. Historic Environment Scotland describes them as a pair of Neolithic tombs more than 5,000 years old, with human remains found during nineteenth-century excavation. Their modern appeal is partly archaeological and partly imaginative: they are old enough, remote enough and physically accessible enough to feel like thresholds into a different world.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.historicenvironment.scot/visit/all/grey-cairns-of-camster/history-and-stories/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: historicenvironment.scot">[Historic Environment Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">historicenvironment.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on historicenvironment.scot.</span></span></span>

For a folklore page, the key point is not to invent legends where evidence is thin. Caithness’s prehistoric sites matter because they show the kind of landscape that later legend feeds on: rows with no agreed explanation, tombs on open moorland, brochs with hollow interiors, and coastal towers whose original purposes were half-forgotten long before modern heritage interpretation arrived.

## Witches, fairies and second sight

Caithness has a place in Scottish witchcraft history, but the evidence needs careful handling. Witch-trial records are not neutral windows into belief: they were produced under pressure, fear, interrogation and law. Even so, they preserve fragments of popular ideas about fairies, charms, healing and second sight.

The strongest Caithness-linked example is Elspeth Reoch, executed in Kirkwall in 1616. The Survey of Scottish Witchcraft records her case, including healing, clairvoyance and magical claims. Later summaries note that she was born in Caithness, spent time in Lochaber and Orkney, and told a story involving fairy-like figures, second sight and charm-working.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://witches.hca.ed.ac.uk/case/C/EGD/2212" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: witches.hca.ed.ac.uk">[witches.hca.ed.ac.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">witches.hca.ed.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on ed.ac.uk.</span></span></span>

Reoch’s story matters because it sits at the meeting point of fairy belief and witch prosecution. In Scottish witchcraft studies, fairy encounters could be reinterpreted by courts as dealings with the Devil. The Survey of Scottish Witchcraft is based on legal and church records from 1563 to 1736, and its source guide includes Caithness presbytery records among the materials used for the wider database.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://witches.hca.ed.ac.uk/sources/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: witches.hca.ed.ac.uk">[witches.hca.ed.ac.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">witches.hca.ed.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on ed.ac.uk.</span></span></span>

Caithness folklore should therefore avoid the easy phrase “real witch”. A better reading is that people accused of witchcraft could be healers, wanderers, marginal people, charmers, neighbours in conflict, or storytellers whose claims were recast in dangerous legal language. Reoch’s Caithness connection also links the county to a broader northern world of Orkney, sea routes and fairy traditions, rather than to a neat county-bound legend.

## Saints, chapels and ritual custom

Caithness supernatural tradition is not only about fairies and sea creatures. Christian holy places and seasonal customs also shaped local belief. One striking example is St Tears Chapel in the parish of Wick. R. C. Maclagan’s *Folklore of Scottish Lochs and Springs* records that the chapel was visited on Childermas, 28 December, by people who left bread and cheese as offerings to the souls of the Holy Innocents. The practice reportedly continued until about the beginning of the nineteenth century.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/files/56034/56034-h/56034-h.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on gutenberg.org.</span></span></span>

This is an important kind of folklore because it is not a dramatic legend but a repeated act. Offerings of food at a chapel show how older devotional practice could survive locally as custom, even when official religious culture had changed. It also connects Caithness to wider traditions of holy wells, saintly sites and votive offerings across Scotland.

St Triduana gives another saintly connection. Her main well-known shrine is at Restalrig in Edinburgh, where Historic Environment Scotland describes a holy spring associated with cures for eye ailments and a fifteenth-century chapel and wellhouse. Other dedications to Triduana include Ballachly in Caithness, showing how saintly geography could stretch between Caithness, Orkney and the south of Scotland.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.historicenvironment.scot/visit/all/st-triduanas-chapel-restalrig-collegiate-church/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: historicenvironment.scot">[Historic Environment Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">historicenvironment.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on historicenvironment.scot.</span></span></span>

Calder also noted Caithness funeral customs, including a white flag carried before a funeral and a Thurso practice in which a kirk officer went through the streets with small bells to invite people to attend. These customs are not “supernatural” in the narrow sense, but they belong to the same belief culture: public ritual, death, sound, procession and the community’s duty to the dead.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/stream/sketchcivilandt00sincgoog/sketchcivilandt00sincgoog_djvu.txt" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on archive.org.</span></span></span>

## Old sources and modern retellings

Caithness folklore survives through a patchwork of sources rather than one definitive county collection. James T. Calder is central because his nineteenth-century history preserved traditional material, including mermaid stories, local customs and antiquarian notes. The *Dictionary of National Biography* entry for Calder records that he was born in Castletown, became parish teacher at Canisbay, published *Sketches from John o’ Groat’s* in 1842 with a chapter on “Ancient Superstitions and Customs in Caithness”, and later published his history of the county.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Dictionary_of_National_Biography%2C_1885-1900/Calder%2C_James_Tait" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: en.wikisource.org">[Wikisource]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">en.wikisource.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Calder, James Tait</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Calder, James Tait</span></span></span>

There was an attempt to bring Caithness into the Folklore Society’s county folklore programme, but the planned volume never emerged. A study of the County Folklore series notes that work on a Caithness volume was put forward around 1898, while later research into the series states that a Caithness volume was being worked on but “never emerged”. This helps explain why Caithness feels rich in examples yet scattered in documentation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.jstor.org/stable/43297711" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: jstor.org">[JSTOR]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">jstor.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on jstor.org.</span></span></span>

Modern retellings now do some of the work that a county collection never did. Folklore Scotland retells the Fairies of Bruan Broch; The Faerie Folk podcast has retold the Mermaid of Dunnet Bay and the Seal-Catcher’s Adventure; local tourism sites present selkies, brochs and coastal legends for visitors. These sources are useful for seeing what is alive in public memory today, but they should be read alongside older texts and institutional records rather than replacing them.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://folklorescotland.com/the-fairies-of-bruan-broch/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: folklorescotland.com">[folklorescotland.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">folklorescotland.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on folklorescotland.com.</span></span></span>

The best modern Caithness folklore writing therefore has to balance charm and caution. Some stories are old printed traditions; some are antiquarian summaries; some are modern heritage storytelling; some are interpretations of archaeology; and some are claims once debated as possible natural history. Keeping those layers visible makes the county’s folklore stronger, because it shows how stories are made, preserved and remade.

## Why Caithness folklore still matters

Caithness folklore matters because it gives the county a cultural shape as distinctive as its geography. The legends are not random decorations added to a northern landscape; they are ways of thinking about that landscape. The sea is generous and dangerous. Seals are animals, livelihoods and possible fairy folk. Brochs are ruins and fairy mounds. Stone rows are archaeology and mystery. Chapels are Christian sites and places of offering. Witch-trial records are legal documents and damaged traces of popular belief.

The county’s folklore also resists a simple Highland stereotype. Caithness looks north and east as well as south and west. Its stories share motifs with Orkney, Shetland, Sutherland and the wider Scottish mainland, but they keep local anchors: Reay Manse, Dunnet Bay, Dwarwick Head, John o’ Groats, Bruan, Wick and the Latheron coast. That is why Caithness works well as a historic-county folklore page: it is both connected and particular.

The most honest summary is that Caithness is not a county with one dominant legend, but a county of thresholds. Its folklore lives where categories blur: animal and human, sea and land, ruin and fairy hill, saint and spring, custom and belief, archaeology and imagination. Those blurred edges are exactly what make the far north of mainland Scotland so memorable.
