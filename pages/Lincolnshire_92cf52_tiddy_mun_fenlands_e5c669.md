## Introduction

Tiddy Mun is [Lincolnshire]({{ 'what-haunts-lincolnshires-fields-fens/' | relative_url }})’s great fen spirit: a small, grey, white-bearded being of the carrs, marshes and misty water-holes, best remembered through a tale in which drainage angers the old wetland power and brings sickness, bad harvests, animal disease and collapsing homes. The story matters because it turns a real historical upheaval — the draining and remaking of Lincolnshire’s wet places — into a ritual drama of apology. People did not simply defeat the water; in the tale, they had to ask forgiveness from the spirit of the water.



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/Lincolnshire_92cf52_tiddy_mun_fenlands_e5c669-overview.webp" | relative_url }}" alt="Overview image for Tiddy Mun" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

The tradition is most strongly attached to the Lincolnshire Carrs, especially the Ancholme Valley in north Lincolnshire, although later retellings often broaden him into a spirit of the wider Fens. Its key printed source is Marie Clothilde Balfour’s “Legends of the Lincolnshire Cars”, published in *Folk-Lore* in 1891, and later discussed by folklorists and local researchers as both a strange fairy tale and a memory of environmental change. Balfour’s text presents Tiddy Mun not as a simple monster, but as a being whose anger explains what happens when a wetland world is suddenly made dry.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cantab.net/users/michael.behrend/repubs/balfour_lincs_cars/pages/index.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cantab.net">[Alumni]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cantab.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Alumni Base &lt; Balfour, Legends of the Cars</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Alumni Base &lt; Balfour, Legends of the Cars</span></span></span>

## The spirit of the carrs

Tiddy Mun belongs to the carr country: low, wet ground, scrubby woodland, bog-holes, ditches, mist, grazing and flood. In Balfour’s version, he lives deep in still green water and comes out in the evening as the mist rises. He is no bigger than a three-year-old child, yet he looks ancient, with long white hair and a tangled white beard. His grey clothing makes him hard to distinguish from the dusk, and his laugh is compared with the cry of the peewit, or lapwing.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Folk-Lore/Volume_2/Legends_of_the_Lincolnshire_Cars%2C_Part_1" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: en.wikisource.org">[Wikisource]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">en.wikisource.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Folk-Lore/Volume 2/Legends of the Lincolnshire Cars, Part 1</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Folk-Lore/Volume 2/Legends of the Lincolnshire Cars, Part 1</span></span></span>

That description is important because it makes him part of the landscape rather than a creature merely placed inside it. His colour is mist-grey, his sound is a wetland bird, his movement is associated with wind and running water, and his home is the bog-hole. He is frightening, but not wholly wicked. Before the drainage crisis in the tale, families call to him when floodwater threatens the tracks and doorsteps; after hearing his peewit-like cry, they find the water gone down by morning.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Folk-Lore/Volume_2/Legends_of_the_Lincolnshire_Cars%2C_Part_1" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: en.wikisource.org">[Wikisource]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">en.wikisource.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Folk-Lore/Volume 2/Legends of the Lincolnshire Cars, Part 1</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Folk-Lore/Volume 2/Legends of the Lincolnshire Cars, Part 1</span></span></span>

This makes Tiddy Mun different from many “goblin” or “boggart” figures in English folklore. He can frighten people, punish them and ruin their luck, but his power is also protective. The old arrangement is reciprocal: the carr-folk live with wet ground, fear its dangers, and appeal to the being who seems to command it. The story therefore begins with a working relationship between people and wetland, not with a simple fight between civilisation and wilderness.

The name itself is part of the local feel. Modern summaries usually explain “Tiddy Mun” as “small man”, and Balfour’s tale stresses that he is “wi’out a name” — not a grand classical god, but a nameless local power addressed through dialect and habit. The Lincolnshire Folk Tales Project describes him as a white-bearded, toddler-sized boggart of the marshy Carrs of the Ancholme Valley, and places the story within a wider cluster of “tiddy people” traditions in which small beings help crops, receive offerings and belong to the moral life of the land.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://lincolnshirefolktalesproject.com/2023/09/05/hello-world/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: lincolnshirefolktalesproject.com">[Lincolnshire Folk Tales Project]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">lincolnshirefolktalesproject.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Lincolnshire Folk Tales Project Tiddy Mun</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Lincolnshire Folk Tales Project Tiddy Mun</span></span></span>

## Drainage, sickness and resentment

The plot turns when outsiders begin to drain the carrs. In Balfour’s 1891 telling, the “Dutchies” come across the sea to dig, promising that mists will lift, bogs will become dry fields and there will be no more ague. The carr-folk dislike the change, refuse hospitality to the workers, and fear that drying the bog-holes will “unhap” Tiddy Mun. As the dykes grow longer and deeper, the black soft bog-land is transformed into enclosed green fields.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Folk-Lore/Volume_2/Legends_of_the_Lincolnshire_Cars%2C_Part_1" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: en.wikisource.org">[Wikisource]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">en.wikisource.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Folk-Lore/Volume 2/Legends of the Lincolnshire Cars, Part 1</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Folk-Lore/Volume 2/Legends of the Lincolnshire Cars, Part 1</span></span></span>

This is folklore, not a neutral engineering report, but it sits beside a real history of major drainage and reclamation in eastern England. Dutch engineers and investors were involved in seventeenth-century drainage schemes, including the work associated with Sir Cornelius Vermuyden at Hatfield Chase on the Lincolnshire-Yorkshire border from the 1620s. Historical research on Hatfield Chase shows that such schemes were bound up with capital, land rights, enclosure, commoners’ resistance, litigation and conflict, not merely with technical improvement.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.bahs.org.uk/AGHR/ARTICLES/64_1_vanCruyningen.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: bahs.org.uk">[bahs.org.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">bahs.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">64 1 van Cruyningen</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">64 1 van Cruyningen</span></span></span>

The Ancholme Valley also underwent major drainage and river modification. Local history sources note a Royal Commission to drain the Ancholme Level in 1635, followed by embankments, new channels, sluices and drains in the 1630s–1650s. Natural England’s landscape profile describes the Ancholme as a low-lying vale near sea level in places, with heavily modified watercourses and a straight, embanked channel cut in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries beside remnants of the old river.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://e-voice.org.uk/bonbyhistory/ancholme-valley/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: e-voice.org.uk">[E-Voice]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">e-voice.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">E-Voice Ancholme Valley</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">E-Voice Ancholme Valley</span></span></span>

In the tale, the cost of drainage appears as a curse. Dutch workers vanish. Cows pine, pigs starve, ponies go lame, lambs waste away, milk curdles, thatch falls in, walls burst out, children sicken and harvests fail. The carr-folk first blame witches and other uncanny beings, but eventually recognise that Tiddy Mun himself is angered because his watery home has been emptied.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Folk-Lore/Volume_2/Legends_of_the_Lincolnshire_Cars%2C_Part_1" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: en.wikisource.org">[Wikisource]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">en.wikisource.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Folk-Lore/Volume 2/Legends of the Lincolnshire Cars, Part 1</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Folk-Lore/Volume 2/Legends of the Lincolnshire Cars, Part 1</span></span></span>

The striking point is that much of the curse sounds like a folk explanation of ecological and social disruption. Darwin Horn’s 1987 article, “Tiddy Mun’s Curse and the Ecological Consequences of Land Reclamation”, is repeatedly cited in later discussions for arguing that the misfortunes in the story can be read alongside real consequences of sudden landscape change. South Holland Heritage summarises that interpretation: altered grazing and feed could affect animals, a lowered water table could contribute to subsidence and cracking walls, hardened ground could lame animals used to wet pasture, and disease could plausibly worsen amid disturbed wetland conditions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.jstor.org/stable/1259396" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: jstor.org">[JSTOR]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">jstor.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on jstor.org.</span></span></span>

That does not mean every detail is literal history. Curdled milk, vanishing workers and a spirit’s anger are fairy-tale language. But the story’s emotional logic is historically credible: drainage promised health, productivity and profit, while also threatening common rights, familiar livelihoods, wetland ecology and older ways of understanding place. Tiddy Mun gives that upheaval a face.

## Offerings, apologies and modern retellings

The most memorable part of the Tiddy Mun tradition is not the curse but the apology. In Balfour’s version, someone remembers that people once called on him at the new moon when the waters rose. The carr-folk decide to call again, not to command him, but to show that they wish him well and would give the water back if they could. Later retellings describe them going to the dykes, pouring in water and asking him to take the spell away.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Folk-Lore/Volume_2/Legends_of_the_Lincolnshire_Cars%2C_Part_1" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: en.wikisource.org">[Wikisource]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">en.wikisource.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Folk-Lore/Volume 2/Legends of the Lincolnshire Cars, Part 1</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Folk-Lore/Volume 2/Legends of the Lincolnshire Cars, Part 1</span></span></span>

This is a small ritual, but it carries the whole meaning of the legend. The people cannot undo the drainage. They cannot restore the old bog-holes by themselves. What they can do is acknowledge harm. They bring water back symbolically, address the spirit by his humble name, and admit that the new order has injured the old wetland world.

The chant varies in retelling, but its structure is consistent: Tiddy Mun is called as the nameless small man, water is offered, and the bad spell is asked to end. The Lincolnshire Folk Tales Project says that in the tale the ritual works, bringing a return of thriving in the carrs, and that the practice is said to continue for a long time. South Holland Heritage gives a modern prose version in which offerings of fresh water maintain a truce until Tiddy Mun is thought to vanish with the fens themselves.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://lincolnshirefolktalesproject.com/2023/09/05/hello-world/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: lincolnshirefolktalesproject.com">[Lincolnshire Folk Tales Project]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">lincolnshirefolktalesproject.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Lincolnshire Folk Tales Project Tiddy Mun</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Lincolnshire Folk Tales Project Tiddy Mun</span></span></span>

For modern readers, that apology is why Tiddy Mun feels unusually powerful. He is not only a picturesque “bog sprite”. He is a mechanism for thinking about environmental debt. The story asks what a community owes to a landscape after it has been improved, damaged, enclosed or made profitable. In that sense, Tiddy Mun belongs naturally beside other Lincolnshire traditions tied to place and change, but he is especially suited to the fenland imagination because his power is water-level, mist, sickness and memory.

The tale also survives because it has been repeatedly retold. Balfour’s 1891 “Legends of the Lincolnshire Cars” became the seed for later adaptations by writers and storytellers, including Kevin Crossley-Holland and others listed by Maureen James’s Telling History work. The Lincolnshire Folk Tales Project notes versions in modern Lincolnshire and fenland collections, including Maureen James’s *Lincolnshire Folk Tales*, Margaret Read MacDonald’s *Earth Care*, Polly Howat’s *Ghosts and Legends of Lincolnshire and the Fen Country*, Kevin Crossley-Holland’s *The Old Stories*, and Adrian Gray’s *Tales of Old Lincolnshire*.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://tellinghistory.co.uk/content/stories-0" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: tellinghistory.co.uk">[Telling History]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">tellinghistory.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">stories 0</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">stories 0</span></span></span>

There is also a live modern heritage afterlife. The upgraded A47 bridge at Guyhirn was named Tiddy Mun Bridge in 2022 after a local naming competition, showing how a figure once attached to marsh fear and folk apology can become a public symbol of fenland identity. That bridge is outside the tight Ancholme Valley setting of Balfour’s tale, but the choice makes sense within the wider Fenland world: Tiddy Mun has become a shorthand for the old relationship between people, roads, water and engineered land.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.saferhighways.co.uk/post/a47-guyhirn-junction-officially-opened" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: saferhighways.co.uk">[Insight]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">saferhighways.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Insight A47 Guyhirn junction officially opened</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Insight A47 Guyhirn junction officially opened</span></span></span>

## How old is the tradition?

The safest answer is that Tiddy Mun is securely documented from 1891, but the tale claims older oral roots. Balfour published “Legends of the Lincolnshire Cars” in *Folk-Lore* in three parts in 1891, presenting the material as collected from local people in the Lincolnshire Carrs. A modern republication notes that she collected ten folktales from the wetlands of north Lincolnshire between Lincoln Edge and the Wolds, while also warning that she sometimes wrote in a dialect coloured by her Scottish background.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cantab.net/users/michael.behrend/repubs/balfour_lincs_cars/pages/index.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cantab.net">[Alumni]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cantab.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Alumni Base &lt; Balfour, Legends of the Cars</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Alumni Base &lt; Balfour, Legends of the Cars</span></span></span>

That caution matters. Balfour was a skilled literary storyteller, and the “Legends of the Cars” are unusually vivid. Their authenticity has therefore been questioned by some later scholars and defended by others. Maureen James’s research argues that the tales’ folkloric content and dialect are predominantly from North Lincolnshire and South Yorkshire, and that their oral features — framing, asides, repetition, poetic devices and simpler sentence patterns — point away from pure literary invention. James concludes that, even if Balfour shaped the telling, the stories are strongly linked to the Carrs and were probably composed within that world sometime between the seventeenth and mid-nineteenth century.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://tellinghistory.co.uk/content/legends-carrs" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: tellinghistory.co.uk">[Telling History]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">tellinghistory.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Telling History Legends of the Carrs &#124; Telling History</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Telling History Legends of the Carrs &#124; Telling History</span></span></span>

This makes Tiddy Mun a good example of how folklore evidence often works. We do not have a medieval manuscript proving continuous belief in a named fen spirit. We have a late-Victorian collector’s text, later scholarly debate, strong local landscape fit, and a tale whose internal concerns match known drainage conflicts and environmental changes. The tradition is therefore well-attested as a nineteenth-century collected tale, plausible as an older oral memory, but not provable as an ancient cult or direct survival of pre-Christian worship.

It is also important not to flatten Tiddy Mun into a generic fairy. E. H. Rudkin’s later work on Lincolnshire folklore recorded “Tiddy people” or “Strangers” in the Ancholme Valley: small, mischievous but often helpful beings associated with offerings, crops and stones. That wider pattern helps explain why Tiddy Mun feels at home in Lincolnshire fairy lore, but the drainage curse remains his distinctive story.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.jstor.org/stable/1258804" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: jstor.org">[JSTOR]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">jstor.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on jstor.org.</span></span></span>

## Why Tiddy Mun matters to Lincolnshire

Tiddy Mun matters because he makes Lincolnshire’s drained landscape readable as folklore. Much of the county’s lowland identity is shaped by water management: dykes, straightened rivers, embanked channels, pumping, enclosure, flat fields and the uneasy knowledge that useful land has been made by controlling what once flooded. Natural England’s profile of the Central Lincolnshire Vale still describes a landscape of modified watercourses, regular drained fields, ditches, peaty soils in places, and continuing water-quality and floodplain questions around the Ancholme.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.n-kesteven.gov.uk/sites/default/files/2023-03/ENV004b%20Natural%20England%20Landscape%20Character%20Area%20Central%20Lincs%20Vale.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: n-kesteven.gov.uk">[North Kesteven District Council]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">n-kesteven.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">North Kesteven District Council</span></span></span>

The tale gives that engineered landscape a memory of what came before. The carrs are not presented as empty wasteland waiting to become proper farmland. They are inhabited, worked, feared, known and spiritually charged. Drainage brings benefits, but also loss. That balance is why the story still feels modern: it refuses both simple nostalgia and simple progress.

For a Lincolnshire folklore map, Tiddy Mun is a central figure because he links several themes at once:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **Fairy tradition:** he belongs to the county’s small-being and boggart lore, but with a stronger ecological role than many household or field spirits.
* **Wetland history:** his anger is tied directly to drainage, dykes, water-holes, carrs and the remaking of fenland.
* **Local memory:** the tale encodes resentment towards outsiders, anxiety over common land and fear of sudden change.
* **Ritual behaviour:** the offering of water at the new moon turns apology into a repeated communal act.
* **Modern retelling:** he survives in books, storytelling projects, heritage writing and even road naming.

</div>

Tiddy Mun’s power as folklore lies in that mixture. He is small enough to be called “tiddy”, but large enough to stand for a whole wetland world. He is frightening enough to curse a community, but familiar enough to be asked for help. Above all, he makes the drained fenlands feel haunted not by a random ghost, but by the memory of water itself.
