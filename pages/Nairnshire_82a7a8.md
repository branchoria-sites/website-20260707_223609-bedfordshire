## Introduction

As a historic county, Nairnshire sits at the head of the Moray Firth, bounded by Inverness-shire to the west and south and Morayshire to the east; Scotland’s People describes Nairn county as north-eastern Scotland, also known as Nairnshire, while noting that counties as local government areas were abolished in 1975.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://wikishire.co.uk/wiki/Nairnshire" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wikishire.co.uk">[Wikishire]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wikishire.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Wikishire Nairnshire</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wikishire Nairnshire</span></span></span><span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.scotlandspeople.gov.uk/content/nairn-county" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: scotlandspeople.gov.uk">[Scotland&#x27;s People]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">scotlandspeople.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Scotland&#x27;s People Nairn county &#124; Scotland&#x27;s People</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Scotland&#x27;s People Nairn county &#124; Scotland&#x27;s People</span></span></span> For folklore, that historic frame matters: the stories follow older parishes, fishing communities, estates, burns, dunes and castle roads more naturally than modern council boundaries.

## Where Nairnshire Folklore Lives

Nairnshire is one of Scotland’s smaller counties, but its folklore has a strong sense of terrain. The coast gives the county its fishing customs and its most dramatic vanished-land story. Inland, Auldearn, [Cawdor]({{ 'cawdor/' | relative_url }}), Rait and the River Nairn valley hold witchcraft records, castle legends, old religious sites and prehistoric monuments. Wikishire’s county profile places Nairnshire’s northern edge on the Moray Firth and describes a landscape of low dunes and fertile coastal plain rising southwards into hill country; it also notes the Findhorn and Nairn as the county’s main rivers.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://wikishire.co.uk/wiki/Nairnshire" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wikishire.co.uk">[Wikishire]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wikishire.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Wikishire Nairnshire</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wikishire Nairnshire</span></span></span>

The historic county also had boundary oddities, including detached parts, and surrounding parishes sometimes straddled neighbouring counties. That is important for readers following folklore on the ground: a story may be “Nairnshire” by old county identity, “Highland” by modern administration, and part of a wider Moray Firth or Strathnairn tradition by cultural geography. The county centre of gravity, however, remains clear: Nairn town, Auldearn, Cawdor, Ardclach, Rait and the coast between the Nairn and Findhorn worlds.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://wikishire.co.uk/wiki/Nairnshire" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wikishire.co.uk">[Wikishire]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wikishire.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Wikishire Nairnshire</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wikishire Nairnshire</span></span></span>

This is not a county with a neat list of dragons, giants and black dogs attached to every parish. Its best folklore is more local and documentary: witch trial testimony, museum-preserved oral stories, castle ghost traditions, seasonal fishing customs and legends explaining visible landscape changes. That makes Nairnshire especially useful for showing the difference between old belief, legal record, antiquarian retelling, tourism legend and modern heritage interpretation.

## Isobel Gowdie and the Fairy-Witch World of Auldearn

The best-known folklore figure in Nairnshire is Isobel Gowdie of Auldearn, often treated as one of Scotland’s most remarkable accused witches. Her case matters because it preserves a rare, highly detailed 17th-century account of witchcraft belief in which demonic, fairy and folk-healing ideas sit side by side. The Spirit of the Highlands and Islands archive, using material gifted by Nairn Museum, states that at least seven witches were tried in the parish of Auldearn, and that Isobel Gowdie was among them; it also cites the Survey of Scottish Witchcraft estimate of at least 3,873 people accused of witchcraft in Scotland between 1563 and 1737.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://discoverhighlandsandislands.scot/en/story/isobel-gowdie-witch-of-auldearn" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: discoverhighlandsandislands.scot">[Discover Highlands and Islands]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">discoverhighlandsandislands.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Discover Highlands and Islands Isobel Gowdie</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Discover Highlands and Islands Isobel Gowdie</span></span></span>

Gowdie’s confessions were recorded in April 1662 before local ministers, lairds and church elders. The material is extraordinary because it does not just say “witchcraft happened”; it gives a whole imagined world of midnight meetings, charms, image magic, shape-shifting, flight and fairy-land. She claimed to turn into a jackdaw, cat and hare, to meet with a coven at the old kirk of Auldearn, and to use clay figures against neighbours, including Harry Forbes, the minister of Auldearn.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://discoverhighlandsandislands.scot/en/story/isobel-gowdie-witch-of-auldearn" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: discoverhighlandsandislands.scot">[Discover Highlands and Islands]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">discoverhighlandsandislands.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Discover Highlands and Islands Isobel Gowdie</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Discover Highlands and Islands Isobel Gowdie</span></span></span>

The fairy element is one of the reasons Gowdie remains so important to folklore. In her account, she travelled to Elfane, a fairy realm, where she saw elf-boys making arrowheads that could be used to harm people. The University of Edinburgh’s Survey of Scottish Witchcraft case entry records details including a Fairy Queen dressed in white and brown and a Fairy King described as a well-favoured man with a broad face.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://witches.hca.ed.ac.uk/case/C/EGD/1558" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: witches.hca.ed.ac.uk">[Scottish Witchcraft Survey]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">witches.hca.ed.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on ed.ac.uk.</span></span></span> This is not proof that such beings existed; it is evidence that fairy belief could be woven into the language of witchcraft accusation and confession in 17th-century Nairnshire.

There are two traps to avoid. The first is treating Gowdie as a Halloween caricature. She was a real woman caught in a dangerous legal and religious culture. The second is treating every word of the confession as straightforward oral folklore. These records were produced under pressure, in a witch-trial setting, and filtered through clerks, ministers and interrogators. Yet the richness of the testimony still gives Nairnshire one of the most powerful windows into Scottish fairy-witch belief: charms, healing, fear of crop destruction, hare transformation, elf-shot, midnight assemblies and the moral anxieties of a small parish world.

Gowdie’s afterlife is also part of Nairnshire’s modern folklore. She has inspired writers, historians, local drama and music, including James MacMillan’s 1990 orchestral work *The Confession of Isobel Gowdie*. Nairn Museum’s account also records that local writer John Lawson’s play *The Witch of Auldearn* was performed in Nairn in 1974.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://discoverhighlandsandislands.scot/en/story/isobel-gowdie-witch-of-auldearn" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: discoverhighlandsandislands.scot">[Discover Highlands and Islands]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">discoverhighlandsandislands.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Discover Highlands and Islands Isobel Gowdie</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Discover Highlands and Islands Isobel Gowdie</span></span></span> In modern retelling, she often shifts from “witch” to victim, visionary, folk-healer, storyteller or symbol of women persecuted by religious and legal power.

## Rait Castle and the Handless Ghost

Rait Castle gives Nairnshire its strongest castle ghost story. The ruin stands a couple of miles south of Nairn at the foot of Ord Hill, and Nairn Museum’s account describes it as a 13th-century hall-house with thick walls, gothic windows, a tower and the remains of St Mary’s Chapel nearby.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://discoverhighlandsandislands.scot/en/story/rait-castle" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: discoverhighlandsandislands.scot">[Discover Highlands and Islands]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">discoverhighlandsandislands.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Discover Highlands and Islands Rait Castle &#124; Spirit: Stories</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Discover Highlands and Islands Rait Castle &#124; Spirit: Stories</span></span></span> Historic Environment Scotland’s Trove record also identifies Rait Castle as a recognised historic place with scheduled-monument associations and a substantial antiquarian bibliography.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.trove.scot/place/15163" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: trove.scot">[Trove Scot]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">trove.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on trove.scot.</span></span></span>

The legend is a clan-treachery story. In the Nairn Museum version, the Cummings of Rait plan to invite the Mackintoshes to a feast and murder them after a toast. The Cumming daughter, whose lover is a young Mackintosh, cannot openly reveal the plot because the household has sworn secrecy. Instead, she tells the secret to a stone near the castle, knowing her lover is hidden behind it. The Mackintoshes arrive forewarned, armed beneath their plaids, and when the signal for slaughter comes they kill the Cummings instead.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://discoverhighlandsandislands.scot/en/story/rait-castle" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: discoverhighlandsandislands.scot">[Discover Highlands and Islands]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">discoverhighlandsandislands.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Discover Highlands and Islands Rait Castle &#124; Spirit: Stories</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Discover Highlands and Islands Rait Castle &#124; Spirit: Stories</span></span></span>

The ghost enters at the end of the tale. The Cumming chief, realising his daughter has betrayed him, chases her to her chamber. As she tries to escape through a window, he cuts off her hands and she falls to her death. The castle is said never to have been occupied afterwards, and the daughter is said to appear at dusk in a blood-stained dress, wandering the ruin with bloody stumps for hands.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://discoverhighlandsandislands.scot/en/story/rait-castle" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: discoverhighlandsandislands.scot">[Discover Highlands and Islands]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">discoverhighlandsandislands.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Discover Highlands and Islands Rait Castle &#124; Spirit: Stories</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Discover Highlands and Islands Rait Castle &#124; Spirit: Stories</span></span></span>

As folklore, Rait’s story does several things at once. It explains a ruin as a morally cursed place. It turns clan conflict into family tragedy. It gives a named landscape feature, the Stone of the Maiden, a narrative purpose. And it turns a woman’s warning into the emotional centre of the story, even though the formal conflict is between male chiefs and armed retainers. Whether or not the tale preserves a historical event, it is exactly the sort of local legend that makes a ruin feel inhabited by memory.

## Cawdor: Macbeth, the Holly Tree and the Blue-Dressed Ghost

Cawdor Castle is the Nairnshire site most visitors are likely to recognise, but its folklore is complicated by Shakespeare. The castle’s official site calls it a traditional Scottish castle inhabited by the Cawdor family for over 600 years and built around a legendary holly tree.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cawdorcastle.com/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cawdorcastle.com">[Cawdor Castle]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cawdorcastle.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cawdorcastle.com.</span></span></span> Historic Environment Scotland places it near the village of Cawdor, about five miles south-west of Nairn, overlooking Cawdor Burn and the River Nairn valley.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://portal.historicenvironment.scot/apex/f?p=1505%3A300%3A%3A%3A%3A%3AVIEWTYPE%2CVIEWREF%3Adesignation%2CGDL00099" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: portal.historicenvironment.scot">[Historic Environment Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">portal.historicenvironment.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on historicenvironment.scot.</span></span></span>

The Macbeth connection is famous but historically misleading. The Spirit archive explains that Shakespeare’s *Macbeth* links the hero to the title Thane of Cawdor, but that Macbeth died long before the title was granted and more than 300 years before the first stone of Cawdor Castle was laid. It also notes the tradition that Macbeth’s Hillock near Brodie Castle, between Forres and Nairn, is identified as the “Blasted Heath” where Macbeth meets the witches.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://discoverhighlandsandislands.scot/en/story/cawdor-castle-macbeth-and-a-ghost" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: discoverhighlandsandislands.scot">[Discover Highlands and Islands]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">discoverhighlandsandislands.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on discoverhighlandsandislands.scot.</span></span></span>

That distinction matters. Cawdor’s Macbeth reputation is literary folklore: a powerful story attached to a real place after the fact. It is not an old local memory of Macbeth living in the present castle. Visitors still come because Shakespeare gives the name “Cawdor” a dark glamour, but the responsible retelling is to separate the historical king, the later castle, the title, and the theatrical witches.

Cawdor also has its own ghost tradition. The Spirit archive records sightings of a ghost in a blue velvet dress in the castle grounds, locally associated with Muriel Calder, and another ghost said to be her husband, John Campbell.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://discoverhighlandsandislands.scot/en/story/cawdor-castle-macbeth-and-a-ghost" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: discoverhighlandsandislands.scot">[Discover Highlands and Islands]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">discoverhighlandsandislands.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on discoverhighlandsandislands.scot.</span></span></span> This is a classic aristocratic-house haunting: a named family figure, a distinctive garment, repeated sightings in a heritage setting, and a story that turns genealogy into atmosphere.

The holly-tree foundation legend belongs to a different type of tradition. In broad outline, it explains why the castle stands where it does: the site is chosen through a sign, and the natural object becomes part of the building’s identity. Whether told as family tradition, visitor interpretation or romantic castle lore, it gives Cawdor a rootedness that the Macbeth link cannot.

## Culbin Sands: A Lost Estate Under the Dunes

Culbin Sands is one of the most memorable Nairnshire-borderland legends because it turns environmental disaster into moral story. The site lies in the wider Moray coast landscape, but it is deeply relevant to Nairnshire folklore because Nairn Museum includes it in its local story cycle and because the tale is part of the coastal imagination around Nairn, Findhorn and the Moray Firth.

The historical core is stark. The Spirit archive says Culbin was once a farming township and part of the Kinnaird Estate, but vanished in a sandstorm in 1694. Removal of marram grass, used for thatching, helped destabilise the dunes; wind then drove sand inland, damaging fertile land, fields, orchards and buildings. The estate was abandoned in 1695, and later planting, including Forestry Commission work from 1922, helped stabilise the sands.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://discoverhighlandsandislands.scot/en/story/the-culbin-sands" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: discoverhighlandsandislands.scot">[Discover Highlands and Islands]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">discoverhighlandsandislands.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Discover Highlands and Islands The Culbin Sands &#124; Spirit: Stories</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Discover Highlands and Islands The Culbin Sands &#124; Spirit: Stories</span></span></span> Robert Gordon University’s “Disappeared Village” project similarly frames Culbin as a place swallowed by sand in 1694, later surrounded by centuries of mystery, folklore and natural explanation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.rgu.ac.uk/rgview/impactful-research/5283-culbin-the-disappeared-village-project" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: rgu.ac.uk">[Robert Gordon University]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">rgu.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">5283 culbin the disappeared village project</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">5283 culbin the disappeared village project</span></span></span>

The legends explain the disaster as punishment. One version says young Kinnaird condemned local women for witchcraft, broke the Sabbath by playing cards past midnight, boasted he would play with the Devil, and then failed to notice the sands engulfing the estate while the game continued. Another says the storm punished locals for hiding smugglers among the dunes.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://discoverhighlandsandislands.scot/en/story/the-culbin-sands" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: discoverhighlandsandislands.scot">[Discover Highlands and Islands]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">discoverhighlandsandislands.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Discover Highlands and Islands The Culbin Sands &#124; Spirit: Stories</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Discover Highlands and Islands The Culbin Sands &#124; Spirit: Stories</span></span></span>

The story also preserves eerie discovery motifs: shifting sands revealing a dovecot, chapel, manor-house remains, a chimney that answers a boy who shouts down it, and orchard branches that still blossom after being uncovered.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://discoverhighlandsandislands.scot/en/story/the-culbin-sands" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: discoverhighlandsandislands.scot">[Discover Highlands and Islands]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">discoverhighlandsandislands.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Discover Highlands and Islands The Culbin Sands &#124; Spirit: Stories</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Discover Highlands and Islands The Culbin Sands &#124; Spirit: Stories</span></span></span> These details are folklore doing its best work. They turn a changing landscape into a half-visible past, where archaeology, rumour and imagination all seem to rise and sink with the sand.

The useful reading is not “curse versus science”. Culbin’s folklore and its environmental explanation belong together. The dune disaster was real; the moral stories show how people understood catastrophe in a religious and superstitious culture. In Nairnshire’s wider pattern, Culbin also echoes Isobel Gowdie’s world: witchcraft accusation, devil stories, Sabbath-breaking, elf-bolts and the fear that hidden wrongdoing might return through the land itself.

## Fisherfolk, Ganseys and Everyday Superstition

Nairnshire folklore is not only castles and witch trials. Some of its richest material belongs to Fishertown, Nairn’s fishing community. Here, belief lived in work routines, clothing, weather signs, boat handling and New Year customs rather than in grand supernatural episodes.

Nairn Museum’s gansey account defines a gansey as a knitted woollen pullover traditionally worn by fishermen, practical and warm but often beautifully patterned. It notes that the Moray Firth Partnership’s Gansey Project, established in 2010, recorded local patterns, and that Nairn ganseys used complex combinations inspired by fishing life, including waves, nets, ropes and herring bones.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://discoverhighlandsandislands.scot/en/story/weaving-yarns-gansies-folklore-and-superstition" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: discoverhighlandsandislands.scot">[Discover Highlands and Islands]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">discoverhighlandsandislands.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on discoverhighlandsandislands.scot.</span></span></span>

The folklore around ganseys is especially interesting because it includes a modern myth as well as older superstition. The common claim that every fishing community had unique patterns so drowned fishermen could be identified has no firm proof, according to the Nairn Museum account, though a gansey could sometimes help identify a person because a knitter recognised her own work. Another belief held that if a gansey was unfinished at midnight on New Year’s Eve it brought bad luck, and that if the intended wearer died before it was finished, the garment should be unravelled and the wool kept aside for at least a year.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://discoverhighlandsandislands.scot/en/story/weaving-yarns-gansies-folklore-and-superstition" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: discoverhighlandsandislands.scot">[Discover Highlands and Islands]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">discoverhighlandsandislands.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on discoverhighlandsandislands.scot.</span></span></span>

Fishing superstition in Nairn was practical, protective and often highly specific. It was unlucky to whistle in case it summoned the devil or gale-force winds. Green could be avoided because it was the colour of land and might draw the boat shoreward. Certain animals, including pig and salmon, were not named directly. Meeting a minister or a red-haired woman on the way to the boats could make men turn back, while bad luck could be countered by calling out “cauld iron” and touching iron on the boat.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://discoverhighlandsandislands.scot/en/story/weaving-yarns-gansies-folklore-and-superstition" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: discoverhighlandsandislands.scot">[Discover Highlands and Islands]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">discoverhighlandsandislands.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on discoverhighlandsandislands.scot.</span></span></span>

One particularly vivid custom concerned the direction of departure: when setting out from shore or harbour, boats’ bows should be turned with the sun, clockwise, or bad luck would follow. On New Year’s Day, fisherfolk collected salt water and seaweed; the water was sprinkled around the house and the seaweed placed above the door, on the hearth and in the roof-beam to bring good fortune in the coming season.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://discoverhighlandsandislands.scot/en/story/weaving-yarns-gansies-folklore-and-superstition" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: discoverhighlandsandislands.scot">[Discover Highlands and Islands]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">discoverhighlandsandislands.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on discoverhighlandsandislands.scot.</span></span></span>

These customs matter because they show folklore as everyday risk management. In a community dependent on weather, tides, fish, boats and male absence at sea, superstition gave people a way to act when danger could not be controlled. It also linked domestic labour, women’s knitting, household luck and maritime survival into one belief system.

## Stones, Cairns and Older Sacred Landscapes

Nairnshire also has prehistoric sites that attracted later ritual and superstition. The Mains of Moyness ring-cairn is one concrete example. Highland Council’s Historic Environment Record describes the remains of a ring-cairn with part of its outer kerb surviving, one monolith still standing, other likely monoliths fallen or broken, and an urn found during excavation in 1856.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://her.highland.gov.uk/monument/MHG7208" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: her.highland.gov.uk">[Highland Historic Environment Record]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">her.highland.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on highland.gov.uk.</span></span></span>

Later folklore often attaches itself to such ancient monuments because their original purpose is no longer remembered. Local history summaries of Nairn record traditions that some stone circles and standing stones later became linked with superstition, law and ritual, including a Moyness rocking-stone used to test guilt and a custom at Dundeasil near Clunas of circling the site three times before beginning a day’s work for luck.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Nairn" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">History of Nairn</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">History of Nairn</span></span></span> These claims should be treated carefully: they are later reported customs, not secure evidence of prehistoric practice. Their value lies in showing how old stones remained socially meaningful long after their builders were forgotten.

The broader pattern is familiar across Scotland. Ancient stones become judges, luck-bringers, fairy places, burial markers, pagan survivals or Christianised warning sites depending on local memory. In Nairnshire, the stone landscape connects well with Gowdie’s Auldearn testimony, because her alleged witch meeting at the old kirk is said in the museum account to have stood on the site of a pre-Christian stone circle.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://discoverhighlandsandislands.scot/en/story/isobel-gowdie-witch-of-auldearn" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: discoverhighlandsandislands.scot">[Discover Highlands and Islands]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">discoverhighlandsandislands.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Discover Highlands and Islands Isobel Gowdie</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Discover Highlands and Islands Isobel Gowdie</span></span></span>

The safest interpretation is layered. The archaeology is ancient. The folklore is later. The modern visitor meets both at once: a real prehistoric monument, an antiquarian record, a local custom and a continuing appetite for mystery.

## How Nairnshire’s Traditions Are Retold Today

Modern Nairnshire folklore is preserved less through one canonical book than through museums, heritage archives, local performances, visitor sites and community storytelling. The Spirit of the Highlands and Islands archive hosts a series of stories gifted by Nairn Museum, including Isobel Gowdie, Rait Castle, Cawdor, Culbin Sands and gansey traditions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://discoverhighlandsandislands.scot/en/story/isobel-gowdie-witch-of-auldearn" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: discoverhighlandsandislands.scot">[Discover Highlands and Islands]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">discoverhighlandsandislands.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Discover Highlands and Islands Isobel Gowdie</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Discover Highlands and Islands Isobel Gowdie</span></span></span><span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://discoverhighlandsandislands.scot/en/bank/stories-from-nairn-museum" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: discoverhighlandsandislands.scot">[Discover Highlands and Islands]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">discoverhighlandsandislands.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on discoverhighlandsandislands.scot.</span></span></span>

That matters because Nairnshire’s folklore is easy to flatten into tourist shorthand: “Macbeth’s castle”, “Scotland’s famous witch”, “haunted ruin”, “lost village”. The better retelling keeps the distinctions clear.

**Old legal record:** Isobel Gowdie’s confessions are historical documents from a witch-trial culture, preserving fairy and witch beliefs through coercive legal circumstances.

**Local legend:** Rait Castle’s handless ghost and Culbin’s devil-card game are traditional explanatory stories tied to specific places.

**Literary invention:** Cawdor’s Macbeth glamour is mostly Shakespearean and post-medieval, not evidence that Macbeth lived in the present castle.

**Workplace custom:** Nairn’s ganseys, seaweed rites, fishing taboos and boat-turning practices belong to lived community belief rather than elite romance.

**Heritage retelling:** Museums, archives and tourism bodies now preserve and reshape these stories for visitors and residents.

Nairnshire’s folklore identity is therefore not about having the largest bestiary or the most spectacular supernatural claim. Its strength is intimacy. It shows how a small historic county can hold a dense set of traditions around women accused of witchcraft, dangerous coastlines, working fishing families, ruined castles, Shakespearean misunderstanding and old stones whose meanings have been remade again and again.
