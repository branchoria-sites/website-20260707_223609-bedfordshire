## Introduction

For this project, Sutherland means the historic county: the northern Highland shire bounded by Caithness to the north-east and Ross-shire, with the Ullapool parcel of Cromartyshire, to the south. It is now within the Highland Council area, and Scottish counties as local government units were abolished in 1975, but the historic county remains the useful frame for old parish records, antiquarian collections, estate traditions and mapped county folklore.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://wikishire.co.uk/wiki/Sutherland" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wikishire.co.uk">[Wikishire]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wikishire.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Wikishire Sutherland</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wikishire Sutherland</span></span></span>

## Where Sutherland’s folklore sits on the map

Sutherland is one of the northernmost counties of Great Britain, despite a name meaning “southern land” from the viewpoint of Norse power in Orkney and Caithness. That point matters for folklore because the county’s stories draw from several overlapping worlds: Norse sea routes, Gaelic-speaking Highland communities, clan territories such as Assynt and Strathnaver, medieval church culture at Dornoch, and estate life around places such as Skibo and Dunrobin. Wikishire describes Sutherland as mountainous, sparsely populated, with two coasts: the North Sea coast in the east and the Atlantic and north coast on the other side of the shire.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://wikishire.co.uk/wiki/Sutherland" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wikishire.co.uk">[Wikishire]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wikishire.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Wikishire Sutherland</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wikishire Sutherland</span></span></span>

A reader should not expect Sutherland folklore to behave like a neat administrative archive. Stories cross into Ross-shire, Caithness, Orkney, the Hebrides and the wider Highlands. The old people who told them were not thinking in modern council boundaries. Even Dempster’s material includes comparative notes and traditions that touch neighbouring districts, but the strongest Sutherland examples attach to places such as Dornoch, the Shin, Loch Migdale, Ben Stack, Ben Loyal, Loch Merkland, Tongue, Assynt, Lochinver and Loch Assynt.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Folk-Lore_Journal/Volume_6/Folk-Lore_of_Sutherlandshire_%28September%29" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: en.wikisource.org">[Wikisource]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">en.wikisource.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Folk-Lore Journal/Volume 6/Folk-Lore of Sutherlandshire (September</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Folk-Lore Journal/Volume 6/Folk-Lore of Sutherlandshire (September</span></span></span>

The landscape explains much of the mood. Brochs, henges, sea-pools, burns, ferry-crossings, lochs, moorland and cathedral precincts all become places where the ordinary world feels thin. Dun Dornaigil, for example, is a real Iron Age broch in Sutherland, free to visit and under Historic Environment Scotland care, while Loch Migdale has a small prehistoric henge likely to date to the Later Neolithic or Earlier Bronze Age. These are not “proof” of the supernatural, but they show why Sutherland’s stories so often settle on old stones, water and lonely tracks.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.historicenvironment.scot/visit-a-place/places/dun-dornaigil-broch/prices-and-opening-times/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: historicenvironment.scot">[Historic Environment Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">historicenvironment.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on historicenvironment.scot.</span></span></span>

## The collector who preserved much of the county’s old lore

The key name for Sutherland folklore is Charlotte Louisa Dempster. Her manuscript “Tales from the people of Sutherland” is held by the National Library of Scotland, which notes that most of the tales were published as “The Folk-lore of Sutherlandshire” in *The Folk-Lore Journal*, volume six, in 1888.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://manuscripts.nls.uk/repositories/2/archival_objects/25033" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: manuscripts.nls.uk">[National Library of Scotland Manuscripts]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">manuscripts.nls.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nls.uk.</span></span></span>

Dempster’s introduction is unusually valuable because it tells us how she understood her work. She said she gathered the tales and sayings in 1859, thanked the local people who gave her the material, and warned that it had already become difficult to collect such stories and would soon be impossible to gather them in Sutherland in the same way. She linked the loss to changes in language, reading habits, religious attitudes and modern progress.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Folk-Lore_Journal/Volume_6/Folk-Lore_of_Sutherlandshire_%28September%29" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: en.wikisource.org">[Wikisource]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">en.wikisource.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Folk-Lore Journal/Volume 6/Folk-Lore of Sutherlandshire (September</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Folk-Lore Journal/Volume 6/Folk-Lore of Sutherlandshire (September</span></span></span>

That does not mean every tale she printed is a direct, untouched transcript of oral performance. Dempster sometimes shaped, annotated and compared the stories with European parallels. She also wrote from a landowning and antiquarian world, not from the social position of every teller she records. Still, her collection is central because it preserves named local informants, place-linked tales and a form of Sutherland story culture that later writers, tourist guides and online retellings repeatedly draw upon.

The most useful way to read Dempster is as a bridge: she stands between living oral tradition and printed folklore. Some stories in her collection have the rhythm of old international tale-types; others feel close to local belief reports, like warnings about pools, corpse omens, dogs, Fridays, new moons and unlucky journeys. Her work is strongest when it keeps the story tied to a named place or person rather than turning Sutherland into generic “Celtic mythology”.

## Fairies, changelings and the little people

Sutherland’s fairies are not the tiny harmless flower-figures of later children’s books. Dempster’s fairy material includes working “little men”, fairy servants, changelings, music, fairy hunting, and dangerous dealings with beings who may help, mock, frighten or punish humans. In her September instalment, Donald-Duival Mackay can compel the “little men” to work for him, but they are not cosy helpers: the episode becomes comic, frightening and magical at once, with impossible labour and weather-making attached to the figure.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Folk-Lore_Journal/Volume_6/Folk-Lore_of_Sutherlandshire_%28September%29" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: en.wikisource.org">[Wikisource]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">en.wikisource.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Folk-Lore Journal/Volume 6/Folk-Lore of Sutherlandshire (September</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Folk-Lore Journal/Volume 6/Folk-Lore of Sutherlandshire (September</span></span></span>

The changeling story is one of the clearest Sutherland examples. Dempster begins with a tailor and his wife whose child cries constantly and gives them no peace. When the servant Donald enters, the child speaks with strange sharpness and insists on joining him in the barn. The tale belongs to a wider Scottish and European family of changeling stories, where a strange, precocious or sickly child is suspected of being a fairy substitute.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Folk-Lore_Journal/Volume_6/Folk-Lore_of_Sutherlandshire_%28December%29" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: en.wikisource.org">[Wikisource]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">en.wikisource.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Folk-Lore Journal/Volume 6/Folk-Lore of Sutherlandshire (December</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Folk-Lore Journal/Volume 6/Folk-Lore of Sutherlandshire (December</span></span></span>

For modern readers, changeling tales need careful handling. They are folklore, not medical evidence. They show how earlier communities made sense of infant illness, disability, difference, exhaustion and fear under conditions where diagnosis and care were limited. In Sutherland, as elsewhere, the changeling motif turns family anxiety into a story about exchange between worlds. It also carries a moral charge: the human household must recognise the trick, but the story’s humour and cruelty can sit uncomfortably together.

Dempster also records fairy haunts with a strong place-sense. Three conical hills, including two called Torr Berrichan, are described as principal fairy haunts in Sutherland; the fairies there are “dressed fairies” with green clothes, horns, bagpipes, reels and hounds. That detail matters because it gives Sutherland fairy lore a local social shape. The fairy world mimics human leisure, hunting and music, but at a dangerous angle.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Folk-Lore_Journal/Volume_6/Folk-Lore_of_Sutherlandshire_%28December%29" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: en.wikisource.org">[Wikisource]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">en.wikisource.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Folk-Lore Journal/Volume 6/Folk-Lore of Sutherlandshire (December</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Folk-Lore Journal/Volume 6/Folk-Lore of Sutherlandshire (December</span></span></span>

## Water-women, kelpies and the pull of lochs and burns

Sutherland’s supernatural water beings are some of the county’s most distinctive figures. Dempster uses terms such as Vaugh, Baugh, banshee, weird woman of the water and kelpie, and her examples are tied to real waterways: the Shin, the Laxford, Loch Migdale, the Oikel and mills or fords where people and animals had to cross.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Folk-Lore_Journal/Volume_6/Folk-Lore_of_Sutherlandshire_%28December%29" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: en.wikisource.org">[Wikisource]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">en.wikisource.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Folk-Lore Journal/Volume 6/Folk-Lore of Sutherlandshire (December</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Folk-Lore Journal/Volume 6/Folk-Lore of Sutherlandshire (December</span></span></span>

The Vaugh of the Shin appears when a poacher gets help landing salmon and realises his helper is no ordinary accomplice. The creature comes to claim “halves”, dressed in green and terrifying enough to change the man’s hair in a night. In the Vaugh of the Laxford, a man’s large dog attacks the water-fairy by a loch near Ben Stack, and both creature and dog disappear over the terraces of the hill.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Folk-Lore_Journal/Volume_6/Folk-Lore_of_Sutherlandshire_%28December%29" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: en.wikisource.org">[Wikisource]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">en.wikisource.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Folk-Lore Journal/Volume 6/Folk-Lore of Sutherlandshire (December</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Folk-Lore Journal/Volume 6/Folk-Lore of Sutherlandshire (December</span></span></span>

Loch Migdale has one of the most memorable water-fairy scenes. A female figure connected with MoulinnaVaugha, “the kelpie’s mill”, is captured, carried on horseback, pricked with an awl and needle, and then drops like a fallen star into a small lump of jelly when light falls on her. The story may sound bizarre, but it shows how local folklore turns natural oddities, dangerous mills, night travel and water-crossings into a single uncanny episode.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Folk-Lore_Journal/Volume_6/Folk-Lore_of_Sutherlandshire_%28December%29" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: en.wikisource.org">[Wikisource]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">en.wikisource.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Folk-Lore Journal/Volume 6/Folk-Lore of Sutherlandshire (December</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Folk-Lore Journal/Volume 6/Folk-Lore of Sutherlandshire (December</span></span></span>

Dempster also distinguishes “dressed fairies” from water-kelpies, saying Highlanders viewed kelpies as more mischievous and particularly associated them with mills and fords. That distinction is important for Sutherland because much of the county’s practical life depended on crossing burns, using mills and reading water conditions. The kelpie is a supernatural figure, but it also encodes a very real warning: water, especially after dark, can kill.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Folk-Lore_Journal/Volume_6/Folk-Lore_of_Sutherlandshire_%28December%29" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: en.wikisource.org">[Wikisource]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">en.wikisource.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Folk-Lore Journal/Volume 6/Folk-Lore of Sutherlandshire (December</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Folk-Lore Journal/Volume 6/Folk-Lore of Sutherlandshire (December</span></span></span>

## Dragons, saints and heroic beasts

Sutherland has a striking dragon tradition centred on Dornoch and St Gilbert. Dempster’s account says a great dragon burned the fir-woods of Ross, Sutherland and Reay, leaving charred remains in the mosses. The beast comes near Dornoch, cries out against the town, and is killed by St Gilbert with an arrow. The townspeople bury it, and Dempster says men still reckoned distance by “the stone of the Beast” on the moor between Skibo and Dornoch.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Folk-Lore_Journal/Volume_6/Folk-Lore_of_Sutherlandshire_%28September%29" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: en.wikisource.org">[Wikisource]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">en.wikisource.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Folk-Lore Journal/Volume 6/Folk-Lore of Sutherlandshire (September</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Folk-Lore Journal/Volume 6/Folk-Lore of Sutherlandshire (September</span></span></span>

This is not an isolated monster anecdote. It ties together several layers of Sutherland identity: the medieval saint of Dornoch, the cathedral landscape, the old forested Highlands, peat-moss memories of buried timber, and the broad European pattern of saintly dragon-slayers. Dempster herself compares St Gilbert with other saint and hero dragon-slayers, showing that the tale belongs both to Sutherland local tradition and to a wider Christian legendary pattern.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Folk-Lore_Journal/Volume_6/Folk-Lore_of_Sutherlandshire_%28September%29" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: en.wikisource.org">[Wikisource]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">en.wikisource.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Folk-Lore Journal/Volume 6/Folk-Lore of Sutherlandshire (September</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Folk-Lore Journal/Volume 6/Folk-Lore of Sutherlandshire (September</span></span></span>

The historical St Gilbert was Gilbert de Moravia, associated with Dornoch Cathedral. The University of Glasgow’s Saints in Scottish Place-Names project records the cult of St Gilbert at Dornoch in the sixteenth century and notes that the cathedral was founded in the thirteenth century, later serving as the parish kirk.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://saintsplaces.gla.ac.uk/place.php?id=1347302180" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: saintsplaces.gla.ac.uk">[saintsplaces.gla.ac.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">saintsplaces.gla.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Saints in Scottish Place-Names</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Saints in Scottish Place-Names</span></span></span>

Sutherland also has older heroic beast tales connected with the Fianna and Ossianic tradition. Dempster’s “Death of Diarmid; or, the Boar of Ben Laighal” places a huge destructive boar in a den or cave in Ben Loyal, full of the bones of cattle and men. Diarmid kills it, but the king sets him a fatal test: measuring the beast backwards, he is pierced by its bristles and dies. The story’s force comes from the mountain anchor. Ben Loyal is not just scenery; it becomes a heroic grave-landscape.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Folk-Lore_Journal/Volume_6/Folk-Lore_of_Sutherlandshire_%28September%29" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: en.wikisource.org">[Wikisource]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">en.wikisource.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Folk-Lore Journal/Volume 6/Folk-Lore of Sutherlandshire (September</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Folk-Lore Journal/Volume 6/Folk-Lore of Sutherlandshire (September</span></span></span>

Dempster also records two dragons on Loch Merkland, one winged and one not, killed by a carrier and a soldier to make the place safe for travellers. That small detail gives the story a practical Highland feel: monsters are not only symbols of evil; they block routes, endanger movement and must be cleared from the road-world of ordinary people.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Folk-Lore_Journal/Volume_6/Folk-Lore_of_Sutherlandshire_%28December%29" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: en.wikisource.org">[Wikisource]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">en.wikisource.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Folk-Lore Journal/Volume 6/Folk-Lore of Sutherlandshire (December</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Folk-Lore Journal/Volume 6/Folk-Lore of Sutherlandshire (December</span></span></span>

## Dornoch, witchcraft and the story of Janet Horne

No Sutherland folklore page can avoid Janet Horne, but the story must be told carefully. Historylinks Museum in Dornoch states that in 1727 Janet Horne and her daughter were arrested and jailed in Dornoch, accused of witchcraft, and that Janet was the last person in Britain to be tried and executed for witchcraft. The same account says she was probably showing signs of what would now be recognised as senile dementia, while her daughter had physical deformities; the trial was rushed, and the daughter escaped.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.historylinks.org.uk/18th-century" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: historylinks.org.uk">[historylinks.org.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">historylinks.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">18th Century &#124; Historylinks</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">18th Century &#124; Historylinks</span></span></span>

This is a case where folklore, local memory and legal history blur. Modern writers have pointed out that the Janet Horne story is preserved through later accounts and local tradition rather than a clean surviving trial record; the memorial stone in Dornoch is therefore both a historical marker and a folklore object. The Bottle Imp’s discussion of the “Cailleach of the Borehole” stresses that the attending stories are muddled and conflicted, and that parts of the familiar version were not printed until long after the alleged execution.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.thebottleimp.org.uk/2019/12/the-cailleach-of-the-borehole/?print=print" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: thebottleimp.org.uk">[The Bottle Imp]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">thebottleimp.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Bottle Imp The Cailleach of the Borehole</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Bottle Imp The Cailleach of the Borehole</span></span></span>

That uncertainty does not make the story unimportant. It makes it more revealing. Janet Horne’s memory sits at the junction of three things Sutherland folklore often does: it turns social fear into supernatural accusation, attaches trauma to a physical stone, and keeps retelling a local event in new forms. Dornoch’s heritage trail still points visitors to the Witch’s Stone and notes that the stone’s date of 1722 is wrong if the execution is placed in 1727.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.thisisdornoch.com/inspiration/holidays-and-breaks/heritage-trail/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: thisisdornoch.com">[Dornoch]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">thisisdornoch.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on thisisdornoch.com.</span></span></span>

The modern afterlife of Janet Horne is also significant. Rona Munro’s play *The Last Witch*, premiered at the Edinburgh International Festival in 2009, uses the gaps and ambiguities of the story dramatically rather than pretending that every detail is settled. That is typical of how Sutherland folklore survives today: not only as old belief, but as theatre, fiction, heritage interpretation and debate about how Scotland remembers people accused of witchcraft.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.theguardian.com/culture/2009/aug/09/rona-munro-edinburgh-festival" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theguardian.com">[The Guardian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theguardian.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Guardian Rona Munro burns bright at Edinburgh</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Munro, known for writing the final episode of the original *Doctor Who* series, asserts her dislike for the romantic and sexualized direc...</span></span></span>

## Ghosts, omens and everyday supernatural rules

Sutherland folklore is not only made of grand beings. Dempster’s collection is full of small rules, omens and household practices: lucky dreams of deer, unlucky dreams of water or black, washing a baby with silver, the danger of seeing the new moon through glass, the bad luck of turning back on a journey, a dog howling at night, a candle suddenly going out, or baking bread while a corpse is in the house.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Folk-Lore_Journal/Volume_6/Folk-Lore_of_Sutherlandshire_%28December%29" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: en.wikisource.org">[Wikisource]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">en.wikisource.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Folk-Lore Journal/Volume 6/Folk-Lore of Sutherlandshire (December</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Folk-Lore Journal/Volume 6/Folk-Lore of Sutherlandshire (December</span></span></span>

These details are easy to dismiss as superstition, but they are valuable because they show belief working at the scale of daily life. A county’s folklore is not just its showpiece legends; it is also the practical code by which people judged luck, illness, death, travel, childbirth and weather. In scattered rural communities, a cracked sound in furniture, a dream, a dog’s cry or a strange light on a hill could become meaningful because ordinary safety was fragile.

Dempster’s death lore is particularly vivid. She records beliefs that death was expected early in the morning or as the tide receded, that doors were opened during the death struggle to help the spirit depart, and that salt and candles were used around the dead. She also records the wraith heard in carpenters’ shops, selecting boards for a coffin before a death.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Folk-Lore_Journal/Volume_6/Folk-Lore_of_Sutherlandshire_%28December%29" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: en.wikisource.org">[Wikisource]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">en.wikisource.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Folk-Lore Journal/Volume 6/Folk-Lore of Sutherlandshire (December</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Folk-Lore Journal/Volume 6/Folk-Lore of Sutherlandshire (December</span></span></span>

Some of the most haunting reports are landscape-based. On the Embo moor, Dempster records “spectral hosts” seen charging and repulsing one another before sunrise. At AltnaHierinn, the Burn of the Maiden, a murdered girl is said to haunt the place, and a spectral dog and man appear near the spot where the snow supposedly never lies. These are not polished fairy tales but localised haunting traditions, the kind that make a moor, burn or boundary feel morally marked.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Folk-Lore_Journal/Volume_6/Folk-Lore_of_Sutherlandshire_%28December%29" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: en.wikisource.org">[Wikisource]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">en.wikisource.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Folk-Lore Journal/Volume 6/Folk-Lore of Sutherlandshire (December</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Folk-Lore Journal/Volume 6/Folk-Lore of Sutherlandshire (December</span></span></span>

## Holy wells, stones and prehistoric places

Sutherland’s holy-well evidence in Dempster is thinner than its fairy and water-spirit material, but it still matters. Her “Holy Wells” section includes a note on St Mary’s Well at Skibo Castle, where patients once visited and hung red rags on the surrounding trees. She also compares the practice with other healing wells where people bathed or drank before sunrise and left rags or strings behind.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Folk-Lore_Journal/Volume_6/Folk-Lore_of_Sutherlandshire_%28December%29" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: en.wikisource.org">[Wikisource]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">en.wikisource.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Folk-Lore Journal/Volume 6/Folk-Lore of Sutherlandshire (December</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Folk-Lore Journal/Volume 6/Folk-Lore of Sutherlandshire (December</span></span></span>

The Skibo example links Sutherland to a much wider Scottish and British pattern of healing wells, votive cloths and saintly water. In the county itself, the stronger pattern is that water places become charged: wells, burns, fords, lochs, ferry sites and sea-pools all become places where danger, cure, curse, memory and supernatural contact meet.

Standing stones and prehistoric monuments also matter, even where the surviving folklore is fragmentary. Loch Migdale’s henge, recorded by Wessex Archaeology as a small Class 1 henge with an external bank, internal ditch and east-facing entrance, is likely prehistoric even though no dating evidence was recovered. Historic Environment Scotland’s scheduled monument record describes it as a small, almost circular henge on a low rise near the west end of Loch Migdale.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.wessexarch.co.uk/our-work/loch-migdale-sutherland" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wessexarch.co.uk">[Wessex Archaeology]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wessexarch.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Wessex Archaeology Loch Migdale, Sutherland &#124; Our Work &#124; Wessex Archaeology</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wessex Archaeology Loch Migdale, Sutherland &#124; Our Work &#124; Wessex Archaeology</span></span></span>

Sites like Loch Migdale are not automatically “fairy places”, and responsible folklore writing should not paste generic myth onto every ancient stone. Their relevance is subtler: prehistoric earthworks and brochs give later communities visible oldness. They are places where nobody in the nineteenth century knew the original meaning, so story could gather around them. In Sutherland, this process is clearest where old structures lie beside water, tracks or lonely ground.

## Mermaids and modern Assynt retellings

The best-known modern Sutherland mermaid story is the Mermaid of Assynt, usually attached to Loch Assynt and Ardvreck Castle. Current retellings often say that a MacLeod chief’s daughter was promised to the Devil as part of a bargain connected with the castle, threw herself into Loch Assynt, and became a weeping mermaid whose tears explain changes in the loch’s waters. Hidden Scotland and local tourism material retell this version, while the North West Highlands Geopark’s place-name gazetteer notes legendary tales with geological aspects, including the Mermaid of Assynt associated with Ardvreck Castle.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://hiddenscotland.com/journal/the-weeping-mermaid-of-loch-assynt" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: hiddenscotland.com">[Hidden Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">hiddenscotland.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on hiddenscotland.com.</span></span></span>

This tale is useful precisely because it shows how folklore evolves. Dempster’s older Sutherland material includes a separate Lochinver mermaid story: a mermaid falls in love with a fisherman, who tries to secure himself by taking control of her magical belongings. Modern folklore sites often connect this to wider “mermaid wife” tale-types, where a supernatural woman is trapped by the loss of an object that lets her return to the water.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://sites.pitt.edu/~dash/type4080.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sites.pitt.edu">[Pitt Sites]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sites.pitt.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on pitt.edu.</span></span></span>

Ardvreck Castle itself gives the Assynt legend a strong visual anchor. The ruin stands on Loch Assynt’s northern shore near Inchnadamph, and modern heritage accounts describe it as a fifteenth-century MacLeod stronghold later seized by the Mackenzies. Its ruined state, loch setting and clan history make it a natural home for ghost and mermaid stories.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.venture-north.co.uk/listing/ardvreck-castle-loch-assynt/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: venture-north.co.uk">[Venture North]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">venture-north.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Venture North Ardvreck Castle &amp; Loch Assynt</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Venture North Ardvreck Castle &amp; Loch Assynt</span></span></span>

The challenge for readers is to separate levels of tradition. The Lochinver mermaid has a clear printed nineteenth-century source in Dempster. The Mermaid of Assynt is a living tourist and local retelling around Ardvreck and Loch Assynt, with geological and heritage interpretations layered onto it. Both belong to Sutherland folklore, but they are not the same kind of evidence.

## Black dogs and fairy hounds

Sutherland does not have one county-wide black dog as famous as East Anglia’s Black Shuck, but dog-omens and fairy hounds run through Highland tradition and appear in Sutherland material. Dempster lists hearing a dog howl at night among unlucky omens, and her AltnaHierinn haunting includes a spectral dog and man near the burn.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Folk-Lore_Journal/Volume_6/Folk-Lore_of_Sutherlandshire_%28December%29" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: en.wikisource.org">[Wikisource]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">en.wikisource.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Folk-Lore Journal/Volume 6/Folk-Lore of Sutherlandshire (December</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Folk-Lore Journal/Volume 6/Folk-Lore of Sutherlandshire (December</span></span></span>

The wider Highland figure is the fairy hound, often known in Gaelic tradition as the Cù-sìth. It is usually described as a huge otherworldly dog of the Highlands and Islands, connected with fairies, moors, rocks and death-omens. Modern summaries often say it could bark three times, with the third bark bringing fatal terror to anyone not safely sheltered.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/C%C3%B9-s%C3%ACth" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wikipedia.org.</span></span></span>

For Sutherland, the important point is not to force an English black-dog template onto the county. The Sutherland evidence points more to Highland fairy-hound and death-omen patterns than to a single named roadside phantom. In other words, dogs in Sutherland folklore are liminal creatures: they can defend a man against a water-being, appear in a haunting, signal death, or belong to the fairy hunt.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Folk-Lore_Journal/Volume_6/Folk-Lore_of_Sutherlandshire_%28December%29" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: en.wikisource.org">[Wikisource]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">en.wikisource.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Folk-Lore Journal/Volume 6/Folk-Lore of Sutherlandshire (December</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Folk-Lore Journal/Volume 6/Folk-Lore of Sutherlandshire (December</span></span></span>

## Why Sutherland’s folklore still feels distinctive

Sutherland’s folklore matters because it makes the county’s landscape legible in an older emotional language. The stories explain why a pool should be avoided after dusk, why a moor might be haunted by armies, why a hill has a jewel, why a ruined castle has a weeping woman, why a cathedral town remembers a witch execution, and why a saint’s church can be imagined as the last defence against a fire-breathing beast.

The traditions are also unusually good at preserving social memory. The Janet Horne story remembers the violence of witchcraft accusation. The kelpie and Vaugh stories remember risky crossings, mills and illicit fishing. The changeling stories remember family fear around illness and difference. The dragon story remembers charred forests and saintly authority. The fairy stories remember a Gaelic-speaking world of crofts, servants, barns, hills, music and uneasy bargains.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.historylinks.org.uk/18th-century" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: historylinks.org.uk">[historylinks.org.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">historylinks.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">18th Century &#124; Historylinks</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">18th Century &#124; Historylinks</span></span></span>

Modern Sutherland retellings should therefore be lively but careful. It is fair to call these legends mysterious, beautiful or unsettling, but not fair to present them as confirmed supernatural events. The strongest approach is to keep the story attached to its source and place: Dempster’s 1859 oral collecting, Dornoch’s witch memory, St Gilbert’s cathedral, Loch Migdale’s prehistoric ring, Loch Assynt’s ruined castle, and the older Highland habit of reading water, weather, animals and lonely ground as signs.

## How to read Sutherland folklore today

A good Sutherland folklore route begins with Dornoch, not because it explains the whole county, but because it gathers several layers in one small town: St Gilbert’s cathedral, the dragon tradition, the Witch’s Stone, the memory of Janet Horne, and graveyard ghost-tales such as the tailor and the skeleton. From there, the reader can follow the stories west and north: the Shin and Oikel for water-fairy material, Loch Migdale for kelpie and prehistoric landscape, Ben Stack and Ben Loyal for hill-legends, Lochinver and Loch Assynt for mermaids, Ardvreck Castle for modern haunting, and the north coast for the wide, sea-facing world from which “southern land” once made sense.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Folk-Lore_Journal/Volume_6/Folk-Lore_of_Sutherlandshire_%28September%29" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: en.wikisource.org">[wikisource.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">en.wikisource.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Folk-Lore Journal/Volume 6/Folk-Lore of Sutherlandshire (September</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Folk-Lore Journal/Volume 6/Folk-Lore of Sutherlandshire (September</span></span></span>

The county’s folklore is strongest when read as a map of relationships: between people and water, saints and monsters, old women and accusation, children and fear, stones and memory, roads and danger, oral tradition and printed preservation. Sutherland’s tales do not need exaggeration. Their power lies in how precisely they cling to place.

    var joiner = url.indexOf('?') >= 0 ? '&' : '?';
    var params = [];
    if (cfg.mkrid) params.push('mkrid=' + encodeURIComponent(cfg.mkrid));
    if (cfg.campaign_id) params.push('campid=' + encodeURIComponent(cfg.campaign_id));
    params.push('mkevt=1');
    params.push('mkcid=1');
    params.push('toolid=' + encodeURIComponent(cfg.tool_id || '10001'));
    if (reference) params.push('customid=' + encodeURIComponent(reference).slice(0, 256));
    return url + joiner + params.join('&');
  }
  function domainLabel(marketId) {
    var cfg = markets[marketId] || markets[defaultMarket] || {};
    return cfg.domain_label || 'eBay';
  }
  function flagClass(marketId) {
    return 'fr-ebay-market-flag fr-ebay-market-flag--' + String(marketId || '').toLowerCase().replace(/_/g, '-').replace(/[^a-z0-9-]+/g, '');
  }
  function closeMenu(section) {
    var menu = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-menu]');
    var trigger = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-trigger]');
    var picker = section.querySelector('.fr-ebay-market-picker');
    if (menu) menu.hidden = true;
    if (trigger) trigger.setAttribute('aria-expanded', 'false');
    if (picker) picker.classList.remove('fr-ebay-market-picker--open');
  }
  function availableMarkets(section) {
    var select = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-select]');
    if (select) {
      return Array.prototype.slice.call(select.options)
        .map(function (option) { return option.value; })
        .filter(function (marketId) { return !!markets[marketId]; });
    }
    return Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]'))
      .map(function (button) { return button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button'); })
      .filter(function (marketId) { return !!markets[marketId]; });
  }
  function applyMarket(section, marketId, persist) {
    var available = availableMarkets(section);
    if (available.indexOf(marketId)< 0) marketId = available[0] || defaultMarket;
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-localized-link]')).forEach(function (link) {
      var query = link.getAttribute('data-ebay-query') || '';
      var reference = link.getAttribute('data-ebay-reference') || '';
      link.setAttribute('href', buildUrl(query, reference, marketId));
    });
    var select = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-select]');
    if (select && available.indexOf(marketId) >= 0) select.value = marketId;
    var selectedText = marketId;
    if (select && select.selectedIndex >= 0) selectedText = select.options[select.selectedIndex].text;
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-selected-market-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = selectedText;
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-selected-market-flag], [data-ebay-trigger-market-flag]')).forEach(function (flag) {
      flag.className = flagClass(marketId);
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-trigger-market-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = selectedText;
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-domain-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = domainLabel(marketId);
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-option]')).forEach(function (button) {
      button.setAttribute('aria-selected', button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-option') === marketId ? 'true' : 'false');
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      var active = button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button') === marketId;
      button.setAttribute('aria-pressed', active ? 'true' : 'false');
    });
    section.setAttribute('data-ebay-selected-market', marketId);
    if (persist) {
      try { if (window.localStorage) localStorage.setItem('phoenix-ebay-market', marketId); } catch (err) {}
    }
    closeMenu(section);
  }
  sections.forEach(function (section) {
    if (section.getAttribute('data-ebay-localized-init') === '1') return;
    section.setAttribute('data-ebay-localized-init', '1');
    var available = availableMarkets(section);
    var marketId = defaultMarket;
    if (window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation && window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation.inferMarket) {
      marketId = window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation.inferMarket(available, {
        storageKey: 'phoenix-ebay-market',
        defaultMarket: defaultMarket
      });
    } else if (available.indexOf(defaultMarket)< 0) {
      marketId = available[0] || defaultMarket;
    }
    var select = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-select]');
    if (select) {
      select.addEventListener('change', function () {
        applyMarket(section, select.value, true);
      });
    }
    var trigger = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-trigger]');
    var menu = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-menu]');
    if (trigger && menu) {
      trigger.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
        event.stopPropagation();
        var expanded = trigger.getAttribute('aria-expanded') === 'true';
        menu.hidden = expanded;
        trigger.setAttribute('aria-expanded', expanded ? 'false' : 'true');
        var picker = trigger.closest('.fr-ebay-market-picker');
        if (picker) picker.classList.toggle('fr-ebay-market-picker--open', !expanded);
      });
      Array.prototype.slice.call(menu.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-option]')).forEach(function (button) {
        button.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
          event.stopPropagation();
          applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-option'), true);
        });
      });
      document.addEventListener('click', function () { closeMenu(section); });
    }
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      button.addEventListener('click', function () {
        applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button'), true);
      });
    });
    applyMarket(section, marketId, false);
  });
})();
