## Introduction

Isobel Gowdie’s 1662 confessions make Auldearn one of the most important places in [Nairnshire]({{ 'where-nairnshires-strange-stories-still/' | relative_url }}) folklore. They are not simply a witch-trial curiosity: they preserve one of Britain’s most vivid early modern records of fairy belief, shape-shifting, charms, elf-arrows, coven meetings and fear of harmful magic, all filtered through the dangerous machinery of a 17th-century Scottish prosecution. For Nairnshire, the case matters because it gives the county a rare documentary window into a supernatural landscape. Auldearn kirk, Nairn kirkyard, Inshoch, Downie Hill and the farms around Loch Loy are not just background scenery. In the confessions they become a map of fear, story, accusation and imagined travel between the ordinary parish and Fairyland. The material should not be read as proof that witches flew or fairies fired arrows; its value lies in showing how people could explain illness, death, misfortune and social conflict through a blend of Christian demonology and older fairy lore.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://rmtrain.h-net.org/reviews/showrev.php?id=31976" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: rmtrain.h-net.org">[rmtrain.h-net.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">rmtrain.h-net.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">H-Net ReviewsIn the summer of 1662, Isobel Gowdie of the parish of Auldearn in northeastern Scotland was convicted for witchcraft, bringi...</span></span></span>

## Why Auldearn Became Nairnshire’s Witch-Trial Landmark

Auldearn is a village and parish in historic Nairnshire, east of the River Nairn and just outside the county town of Nairn. That location matters: Gowdie’s confessions are rooted in a small parish world rather than in a vague “Highland” backdrop. Wikishire describes Auldearn as a Nairnshire village and parish, while Nairnshire itself sits on the Moray Firth, bordered by Inverness-shire and Morayshire in the historic county frame.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://wikishire.co.uk/wiki/Auldearn" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wikishire.co.uk">[Wikishire]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wikishire.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wikishire.co.uk.</span></span></span>

The surviving case is part of Scotland’s wider witch-prosecution period, which ran under the Scottish Witchcraft Act from 1563 until repeal in 1736. The University of Edinburgh’s Survey of Scottish Witchcraft was created to catalogue people accused of witchcraft in Scotland across that period, and its public database contains nearly 4,000 accused people.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://datashare.ed.ac.uk/handle/10283/45" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: datashare.ed.ac.uk">[datashare.ed.ac.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">datashare.ed.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on ed.ac.uk.</span></span></span> The Auldearn case therefore belongs to a national pattern, but its folklore value is unusually local and unusually rich.

Gowdie’s first confession was recorded at Auldearn on 13 April 1662. A version of the text printed from Robert Pitcairn’s 19th-century edition names the setting, witnesses and Gowdie herself as the spouse of John Gilbert in Lochloy, and presents her as speaking “without any compulsion” before local clerical and landed men.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://stuartmchardy.wordpress.com/2013/04/02/isobel-gowdie/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: stuartmchardy.wordpress.com">[Stuart McHardy]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">stuartmchardy.wordpress.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Stuart Mc Hardy Isobel Gowdie</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Stuart Mc Hardy Isobel Gowdie</span></span></span> That phrase is historically important but not simple. Modern readers should not take it as a guarantee of free testimony in the present-day sense. Accused witches could face imprisonment, fear, pressure, leading questions and the threat of death even when a record claimed that a confession was voluntary.

Auldearn’s distinctiveness comes from the detail of what was written down. The Survey of Scottish Witchcraft notes that Gowdie’s confessions are too detailed to summarise fully in its case entry and warns that, although the case has fed modern ideas about covens, broomsticks, shape-shifting and witch meetings, it was not typical of most Scottish witchcraft cases.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://witches.hca.ed.ac.uk/case/C/EGD/1558" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: witches.hca.ed.ac.uk">[witches.hca.ed.ac.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">witches.hca.ed.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">the case of Issobell GowdieHer confessions in Pitcairn are too detailed to be able to put all the information in this case. Anyone intere...</span></span></span> That warning is crucial: Gowdie is famous because her words are extraordinary, not because every witch-trial record in Scotland looks like hers.

## The 1662 Confessions and the Auldearn Setting

The confessions were recorded over several weeks, from mid-April to late May 1662. They place Gowdie in a recognisable parish landscape: Auldearn kirk, Nairn, Inshoch, Lochloy and nearby rural places. This is one reason the case remains so powerful for Nairnshire folklore. The supernatural claims do not float in a mythical nowhere; they attach themselves to churches, fields, roads, farms and local families.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://stuartmchardy.wordpress.com/2013/04/02/isobel-gowdie/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: stuartmchardy.wordpress.com">[Stuart McHardy]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">stuartmchardy.wordpress.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Stuart Mc Hardy Isobel Gowdie</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Stuart Mc Hardy Isobel Gowdie</span></span></span>

In the first confession, Gowdie described meeting the Devil after going between local farmsteads and then promising to meet him at night in Auldearn kirk. The printed text has her renouncing her baptism, being marked, and receiving a new name in the Devil’s service.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://stuartmchardy.wordpress.com/2013/04/02/isobel-gowdie/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: stuartmchardy.wordpress.com">[Stuart McHardy]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">stuartmchardy.wordpress.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Stuart Mc Hardy Isobel Gowdie</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Stuart Mc Hardy Isobel Gowdie</span></span></span> This is recognisably demonological material: the sort of pact-and-renunciation language that churchmen and witch-prosecutors expected. Yet the same body of testimony also contains far stranger and more locally textured material, including fairy journeys, elf-arrows and animal transformation.

A useful way to read the case is as a collision between two kinds of belief. One is official demonology: the learned, clerical and legal idea that witches made pacts with the Devil, attended meetings and harmed neighbours. The other is popular supernatural belief: fairies under hills, harmful elf-shot, spoken charms, animal disguise and hidden realms near the everyday landscape. Gowdie’s confessions are remarkable because the two are not kept separate. They are fused into a single narrative world.

This does not mean Gowdie’s words can be treated as a neat transcript of “folk belief” untouched by interrogation. The presence of ministers, lairds and notaries shaped the record. The questions asked, the expectations of the court, and the danger facing Gowdie all matter. Even so, the material preserves images and motifs so vivid that historians and folklorists continue to treat the case as a rare insight into the imaginative world of 17th-century Scotland.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://rmtrain.h-net.org/reviews/showrev.php?id=31976" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: rmtrain.h-net.org">[rmtrain.h-net.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">rmtrain.h-net.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">H-Net ReviewsIn the summer of 1662, Isobel Gowdie of the parish of Auldearn in northeastern Scotland was convicted for witchcraft, bringi...</span></span></span>

## Fairyland, Elf-Arrows and Shape-Shifting Claims

The fairy material is the heart of Gowdie’s importance for folklore. According to the heritage account from Spirit: Stories, using material connected with Nairn Museum, Gowdie claimed she travelled to Elfane, or Fairyland, where she saw elf-boys making flint arrows sharpened by the Devil to be used against people.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://discoverhighlandsandislands.scot/en/story/isobel-gowdie-witch-of-auldearn" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: discoverhighlandsandislands.scot">[Discover Highlands and Islands]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">discoverhighlandsandislands.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Discover Highlands and Islands Isobel Gowdie</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Discover Highlands and Islands Isobel Gowdie</span></span></span> In the Survey of Scottish Witchcraft entry, the case includes references to a Fairy Queen dressed in white and brown and a Fairy King described in human-like terms.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://witches.hca.ed.ac.uk/case/C/EGD/1558" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: witches.hca.ed.ac.uk">[witches.hca.ed.ac.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">witches.hca.ed.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">the case of Issobell GowdieHer confessions in Pitcairn are too detailed to be able to put all the information in this case. Elf-arrows are especially revealing because they connect court testimony with older explanations for sudden illness, wasting, pain or death. In Scottish and wider British tradition, small stone arrowheads were sometimes interpreted as fairy weapons. Gowdie’s account gives that belief a witch-trial twist: the arrows are associated with fairies, handled under demonic authority, and used by witches to harm humans or animals. A scholarly chapter on Gowdie’s elf-arrow material describes the Auldearn confessions as among Scotland’s most extraordinary witchcraft records and singles out the elf-arrow passages as especially striking.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1057/9781137355942_9" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: link.springer.com">[Springer Link]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">link.springer.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Link&#x27;We mey shoot them dead at our pleasur&#x27;: Isobel Gowdie, Elf</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Link&#x27;We mey shoot them dead at our pleasur&#x27;: Isobel Gowdie, Elf</span></span></span>

The shape-shifting claims are just as memorable. Gowdie said that she and others could become animals, including a hare, cat and jackdaw. In later summaries of the case, one of the best-known episodes has her in hare form, chased by dogs, escaping from house to house before regaining human shape.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isobel_Gowdie" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Isobel Gowdie</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Isobel Gowdie</span></span></span> This does not need to be read as a literal event to matter. It shows how witchcraft fear overlapped with rural animal lore: the hare in particular often appears in British and Irish witch traditions as an animal associated with milk-theft, escape, disguise and uncanny female power.

The confessions also contain charms and spoken formulae, including verses for transformation and harmful magic. For a modern reader, these are among the most haunting parts of the record because they feel close to oral tradition: short, rhythmic, memorable, and tied to action. But they survive because they passed through a legal record, not because they were collected gently as songs or tales. That tension is part of their power.

## A Witch, a Victim, a Visionary — or All Three?

The hardest question is what to make of Isobel Gowdie herself. The safe answer is that she was an accused woman whose recorded words were shaped by extreme danger. Whether she believed all, some or none of what she confessed cannot be known with certainty. The records give us a voice, but that voice comes through a hostile legal and religious process.

Some older readings treated Gowdie as evidence for a real surviving witch-cult. Margaret Murray famously used material from witch trials to argue for an organised pre-Christian cult behind European witchcraft, but that theory is now widely rejected by historians. The Survey of Scottish Witchcraft’s warning is therefore important: Gowdie’s case helped create modern ideas about covens of thirteen and spectacular witch meetings, but it should not be treated as a normal sample of Scottish witchcraft evidence.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://witches.hca.ed.ac.uk/case/C/EGD/1558" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: witches.hca.ed.ac.uk">[witches.hca.ed.ac.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">witches.hca.ed.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">the case of Issobell GowdieHer confessions in Pitcairn are too detailed to be able to put all the information in this case. More recent scholarship has been interested in the confessions as story, performance, trauma, belief and interrogation. Emma Wilby’s study, *The Visions of Isobel Gowdie*, is widely discussed because it takes seriously the possibility that fairy belief, visionary experience and popular spirituality helped shape Gowdie’s testimony, while reviewers have also noted the need to weigh that against the pressures of prosecution and elite demonological ideas.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.jstor.org/stable/jj.3485506" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: jstor.org">[JSTOR]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">jstor.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on jstor.org.</span></span></span> A review in H-Net describes the confessions as linking fairy and demonic realms with unusual detail, offering a view into the meeting point of “high” and “low” belief in the period.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://rmtrain.h-net.org/reviews/showrev.php?id=31976" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: rmtrain.h-net.org">[rmtrain.h-net.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">rmtrain.h-net.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">H-Net ReviewsIn the summer of 1662, Isobel Gowdie of the parish of Auldearn in northeastern Scotland was convicted for witchcraft, bringi...</span></span></span>

For public folklore, the most useful approach is not to force Gowdie into one role. She can be understood as:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **An accused woman**, caught in a system where witchcraft was a capital offence.
* **A storyteller**, whose recorded speech contains unusually elaborate supernatural scenes.
* **A possible bearer of local belief**, because the confessions preserve fairy and charm material tied to Auldearn’s landscape.
* **A victim of prosecution**, whether or not every detail of pressure, imprisonment or final punishment can be reconstructed.
* **A modern symbol**, retold in music, fiction, heritage writing and local ghost tradition.

</div>

That combination is why she still haunts Auldearn more powerfully than a simpler “witch legend” would. She is not only a character in a tale; she is a real woman known through documents that are both precious and morally disturbing.

## What Happened After the Confessions?

The ending of Gowdie’s story is uncertain. Many modern accounts assume she was executed, because execution was the usual outcome for many convicted witches in Scotland, but the surviving record does not provide a simple, complete execution entry. The Survey of Scottish Witchcraft trial page for Issobell Gowdie records no trial details in the table, while the case page points researchers towards the printed documents because of the exceptional length and complexity of the confessions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://witches.hca.ed.ac.uk/trial/T/JO/1001" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: witches.hca.ed.ac.uk">[witches.hca.ed.ac.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">witches.hca.ed.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on ed.ac.uk.</span></span></span>

This uncertainty matters. It is tempting for folklore retellings to close the story with a dramatic burning, but careful history has to distinguish likelihood from proof. The Scottish legal context was deadly, and the case proceeded far enough for authorities to treat the confessions as serious material. Yet the absence of a surviving final record means that a responsible account should say “probably executed” only when it is presented as probability, not as a documented scene.

The uncertainty has also shaped modern retellings. Gowdie can be remembered as martyr, visionary, witch, healer, storyteller or ghost partly because the archive leaves gaps around her life before the case and around her final fate. Those gaps are not a licence to invent freely, but they do explain why artists and local writers continue to return to her.

## Why the Confessions Matter for Fairy Folklore

Gowdie’s confessions are among the clearest examples of how fairy belief could survive inside a witch-trial document. In many popular accounts, “witches” and “fairies” are treated as separate folklore categories. Auldearn shows that early modern people could imagine them as connected. Fairies might have their own rulers and realm; witches might travel there; elf-arrows might be made or sharpened there; the Devil might intrude into that world and redirect its powers towards maleficium, meaning harmful magic.

This makes the case valuable for understanding Nairnshire’s mythic landscape. The important point is not simply that “fairies were believed in”. It is that fairy belief was useful for explaining misfortune. A sick cow, a wasting child, a sudden pain, a death, a grudge against a minister or laird — such events could be pulled into a story of invisible attack. Gowdie’s testimony gives names, places and mechanisms to that fear.

It also shows how church space could become supernatural space in the imagination of witchcraft. A scholarly article on the witches’ sabbath in Scotland notes Gowdie’s claimed meetings in Auldearn kirk and Nairn kirkyard as examples of accused witches placing gatherings in churches and churchyards, places that were central, recognisable and symbolically charged.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://journals.socantscot.org/index.php/psas/article/download/9787/9754" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journals.socantscot.org">[Antiquaries of Scotland Journals]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journals.socantscot.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Antiquaries of Scotland Journals The Witches&#x27; sabbath in scotland</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Antiquaries of Scotland Journals The Witches&#x27; sabbath in scotland</span></span></span> In Auldearn, the parish kirk becomes not only a Christian building but the stage for renunciation, gathering and fear.

For readers following Nairnshire folklore on the ground, this is the key: Gowdie’s Fairyland is not detached from local geography. It is imagined through nearby hills, kirks, kirkyards and farm routes. The supernatural sits close to the ordinary.

## Modern Retellings and the Green Lady of Auldearn

Modern Auldearn has inherited Gowdie in several forms. Heritage sources connected with Nairn Museum present her as one of the area’s major supernatural and historical figures, while local and popular accounts often call her the “Witch of Auldearn”.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://discoverhighlandsandislands.scot/en/story/isobel-gowdie-witch-of-auldearn" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: discoverhighlandsandislands.scot">[Discover Highlands and Islands]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">discoverhighlandsandislands.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Discover Highlands and Islands Isobel Gowdie</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Discover Highlands and Islands Isobel Gowdie</span></span></span> Some modern retellings also link her to a “green lady” ghost said to appear around Auldearn and Nairn, though this belongs to later legend and local storytelling rather than to the 1662 trial evidence itself.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://discoverhighlandsandislands.scot/en/story/isobel-gowdie-witch-of-auldearn" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: discoverhighlandsandislands.scot">[Discover Highlands and Islands]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">discoverhighlandsandislands.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Discover Highlands and Islands Isobel Gowdie</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Discover Highlands and Islands Isobel Gowdie</span></span></span>

That distinction matters. The 1662 confessions are documentary evidence of accusation, interrogation and recorded belief. The Green Lady is a later ghost tradition that shows how Gowdie has been absorbed into haunted heritage. Both are part of Nairnshire’s folklore now, but they are not the same kind of source.

Gowdie has also travelled far beyond Nairnshire. James MacMillan’s orchestral work *The Confession of Isobel Gowdie*, written in 1990, helped turn her story into a modern act of remembrance as well as a historical subject.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Confession_of_Isobel_Gowdie" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Confession of Isobel Gowdie</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Confession of Isobel Gowdie</span></span></span> Recent heritage events and fiction continue to revisit her, often with an emphasis on women’s history, injustice and the emotional force of the confessions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://museumsandheritagehighland.org.uk/event/unforgotten-highland-women-isobel-gowdie-auldearn" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: museumsandheritagehighland.org.uk">[museumsandheritagehighland.org.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">museumsandheritagehighland.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on museumsandheritagehighland.org.uk.</span></span></span>

This modern afterlife can enrich the story, provided it does not flatten it. Gowdie was not simply a Halloween witch, a fantasy heroine or a confirmed folk-healer wronged by cartoon villains. She was a real accused woman whose words were preserved in a record made by people with power over her life. The best retellings keep that discomfort in view.

## Reading Auldearn Without Sensationalising It

The temptation with Isobel Gowdie is to overstate everything: to call her Scotland’s “queen of witches”, to treat the confessions as literal autobiography, or to turn Auldearn into a simple dark-tourism stop. The more interesting reading is quieter and stronger. Auldearn matters because the records let us see how a small Nairnshire parish could become the centre of a vast imagined world: Devil, fairies, elf-boys, charms, animal bodies, night meetings and neighbourly fear.

The case is also a reminder that folklore is not always harmless entertainment. Some of the most vivid traditions survive because they were written down during moments of accusation and punishment. Gowdie’s confessions preserve fairy belief, but they also preserve the danger of a society in which supernatural explanations could help condemn real people.

Within Nairnshire’s folklore map, Isobel Gowdie belongs beside haunted castles, coastal customs and vanished settlements, but her case has a sharper documentary edge. It is a story of place, belief and power. Auldearn’s fairy-witch confessions still haunt the county because they sit exactly on that border: between oral tradition and court record, between Fairyland and parish life, between a woman’s voice and the system that captured it.
