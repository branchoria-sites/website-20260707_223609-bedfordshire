## Introduction

Dinas Emrys, a wooded rocky hill above the Glaslyn valley near Beddgelert in historic [Caernarfonshire]({{ 'caernarfonshire/' | relative_url }}), is the place most closely tied to the Welsh red dragon legend. In the best-known story, King Vortigern tries to build a fortress on the hill, but its walls fail again and again. A prophetic boy, later identified with Myrddin Emrys or Merlin, reveals the cause: beneath the site lie a pool and two dragons, one red and one white. When the dragons fight, the red dragon eventually defeats or drives away the white one, turning a local hill in Snowdonia into a symbolic birthplace for the dragon of Wales. National Trust and Visit Wales retell the legend in just this landscape setting, linking Dinas Emrys with Vortigern, Merlin, the hidden lake and the red dragon now seen on the Welsh flag.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nationaltrust.org.uk/visit/wales/craflwyn-and-beddgelert/discover-the-legend-of-two-dragons" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nationaltrust.org.uk">[National Trust]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nationaltrust.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Trust Discover the legend of two dragons&#124;Craflwyn &#124; National Trust</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Trust Discover the legend of two dragons&#124;Craflwyn &#124; National Trust</span></span></span>

The power of the tradition lies in the meeting of three things: a dramatic medieval prophecy, a real archaeological site with ramparts, a pool and later castle remains, and a national emblem that still carries emotional force. Dinas Emrys is therefore not simply “a dragon place”. It is one of Caernarfonshire’s clearest examples of a local landscape becoming a stage for national myth.

## Why Dinas Emrys matters in Caernarfonshire folklore

Dinas Emrys stands near Beddgelert, above the southern end of Llyn Dinas and close to the old mountain routes of Snowdonia. Although visitors today will usually see it described as being in Gwynedd or Eryri, its folklore belongs strongly to the historic county of Caernarfonshire: a county where mountain, lake, ruined fort, princely memory and supernatural story often meet in the same place.

The hill itself is modest compared with the surrounding mountains, but that is part of its effect. National Trust’s walking guide presents it as a route through oak woodland and past waterfalls to a summit with a square tower and defensive ramparts associated with the princes of Gwynedd. The same visitor route tells walkers they are climbing to a “legendary hill” where Merlin once trod and where a dragon still sleeps.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nationaltrust.org.uk/visit/wales/craflwyn-and-beddgelert/the-legendary-trail-of-dinas-emrys" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nationaltrust.org.uk">[National Trust]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nationaltrust.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Trust The legendary trail of Dinas Emrys &#124; Wales &#124; National Trust</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Trust The legendary trail of Dinas Emrys &#124; Wales &#124; National Trust</span></span></span>

For folklore readers, that layered experience matters. Dinas Emrys is not a story floating free of place. The legend attaches to a particular climb, a particular summit, and visible remains. The ruined tower and ramparts do not prove the dragons, but they make the narrative feel rooted. A visitor can stand where the fortress was said to fail, look down towards the valley, and understand why a medieval storyteller might choose this hill as a place of concealment, prophecy and contested power.

The site also connects local Caernarfonshire tradition with the wider Welsh red dragon. Visit Wales describes the young Merlin explaining that the red dragon represented the native Britons, while the white dragon represented the invading Saxons; it then notes that the red dragon has become a symbol seen on national sports shirts, exports, town flags and public life.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.wales.com/about/history-and-heritage/why-there-dragon-welsh-flag" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wales.com">[Wales]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wales.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The legend of the Welsh dragon &#124; Visit Wales</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The legend of the Welsh dragon &#124; Visit Wales</span></span></span> In other words, Dinas Emrys is where a county place and a national emblem are made to explain each other.

## Vortigern’s failing fortress

The central story begins with failure. Vortigern chooses Dinas Emrys for a stronghold, but each attempt to build it collapses. In National Trust’s modern retelling, his masons return each morning to find their work undone and the walls fallen. Vortigern’s advisers then propose a blood sacrifice: the ground should be sprinkled with the blood of a fatherless or otherworldly child.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nationaltrust.org.uk/visit/wales/craflwyn-and-beddgelert/discover-the-legend-of-two-dragons" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nationaltrust.org.uk">[National Trust]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nationaltrust.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Trust Discover the legend of two dragons&#124;Craflwyn &#124; National Trust</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Trust Discover the legend of two dragons&#124;Craflwyn &#124; National Trust</span></span></span>

That grim detail is important because it shows the legend before it becomes a cheerful flag origin story. The tale is about a ruler whose power is unstable, whose building project cannot take root, and whose counsellors reach for violence when they do not understand the ground beneath them. The child’s role is not simply to perform magic; he exposes the ignorance of the king’s advisers and replaces sacrifice with interpretation.

The medieval version preserved in the *Historia Brittonum* gives the episode its sharp prophetic meaning. In the Fordham translation, the boy identifies two sleeping serpents, one white and one red, which begin to struggle. At first the white one appears stronger, but the red one recovers and expels it. The boy then interprets the pool as the world, the tent as the kingdom, the two serpents as dragons, the red as “your dragon” and the white as the dragon of the people occupying much of Britain. The prophecy ends with the claim that “our people” will rise and drive away the Saxons.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://sourcebooks.web.fordham.edu/basis/nennius-full.asp" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sourcebooks.web.fordham.edu">[Internet History Sourcebooks]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sourcebooks.web.fordham.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on fordham.edu.</span></span></span>

The story therefore works on several levels at once. On the surface, it explains why Vortigern’s fortress will not stand. Politically, it turns a building problem into a prophecy of Britons and Saxons. Locally, it fixes that prophecy to a hill in Caernarfonshire. Symbolically, it gives the red dragon a role as the apparently weaker power that survives, recovers and ultimately prevails.

## Myrddin Emrys and the hidden dragons

In modern Welsh and Arthurian retellings, the prophetic boy is usually called Merlin, or Myrddin Emrys. The name helps explain the place-name Dinas Emrys: National Trust’s version says that Vortigern’s castle was finally built and named in honour of Myrddin Emrys after the dragons were revealed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nationaltrust.org.uk/visit/wales/craflwyn-and-beddgelert/discover-the-legend-of-two-dragons" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nationaltrust.org.uk">[National Trust]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nationaltrust.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Trust Discover the legend of two dragons&#124;Craflwyn &#124; National Trust</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Trust Discover the legend of two dragons&#124;Craflwyn &#124; National Trust</span></span></span>

The older textual picture is more complicated. The *Historia Brittonum* gives the boy’s name as Ambrose, with a British form rendered in translation as Embresguletic, and identifies him as the son of a Roman consul.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://sourcebooks.web.fordham.edu/basis/nennius-full.asp" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sourcebooks.web.fordham.edu">[Internet History Sourcebooks]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sourcebooks.web.fordham.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on fordham.edu.</span></span></span> Later tradition links or blends this prophetic Ambrose with Merlin, producing the familiar Myrddin Emrys figure of popular retellings. For a public folklore page, the useful distinction is this: “Merlin at Dinas Emrys” is not a random modern invention, but the result of medieval storytelling layers in which Ambrose, Emrys and Merlin are gradually brought together.

The hidden dragons also have a prehistory in Welsh narrative. The tale of Lludd and Llefelys, known from the medieval Welsh story world associated with the *Mabinogion*, gives an earlier explanation for how the dragons came to be confined. In that story, Britain is afflicted by a terrifying scream each May Eve, caused by a red dragon fighting a foreign white dragon. Lludd is advised to trap the dragons, make them sleep, and bury them at the safest place in the island, later identified as Dinas Emrys.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lludd_and_Llefelys" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Lludd and Llefelys</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Lludd and Llefelys</span></span></span>

This makes Dinas Emrys more than the scene of a single revelation. In one strand, the hill is the place where Vortigern discovers the dragons. In another, it is the secure hiding place where they had already been buried. The result is a powerful folklore pattern: dangerous supernatural forces are concealed in the land, and later rulers disturb them at their peril.

The colours carry the political force of the tale. The red dragon belongs to the Britons; the white dragon belongs to the Saxons. The white dragon may dominate at first, but the red dragon survives and wins. This is why the legend has endured: it turns defeat, occupation and uncertainty into a story of delayed recovery. The hill becomes a prophetic theatre in which the future of a people is acted out by creatures hidden under the ground.

## What archaeology can and cannot prove

Dinas Emrys is unusually interesting because the legend is attached to a real and archaeologically complex site. The Gatehouse Record, drawing on archaeological and Royal Commission material, describes Dinas Emrys as a complex stone-walled fort on a volcanic outcrop at the south end of Nant Gwynant. It notes excavations in 1910 and 1954–56, with evidence for Iron Age, Roman and early medieval occupation, while some surviving stonework appears medieval.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gatehouse-gazetteer.info/Welshsites/100.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gatehouse-gazetteer.info">[Gatehouse Gazetteer]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gatehouse-gazetteer.info</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Gatehouse Gazetteer Dinas Emrys Castle (The Gatehouse Record</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Gatehouse Gazetteer Dinas Emrys Castle (The Gatehouse Record</span></span></span>

The Atlas of Hillforts records the site as a volcanic outcrop and notes that excavations by H. N. Savory showed phases beginning with a possible pre-Roman palisade, followed by early Roman material, later Roman ceramics and glassware, and early medieval indicators including imported amphora and a pottery sherd stamped with a chi-rho symbol. It also identifies a later rectangular stone tower or keep, probably belonging to the period of Llywelyn ap Iorwerth, who died in 1240.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://hillforts.arch.ox.ac.uk/records/WA1729.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: hillforts.arch.ox.ac.uk">[Atlas of Hillforts]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">hillforts.arch.ox.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on ox.ac.uk.</span></span></span>

This evidence does not prove that Vortigern stood there, that Merlin spoke there, or that dragons slept under the hill. Folklore should not be forced into archaeology like a key into the wrong lock. What the evidence does show is more subtle and arguably more interesting: Dinas Emrys was not an empty fantasy location. It was a defended and reused place with a long history, including periods close enough to the post-Roman world to make later legendary association understandable.

The pool is the most tempting point of contact between story and site. Modern retellings often note that excavation revealed a lake or pool and fortress remains, echoing the legend’s hidden water beneath the failing stronghold. National Trust’s page says archaeologists discovered a lake and fortress ruins dating to Vortigern’s time, while Visit Wales similarly says excavation showed evidence of a lake and fortress.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nationaltrust.org.uk/visit/wales/craflwyn-and-beddgelert/discover-the-legend-of-two-dragons" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nationaltrust.org.uk">[National Trust]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nationaltrust.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Trust Discover the legend of two dragons&#124;Craflwyn &#124; National Trust</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Trust Discover the legend of two dragons&#124;Craflwyn &#124; National Trust</span></span></span> The more cautious archaeological summaries are better for interpretation: Gatehouse notes “slight remains beside a pool” and says the dating of the remains is not conclusive, with late prehistoric, Roman, sub-Roman and medieval periods all represented.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gatehouse-gazetteer.info/Welshsites/100.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gatehouse-gazetteer.info">[Gatehouse Gazetteer]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gatehouse-gazetteer.info</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Gatehouse Gazetteer Dinas Emrys Castle (The Gatehouse Record</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Gatehouse Gazetteer Dinas Emrys Castle (The Gatehouse Record</span></span></span>

That distinction matters. The archaeology strengthens the site’s atmosphere and plausibility as a legendary focus, but it does not turn the medieval prophecy into a historical report. The honest reading is that Dinas Emrys contains enough real ancient and medieval texture to explain why the legend clung to it, while still leaving the dragons firmly in the realm of tradition, symbolism and story.

## The red dragon from hill legend to national emblem

The Dinas Emrys tale explains why the red dragon feels so deeply Welsh, but the modern flag has its own later history. Museum Wales describes the red dragon as the heraldic symbol of Wales and says it was probably introduced into Britain by Roman legions; medieval Welsh poets then used dragon imagery to praise leaders for bravery. The museum also notes that the dragon formed part of Tudor arms between 1485 and 1603, reappeared as the royal badge for Wales in 1807, and was officially recognised as part of the Welsh national flag in 1959.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://museum.wales/blog/1182/National-emblems-FAQ/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: museum.wales">[Museum Wales]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">museum.wales</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Wales National emblems (FAQ) &#124; Museum Wales</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wales National emblems (FAQ) &#124; Museum Wales</span></span></span>

Visit Wales gives the more public-facing version of the same development: the red dragon was used as a military standard, later associated with fifth-century Welsh kings, and was given new prominence by Henry Tudor, who used a red dragon on a white and green background after his victory at Bosworth in 1485. In 1959, Queen Elizabeth II declared that the red dragon on a green and white flag should be flown on government buildings in Wales.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.wales.com/about/history-and-heritage/why-there-dragon-welsh-flag" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wales.com">[Wales]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wales.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The legend of the Welsh dragon &#124; Visit Wales</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The legend of the Welsh dragon &#124; Visit Wales</span></span></span>

This means the Welsh red dragon did not travel in a straight line from Dinas Emrys to the modern flag. The hill legend is one vital strand. Heraldry, royal badges, Tudor politics, patriotic societies and twentieth-century official recognition are others. Dinas Emrys gives the emblem a mythic origin story; later history gives it public, heraldic and national form.

For Caernarfonshire, that distinction is useful rather than disappointing. The county does not need to claim that the whole flag was “invented” on one hill. Its stronger claim is that Dinas Emrys preserves one of the most memorable narrative explanations for why the red dragon stands for Wales: a small red creature, nearly overcome, recovers its strength and drives out the white invader.

## Treasure, princes and modern retellings

Dinas Emrys also carries motifs beyond the red and white dragons. Local tourism retellings connect the hill with hidden treasure, a cave, a ringing bell and conditions placed on whoever might find what is concealed there. Beddgelert Tourism summarises the dragon story through Vortigern, Myrddin, the hidden pool and the prophecy, then extends the site’s legendary atmosphere through tales of treasure and princely association.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.beddgelerttourism.com/Dinas-Emrys-Princes/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: beddgelerttourism.com">[Beddgelert Tourism]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">beddgelerttourism.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on beddgelerttourism.com.</span></span></span>

The treasure motif fits the hill perfectly. In British and Welsh local tradition, treasure is often guarded, hidden, morally tested or available only to the right person at the right moment. At Dinas Emrys, that pattern echoes the dragons themselves: something powerful lies beneath or within the hill, but it is not freely available. The land keeps secrets. Digging into it may reveal truth, danger, prophecy or fear.

The visible medieval remains add another layer. Gatehouse records an oblong stone keep at the highest point, 13 metres by 11 metres, and connects Dinas Emrys with a small group of early stone-built castles in Gwynedd from the unsettled period after the death of Owain Gwynedd in 1170. These were expressions of princely power and lordship rather than simply frontier defences against outsiders.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gatehouse-gazetteer.info/Welshsites/100.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gatehouse-gazetteer.info">[Gatehouse Gazetteer]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gatehouse-gazetteer.info</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Gatehouse Gazetteer Dinas Emrys Castle (The Gatehouse Record</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Gatehouse Gazetteer Dinas Emrys Castle (The Gatehouse Record</span></span></span>

That princely layer helps explain why the site continues to feel politically charged. The dragon legend speaks of Britons and Saxons; the stone remains speak of Gwynedd’s medieval rulers; the modern flag speaks of Welsh identity. Different periods do not collapse into one another, but they do stack up on the same hill.

## How to read the legend today

The best way to read Dinas Emrys is neither as literal proof of dragons nor as “just a made-up story”. It is a layered tradition in which medieval political prophecy, local topography, archaeology, princely history and modern national symbolism all meet.

As folklore, the story answers a question people still ask: why does Wales have a red dragon? The Dinas Emrys answer is vivid and memorable: because beneath a troubled fortress, a red dragon fought a white dragon and prevailed. As landscape legend, it answers a different question: why does this hill matter? Because its pool, ramparts and ruins have been made into the physical setting for one of Wales’s most famous symbolic battles.

For Caernarfonshire, Dinas Emrys is one of the county’s great mythic landscapes. It belongs with other local places where story and terrain are inseparable: Beddgelert’s faithful hound, Snowdon’s giant and Arthurian associations, holy wells, old passes and haunted or enchanted uplands. But Dinas Emrys has a special reach. It turns a hill near Beddgelert into the place where Welsh resilience can be imagined as a creature sleeping under stone, waking when disturbed, and returning to its lair after victory.
