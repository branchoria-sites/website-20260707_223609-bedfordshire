## Introduction

Fairy lore in Eryri, within historic [Caernarfonshire]({{ 'caernarfonshire/' | relative_url }}), is not just a matter of pretty lakeside enchantment. It is a way of explaining why certain places feel charged: the edge of Llyn Cwellyn, the lonely western tracks up Yr Wyddfa, the lake road between Caernarfon and Beddgelert, and the threshold between safe household life and open mountain country. The best-attested local pattern is simple but powerful: fairies appear near water, dancing places and night roads; humans break rules, misunderstand signs or test the wrong child; and the landscape answers by taking people out of ordinary time, ordinary luck or ordinary kinship. Nineteenth-century printed sources preserve the Llyn Cwellyn fairy-wife tale, while modern Eryri visitor material still places named routes, lakes and hazards beside legendary memory.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/stream/cambrian05unkngoog/cambrian05unkngoog_djvu.txt" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[archive.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on archive.org.</span></span></span>

This page keeps to the Caernarfonshire centre of gravity. Eryri is now a national-park name, and many modern sources use Gwynedd or North Wales, but the lake-and-path traditions around Llyn Cwellyn, Rhyd Ddu, Beddgelert and Yr Wyddfa sit naturally inside the old county setting. Wikishire places Llyn Cwellyn in Caernarfonshire on the west side of Snowdon, and also describes Snowdonia, or Eryri, as a mountain region almost wholly in Caernarfonshire, even though the national park now extends more widely.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://wikishire.co.uk/wiki/Llyn_Cwellyn" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wikishire.co.uk">[Wikishire]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wikishire.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Llyn Cwellyn</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Llyn Cwellyn</span></span></span>

## Why lakes and paths attract fairy stories

The striking thing about Eryri’s fairy lore is how often it lives at the edge of things. Lakes are neither house nor field; mountain paths are neither settlement nor wilderness; dusk and mist blur the ordinary rules of travel. In Welsh fairy tradition, these are exactly the places where a person may meet the otherworld: a lake woman, a dancing company, a misleading cry, or a child who is not quite the child the family thought it had. Wirt Sikes’s nineteenth-century collection, *British Goblins*, describes Welsh fairies in several forms, including lake women and mountain beings, and gives the mountain fairies a specifically dangerous role: they haunt lonely roads and lead night-wanderers astray.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/files/34704/34704-h/34704-h.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Project Gutenberg eBook of British Goblins: Welsh Folk-lore, Fairy Mythology, Legends and Traditions, by Wirt Sikes...</span></span></span>

For Caernarfonshire readers, this matters because the tradition fits the actual geography of western Eryri. Llyn Cwellyn lies on the route between Caernarfon and Beddgelert, with the Snowdon Ranger path beginning on its shore. Modern walkers know this as a practical landscape of car parks, buses, footpaths and weather warnings; older folklore imagines the same terrain as a moral and supernatural testing ground. The old story does not replace the physical danger of the mountain, but it gives that danger a narrative shape: do not wander carelessly, do not break a condition, do not assume the open land obeys household rules.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://eryri.gov.wales/walk/snowdon-ranger/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: eryri.gov.wales">[Eryri National Park]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">eryri.gov.wales</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Eryri National Park Snowdon Ranger &#124; Walks and Routes Eryri National Park</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Eryri National Park Snowdon Ranger &#124; Walks and Routes Eryri National Park</span></span></span>

## Llyn Cwellyn and lake-edge fairy places

Llyn Cwellyn is the strongest local anchor for this subtopic. A nineteenth-century account in *The Cambrian* describes it as an important lake in the Snowdon district, over a mile long, near Castell Cidwm, and “familiar in various forms” to students of myth. Its tale begins with fairies dancing at night near the lake. A young man hides in a thicket, rushes out and seizes a beautiful fairy woman. The rest vanish; she is taken home and eventually agrees to marry him, but only on one condition: if he ever strikes her with cold iron, she will leave him for ever.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/stream/cambrian05unkngoog/cambrian05unkngoog_djvu.txt" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on archive.org.</span></span></span>

The end of the tale is as important as the enchantment. The man and fairy wife have children, but one day he throws a missile at a horse and accidentally hits her. The broken condition sends her back out of mortal life. She disappears, and only a later whispered message in the breeze remains, asking that clothing be given to the children so they will not suffer cold. This is a classic fairy-wife structure: a human marriage crosses the boundary between worlds, but one breach of a taboo returns the fairy woman to the lake or otherworld.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/stream/cambrian05unkngoog/cambrian05unkngoog_djvu.txt" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on archive.org.</span></span></span>

The story is not unique to Llyn Cwellyn, and that is part of its value. Sikes gives the famous Llyn y Fan Fach version in Carmarthenshire, where a lake woman marries a farmer, brings cattle from the water, and leaves after the husband breaks the condition attached to their marriage. The Caernarfonshire version is more compact, but it belongs to the same Welsh pattern: a lake is not merely scenery; it is the opening through which wealth, marriage, beauty and loss can enter ordinary life.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/files/34704/34704-h/34704-h.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Project Gutenberg eBook of British Goblins: Welsh Folk-lore, Fairy Mythology, Legends and Traditions, by Wirt Sikes...</span></span></span>

Within Eryri, the Llyn Cwellyn tale also sits beside other charged lake traditions rather than replacing them. Eryri National Park’s own visitor material points readers to mythical lake landscapes such as Llyn Tegid and to the lakes and paths around Yr Wyddfa, while the Snowdon Ranger route page names Llyn Cwellyn as the path’s starting point and links the route to local legend around Castell Cidwm and Llyn Ffynnon y Gwas. The folklore cluster is therefore not a single isolated anecdote, but a way of reading lakes, crags and upland paths as a connected legendary landscape.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://eryri.gov.wales/visit/plan-your-visit/guides/5-walks-in-eryri-steeped-in-mythology/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: eryri.gov.wales">[Eryri National Park]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">eryri.gov.wales</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Eryri National Park5 walks in Eryri steeped in mythology &#124; Eryri National Park</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Eryri National Park5 walks in Eryri steeped in mythology &#124; Eryri National Park</span></span></span>

## Changelings, wise women and household tests

Not all Eryri fairy lore belongs outside on the mountain. Some of its most unsettling material brings the fairies into the home, especially through anxieties about babies, illness and replacement children. The Carneddau Landscape Partnership, working in the northern Eryri cultural landscape, summarises a local changeling tradition in which the fairies were believed to steal human babies and leave fairy children, called changelings, in their place. In one local story, a wise woman advises a mother to fill an eggshell with beer and watch what the suspicious children say.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://partneriaethcarneddau.cymru/myths-and-legends/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: partneriaethcarneddau.cymru">[Carneddau Landscape Partnership]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">partneriaethcarneddau.cymru</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Carneddau Landscape Partnership Myths and legends</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Carneddau Landscape Partnership Myths and legends</span></span></span>

The logic of the eggshell test is that the changeling betrays itself by speaking with impossible age and knowledge. The tiny container is deliberately absurd: no normal child would read it as a meaningful domestic act, but a fairy child, old in experience despite its infant form, comments on the strangeness. The Carneddau version says the mother then throws the changelings into the nearest river and the human children are returned before she reaches home. To modern readers this is a disturbing story, but as folklore it gives shape to older fears about sickness, infant development, maternal uncertainty and the terrifying possibility that the household has been invaded by something no ordinary care can mend.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://partneriaethcarneddau.cymru/myths-and-legends/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: partneriaethcarneddau.cymru">[Carneddau Landscape Partnership]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">partneriaethcarneddau.cymru</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Carneddau Landscape Partnership Myths and legends</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Carneddau Landscape Partnership Myths and legends</span></span></span>

Sikes records the wider Welsh changeling framework in similar terms. He notes protective measures against fairy theft, including iron objects such as a knife or tongs near the cradle, and says baptism was widely treated as a safeguard because the unbaptised infant was especially vulnerable in popular belief. These details matter because they show the mechanism of the tradition. Fairy danger was not random: it had rules, thresholds and counter-rules. Iron could break enchantment; pious words or ritual status could protect; a wise woman knew how to force the hidden truth into speech.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/files/34704/34704-h/34704-h.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Project Gutenberg eBook of British Goblins: Welsh Folk-lore, Fairy Mythology, Legends and Traditions, by Wirt Sikes...</span></span></span>

Placed beside Llyn Cwellyn, the changeling material shows two halves of the same belief world. At the lake, a man tries to bring a fairy woman into ordinary marriage and loses her by breaking a condition. In the changeling tale, fairies invade the family line and must be exposed by a domestic test. Both stories are about boundary failure. The trouble begins when the otherworld crosses into the household, and it ends only when the boundary is restored, whether through disappearance, return or a harsh ritual act.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/stream/cambrian05unkngoog/cambrian05unkngoog_djvu.txt" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on archive.org.</span></span></span>

## Rules for crossing uncanny landscapes

The old stories around Eryri do not read like modern walking advice, but they do preserve a folk grammar of caution. Their rules are simple: do not travel carelessly at night; do not chase music or dancing lights away from the path; do not seize what belongs to the otherworld; do not break a taboo once accepted; and do not assume that a person, child or place is exactly what it appears to be. Sikes’s mountain-fairy material is especially clear on this point: the dangerous fairies of the Welsh mountains belong to lonely roads, darkness, fear and misdirection.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/files/34704/34704-h/34704-h.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Project Gutenberg eBook of British Goblins: Welsh Folk-lore, Fairy Mythology, Legends and Traditions, by Wirt Sikes...</span></span></span>

That older grammar still feels legible because Eryri remains a landscape where judgement matters. The Snowdon Ranger path begins at Llyn Cwellyn and is classed by Eryri National Park as hard and strenuous, with navigation skills essential. The route climbs from the lake shore to Moel Cynghorion, Bwlch Cwm Brwynog and then towards the summit of Yr Wyddfa, with steep ground above Clogwyn Du’r Arddu. Modern guidance describes the same broad problem that folklore dramatises: the open mountain is not a casual extension of the road or village.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://eryri.gov.wales/walk/snowdon-ranger/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: eryri.gov.wales">[Eryri National Park]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">eryri.gov.wales</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Eryri National Park Snowdon Ranger &#124; Walks and Routes Eryri National Park</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Eryri National Park Snowdon Ranger &#124; Walks and Routes Eryri National Park</span></span></span>

The point is even sharper across Yr Wyddfa’s wider route network. Eryri National Park says all six main summit routes are challenging, warns against treating “easier” routes as easy, and highlights particular risks such as steep rocky ascents, limited parking and the danger of drifting towards Crib Goch from the Pyg Track. This is not folklore, but it explains why folklore about misleading paths and uncanny mountain presences remains persuasive. A walker who misreads weather, distance, mist or terrain can still be “led astray”, even without believing in fairies.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://eryri.gov.wales/visit/plan-your-visit/guides/yr-wyddfa-choosing-the-right-path/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: eryri.gov.wales">[Eryri National Park]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">eryri.gov.wales</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Eryri National Park Yr Wyddfa: Choosing the right path &#124; Eryri National Park</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Eryri National Park Yr Wyddfa: Choosing the right path &#124; Eryri National Park</span></span></span>

The Met Office mountain forecast for Eryri makes the same point in practical language. Mountain hazards apply at and above 300 metres because conditions are more severe at altitude, and the forecast separates valley weather, summit temperatures, visibility, wind and ground conditions. In folklore, mist and dusk are narrative thresholds; in mountain safety, they are navigation problems. The two ways of speaking are different, but they meet in the same readerly lesson: in Eryri, crossing from lowland into upland changes the rules.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://weather.metoffice.gov.uk/specialist-forecasts/mountain/snowdonia" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: weather.metoffice.gov.uk">[Met Office]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">weather.metoffice.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Met Office Eryri / Snowdonia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Met Office Eryri / Snowdonia</span></span></span>

## How old and well-attested is the tradition?

The safest answer is that Eryri’s fairy-lake and dangerous-path lore is well attested as nineteenth-century and later collected tradition, with older motifs behind it, but not always with a traceable medieval record for each individual site. The Llyn Cwellyn fairy-wife story is printed in a nineteenth-century Welsh diaspora periodical account of “Welsh Inland Lakes”, while Sikes’s *British Goblins* gives a broad nineteenth-century synthesis of Welsh fairy belief, including changelings, lake women and mountain fairies. These sources are valuable, but they are not tape-recorded oral testimony; they are antiquarian and literary filters on oral material.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/stream/cambrian05unkngoog/cambrian05unkngoog_djvu.txt" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[archive.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on archive.org.</span></span></span>

John Rhys’s *Celtic Folklore: Welsh and Manx*, published in 1901, is another major scholarly antiquarian source for Welsh fairy tradition, and modern bibliographies continue to treat it as a central collection. Its importance lies less in proving any single supernatural claim than in showing how widespread and patterned these stories were across Wales and the Isle of Man by the time collectors fixed them in print.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/details/celticfolklorewe01rhys" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on archive.org.</span></span></span>

Modern retellings add a third layer. Eryri National Park’s visitor pages now frame lakes, routes and legendary places for walkers, while local heritage projects such as the Carneddau Landscape Partnership present fairy and changeling stories as part of cultural landscape interpretation. These versions are not “less real” as folklore, but they do a different job. Antiquarian collections try to preserve; tourism and heritage pages help people recognise story in place; walking guidance turns the same landscape into routes, risks and responsible access.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://partneriaethcarneddau.cymru/myths-and-legends/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: partneriaethcarneddau.cymru">[partneriaethcarneddau.cymru]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">partneriaethcarneddau.cymru</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Carneddau Landscape Partnership Myths and legends</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Carneddau Landscape Partnership Myths and legends</span></span></span>

## Why this belongs to Caernarfonshire

Fairy lakes and dangerous paths are a particularly good fit for Caernarfonshire because the historic county is defined by the meeting of settlement, sea road, mountain and lake. Llyn Cwellyn sits on the west side of Yr Wyddfa, between Caernarfon and Beddgelert, and the Snowdon Ranger path gives the lake an enduring role as a starting point for ascent. The old county framing helps prevent the folklore from dissolving into a vague “Snowdonia myth” label: this is not just mountain atmosphere, but a set of stories attached to named Caernarfonshire places.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://wikishire.co.uk/wiki/Llyn_Cwellyn" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wikishire.co.uk">[wikishire.co.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wikishire.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Llyn Cwellyn</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Llyn Cwellyn</span></span></span>

The modern name Eryri also matters. The National Park Authority has moved towards using the Welsh names Eryri and Yr Wyddfa in public-facing material, and its walking pages now use those names as standard. For folklore readers, that change is more than branding. It brings official language closer to the place-name texture in which many of these traditions were preserved: lake names, path names, farm names, crags and passes carry memory in ways that English summaries can flatten.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://eryri.gov.wales/visit/plan-your-visit/guides/yr-wyddfa-how-to-pronounce-snowdon-welsh-name/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: eryri.gov.wales">[Eryri National Park]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">eryri.gov.wales</span><span class="citation-popover-title">yr wyddfa how to pronounce snowdon welsh name</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">yr wyddfa how to pronounce snowdon welsh name</span></span></span>

Within the wider Caernarfonshire folklore branch, this page sits naturally beside [Dinas Emrys]({{ 'dinas-emrys/' | relative_url }}), Beddgelert, Yr Wyddfa giant lore and holy-well traditions. But its mechanism is distinct. Dragon stories make hills national and heroic; [Gelert]({{ 'gelert/' | relative_url }}) makes a village morally memorable; fairy lake and path lore makes ordinary movement risky. It asks a different question: not “Who founded this symbol?” or “What tragedy happened here?”, but “What rules must a person obey when crossing a place that is not fully theirs?”<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://eryri.gov.wales/visit/plan-your-visit/guides/5-walks-in-eryri-steeped-in-mythology/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: eryri.gov.wales">[Eryri National Park]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">eryri.gov.wales</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Eryri National Park5 walks in Eryri steeped in mythology &#124; Eryri National Park</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Eryri National Park5 walks in Eryri steeped in mythology &#124; Eryri National Park</span></span></span>

## How the stories are retold today

Today, most people encounter these traditions through a mixture of walking culture, heritage interpretation, local storytelling and online travel writing. Llyn Cwellyn is visited as a reservoir, viewpoint and route landmark; the Snowdon Ranger path is chosen as a quieter western approach to Yr Wyddfa; and official pages present nearby legends of Castell Cidwm, Llyn Ffynnon y Gwas and the mythic landscapes of Eryri alongside practical route information.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://eryri.gov.wales/walk/snowdon-ranger/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: eryri.gov.wales">[Eryri National Park]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">eryri.gov.wales</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Eryri National Park Snowdon Ranger &#124; Walks and Routes Eryri National Park</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Eryri National Park Snowdon Ranger &#124; Walks and Routes Eryri National Park</span></span></span>

That modern setting can easily soften the fairies into decoration, but the older stories are sharper than that. The fairy wife of Llyn Cwellyn is not simply a romantic lake spirit; she is bound by a condition that exposes the fragility of human possession. The changeling test is not cosy whimsy; it belongs to a frightening world of infant vulnerability and household suspicion. The mountain fairies are not harmless sparkle; they are a way of speaking about disorientation, night travel and the fear of being drawn off the safe road.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/stream/cambrian05unkngoog/cambrian05unkngoog_djvu.txt" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[archive.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on archive.org.</span></span></span>

The best modern retelling keeps both layers visible. It lets visitors enjoy the beauty of Llyn Cwellyn and the drama of the western paths, but it also preserves the older caution: water has depth, paths have consequences, and the mountain is never just a backdrop. In Caernarfonshire folklore, Eryri’s fairies wait where certainty thins — at the lake edge, on the night road, in the mist, and at the moment when a traveller forgets that every crossing has rules.
