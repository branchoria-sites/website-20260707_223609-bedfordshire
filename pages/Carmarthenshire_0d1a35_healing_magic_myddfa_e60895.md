## Introduction

[Carmarthenshire]({{ 'why-does-carmarthenshire-feel-so/' | relative_url }})’s healing magic is unusual because it sits on the border between folklore and paperwork. The Physicians of Myddfai belong to a medieval medical tradition wrapped in the fairy-wife legend of Llyn y Fan Fach: a lake woman, her sons, herbal knowledge, and a family of healers near Myddfai. The Cwrt-y-Cadno cunning folk, especially John Harries and his sons, belong to a much later world of nineteenth-century patients, horoscopes, charms, account books and manuscript magic. Together, they show how “magic” in Carmarthenshire often meant medicine, learned books, reputation and practical help rather than stage-like sorcery. The evidence is not all of one kind: some is medieval manuscript tradition, some is oral legend written down by antiquarians, and some is surviving archival material from working practitioners. That mixture is what makes this corner of Carmarthenshire so distinctive.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK558253/?report=printable" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[nih.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">NCBIMedieval Welsh Medical Texts</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NCBI Bookshelf...</span></span></span>

## Why healing is Carmarthenshire’s most practical magic

The county’s best-known supernatural traditions often lead back to landscape: Carmarthen to Merlin, Llyn y Fan Fach to the [fairy bride]({{ 'fairy-bride/' | relative_url }}), and Myddfai to the healers said to inherit her knowledge. But the Myddfai and Cwrt-y-Cadno traditions are not just picturesque legends. They preserve a local idea of power as useful knowledge: knowing which plant to use, how to read a patient’s complaint, how to draw up a horoscope, how to recover stolen property, or how to write a charm in the right book.

That is why these traditions matter within Carmarthenshire folklore. They are not simply stories about “wizards”. They show a rural society in which healing, literacy, manuscripts, astrology and reputation could overlap. The Myddfai material looks back towards medieval Welsh medical writing and the prestige of Dinefwr. The Harries family of Cwrt-y-Cadno looks forward into the nineteenth century, when patients still consulted cunning folk alongside, or instead of, regular medical practitioners.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK558273/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[NCBI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">NCBIThe Nature of the Corpus</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Medieval Welsh Medical Texts - NCBI Bookshelf...</span></span></span>

The geography is important too. Myddfai lies in north-east Carmarthenshire, close to the Black Mountain and the lake landscape of Llyn y Fan Fach. Cwrt-y-Cadno is in the Cothi valley area, also within Carmarthenshire’s rural interior. These are not interchangeable “Welsh mystical” settings: each tradition is attached to named families, named manuscripts and remembered local places.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.visitwales.com/attraction/visitor-centre/myddfai-community-hall-visitor-centre-550791" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: visitwales.com">[Visit Wales]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">visitwales.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Visit Wales Myddfai Community Hall &amp; Visitor Centre &#124; Visit Wales Visitor Centre</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Attraction Information. ## The Physicians of Myddfai and learned herbal healing

The Physicians of Myddfai are usually introduced through legend. A young man marries a mysterious woman from Llyn y Fan Fach; after a broken condition she returns to the lake, but her sons inherit healing knowledge. In the well-known version discussed by the folklorist Robin Gwyndaf, the mother later appears to her eldest son Rhiwallon, gives him medical prescriptions, and reveals the healing qualities of plants and herbs. The sons, named Rhiwallon’s line in the tradition, become physicians to Rhys Gryg, Lord of Dinefwr.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://isfnr.org/files/Printed.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: isfnr.org">[Folk Narrative Research]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">isfnr.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Folk Narrative Research</span></span></span>

The key point for readers is that the legend and the medical texts are related, but not identical. The fairy-wife story gives the physicians a supernatural origin and ties their skill to the lake landscape. The manuscript tradition gives them learned authority. Modern scholarship is careful about this distinction. Diana Luft’s edition of medieval Welsh medical recipes explains that the corpus traditionally ascribed to the Physicians of Myddfai survives in medieval Welsh medical manuscripts, with the four earliest collections dating from the late fourteenth century. These recipes treat everyday conditions such as toothache, constipation and gout, and they belong to the wider medical tradition of medieval Europe rather than standing outside it as pure folk magic.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK558253/?report=printable" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[NCBI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">NCBIMedieval Welsh Medical Texts</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NCBI Bookshelf...</span></span></span>

That does not make the folklore unimportant. The Myddfai story is powerful because it turns medical inheritance into a local origin myth. It says, in effect, that Carmarthenshire healing knowledge came from both family practice and the otherworldly landscape. The lake woman is not merely a decorative fairy; she is the figure who explains why this family’s knowledge matters and why Myddfai became a name associated with medicine.

The manuscripts also complicate a common oversimplification. Luft notes that the famous preface in the Red Book of Hergest names Rhiwallon the Physician and his sons Cadwgon, Gruffydd and Einion, and places them under Rhys Gryg of Dinefwr Castle in Llandeilo. But she also warns that the preface should not be used to attribute every medical text that follows in the manuscript to the Myddfai family. In other words, the Physicians of Myddfai became a prestigious label, and later readers sometimes stretched that label too far.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK558273/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[NCBI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">NCBIThe Nature of the Corpus</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Medieval Welsh Medical Texts - NCBI Bookshelf...</span></span></span>

## How old is the Myddfai tradition?

The safest answer is that the medical manuscript tradition is medieval, while the best-known fairy-lake story is much later in its recorded form. That distinction matters because it prevents two mistakes: dismissing the whole tradition as modern invention, or treating every detail of the familiar lake legend as demonstrably medieval.

The medical material traditionally linked with the Physicians of Myddfai is preserved in medieval Welsh manuscript contexts. Luft’s modern critical edition places the earliest recipe collections in the late fourteenth century and emphasises their relationship with broader European medical learning. The Myddfai name therefore belongs to a real manuscript tradition, not merely to a tourist tale.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK558253/?report=printable" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[NCBI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">NCBIMedieval Welsh Medical Texts</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NCBI Bookshelf...</span></span></span>

The fairy-wife legend, however, is first recorded in print much later. Gwyndaf states that the Llyn y Fan Fach legend was first printed in 1821 in *The Cambro-Briton*, with the fuller and best-known version appearing in 1861 in John Williams’s introduction to *The Physicians of Myddvai: Meddygon Myddfai*. That 1861 version was based on material written down by William Rees of Tonn, Llandovery, from the oral testimony of three elderly people from Myddfai.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://isfnr.org/files/Printed.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: isfnr.org">[Folk Narrative Research]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">isfnr.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Folk Narrative Research</span></span></span>

This is exactly the kind of layered evidence that makes folklore interesting. The story may preserve older motifs, especially the fairy bride or otherworld woman who leaves a mortal husband, but the form most readers know today is an antiquarian-era written version of local oral tradition. The result is not “false”; it is a tradition whose evidence has to be read in layers.

## John Harries and the Cwrt-y-Cadno cunning folk

Cwrt-y-Cadno gives Carmarthenshire a different kind of healing magic: not a medieval origin legend, but a documented nineteenth-century family practice. John Harries, also known as Shon Harri Shon, was born around 1785 at Pantycoy, Cwrt-y-Cadno, and became one of Wales’s best-known cunning men. The Dictionary of Welsh Biography describes him as an astrologer and medical practitioner, educated at Caio and Haverfordwest, though the details of his medical training remain uncertain. His surviving medical transcripts and accounts show him practising in Cwrt-y-Cadno between 1813 and 1831.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://biography.wales/article/s14-HARR-JOH-1839.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: biography.wales">[Dictionary of Welsh Biography]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">biography.wales</span><span class="citation-popover-title">s14 HARR JOH 1839</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Dictionary of Welsh BiographyHARRIES, JOHN (c.1785 - 1839), astrologer and medical practitioner &#124; Dictionary of Welsh Biography...</span></span></span>

The Harries family’s reputation extended far beyond the immediate valley. People came to them for healing of humans and animals, recovery of lost or stolen goods, charms, fortune-telling, astrology, prophecy, protection against witchcraft and the invocation of benign spirits. That list is important because it shows what “cunning folk” meant in practice. They were not only spell-casters in popular imagination; they were problem-solvers in a world where illness, theft, bad luck and suspected witchcraft could be understood together.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://biography.wales/article/s14-HARR-JOH-1839.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: biography.wales">[Dictionary of Welsh Biography]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">biography.wales</span><span class="citation-popover-title">s14 HARR JOH 1839</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Dictionary of Welsh BiographyHARRIES, JOHN (c.1785 - 1839), astrologer and medical practitioner &#124; Dictionary of Welsh Biography...</span></span></span>

The National Library of Wales describes the Harries family as famous in Wales and in neighbouring English border counties as medical practitioners, surgeons and astrologers. It also records how they were criticised by mainstream religious voices in the nineteenth century. That tension is part of the evidence: the Harrieses were socially significant enough to be consulted, feared, mocked and condemned.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.library.wales/discover-learn/digital-exhibitions/manuscripts/modern-period/john-harries-book-of-incantations" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: library.wales">[National Library of Wales]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">library.wales</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Library of Wales John Harries&#x27; Book of Incantations</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Library of WalesJohn Harries&#x27; Book of Incantations - National Library of Wales...</span></span></span>

John Harries’s sons continued the reputation, though unevenly. Henry Gwynne Harries, who died young in 1849, was also remembered as a doctor and cunning man. Another son, John Harries, who died in 1863, is described by the Dictionary of Welsh Biography as the last of the renowned Cwrt-y-Cadno cunning folk, though apparently less impressive than his father and brother.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.library.wales/discover-learn/digital-exhibitions/manuscripts/modern-period/john-harries-book-of-incantations" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: library.wales">[National Library of Wales]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">library.wales</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Library of Wales John Harries&#x27; Book of Incantations</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Library of WalesJohn Harries&#x27; Book of Incantations - National Library of Wales...</span></span></span>

## The locked book, the storm and the manuscript reality

The most memorable Harries legend is the story of the locked book. John Harries was said to keep a powerful volume hidden away, opening it only once a year in a secluded wood to read incantations and summon spirits. The act of opening it supposedly brought on a severe storm. Later accounts imagined a large spell book bound with an iron chain and three locks.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.library.wales/discover-learn/digital-exhibitions/manuscripts/modern-period/john-harries-book-of-incantations" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: library.wales">[National Library of Wales]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">library.wales</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Library of Wales John Harries&#x27; Book of Incantations</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Library of WalesJohn Harries&#x27; Book of Incantations - National Library of Wales...</span></span></span>

This is classic cunning-folk folklore: the power is not only in the person, but in the book. A secret volume gives the practitioner authority, danger and mystery. Yet the archival record is more complicated. The National Library of Wales notes that family tradition claimed such a book was housed there, but no record of the exact legendary book has been found. A later visitor, J. H. Davies, reported finding an old black book with two locks, roughly the size of a family Bible, but this did not neatly match the storm-raising legend.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.library.wales/discover-learn/digital-exhibitions/manuscripts/modern-period/john-harries-book-of-incantations" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: library.wales">[National Library of Wales]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">library.wales</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Library of Wales John Harries&#x27; Book of Incantations</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Library of WalesJohn Harries&#x27; Book of Incantations - National Library of Wales...</span></span></span>

What does survive is still remarkable. John Harries’s Book of Incantations, NLW MS 11117B, is a manuscript volume from his library containing spirit lists and seals from seventeenth-century grimoires. The National Library of Wales explains that the volume includes diagrams, astrological signs and calculations, bills, ledger leaves, a printed final notice for calling in accounts, a prophetic almanac and later memoranda. It is not simply a “spell book” in the storybook sense; it is a working mixture of occult learning, paperwork and professional life.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.library.wales/discover-learn/digital-exhibitions/manuscripts/modern-period/john-harries-book-of-incantations" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: library.wales">[National Library of Wales]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">library.wales</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Library of Wales John Harries&#x27; Book of Incantations</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Library of WalesJohn Harries&#x27; Book of Incantations - National Library of Wales...</span></span></span>

There is a further twist. Textual comparison shows that the first 22 pages were copied from seventeenth-century grimoire material after John Harries’s death, by Henry Harries. That does not destroy the Harries legend, but it changes how it should be read. The manuscripts show a family library and a continuing magical-medical culture, not a single timeless book of power exactly as described in oral accounts.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.library.wales/discover-learn/digital-exhibitions/manuscripts/modern-period/john-harries-book-of-incantations" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: library.wales">[National Library of Wales]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">library.wales</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Library of Wales John Harries&#x27; Book of Incantations</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Library of WalesJohn Harries&#x27; Book of Incantations - National Library of Wales...</span></span></span>

## Manuscripts, patients and what the evidence actually proves

The strongest evidence for both traditions is manuscript evidence, but the manuscripts prove different things.

For Myddfai, the evidence shows a medieval Welsh medical recipe tradition associated with named physicians. It also shows how later editors, antiquarians and storytellers linked that tradition to the fairy-lake legend. The recipes are not simply charms; they include practical treatments and belong to the wider European medical world of learned remedies, plant knowledge and inherited authorities.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK558253/?report=printable" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[NCBI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">NCBIMedieval Welsh Medical Texts</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NCBI Bookshelf...</span></span></span>

For Cwrt-y-Cadno, the evidence is more directly tied to named nineteenth-century practitioners. The Pant-coy Manuscripts, dated 1813–1875, come from the library of John and Henry Harries and include patient prescriptions and payments, draft horoscopes prepared in response to patient requests, an astrological almanac with manuscript entries, patient correspondence, medical lecture notes, medical treatises, account books and miscellaneous papers. That is unusually concrete evidence for a cunning-folk practice.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archives.library.wales/index.php/cwrt-y-cadno-wales" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.library.wales">[National Library of Wales Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.library.wales</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Library of Wales Archives Cwrt-y-cadno (Wales</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Library of Wales Archives Cwrt-y-cadno (Wales</span></span></span>

This evidence does not prove that charms worked, that spirits appeared, or that astrology diagnosed illness. It proves something more historically useful: people consulted these practitioners, paid them, wrote to them, preserved their papers and remembered them. In folklore terms, that is a strong record of belief in action. The Harries family occupied a real social role, and their magic was embedded in ordinary problems: sickness, debt, livestock, theft, fear and hope.

It also shows why “medicine versus magic” is too neat a division. John Harries had medical books, accounts and patient records; he also had astrological and magical manuscripts. The Myddfai physicians were attached to learned medical texts; they were also given a fairy ancestry in popular tradition. Carmarthenshire’s healing folklore lives in that overlap.

## Why these traditions are still retold today

The Myddfai tradition remains visible because it has become part of Carmarthenshire heritage. Myddfai Community Hall and Visitor Centre stands in the village and is promoted as a visitor attraction in Myddfai, Llandovery. Modern local interpretation presents the village as a place where visitors can encounter the story of the Physicians of Myddfai, while the National Botanic Garden of Wales has also used the tradition to discuss medicinal plants and the long history of plant-based healing.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.visitwales.com/attraction/visitor-centre/myddfai-community-hall-visitor-centre-550791" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: visitwales.com">[visitwales.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">visitwales.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Visit Wales Myddfai Community Hall &amp; Visitor Centre &#124; Visit Wales Visitor Centre</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Attraction Information. The continuing appeal is easy to understand. The Myddfai story offers a complete folk landscape: a mountain lake, a fairy bride, a broken taboo, gifted sons, healing herbs and a named village. It can be told as a fairy tale, a local legend, a medical origin story or a heritage trail. Gwyndaf notes that since the 1861 retelling, the legend has appeared widely in Welsh folk-tale collections and has inspired art, exhibitions, film and even opera.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://isfnr.org/files/Printed.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: isfnr.org">[Folk Narrative Research]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">isfnr.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Folk Narrative Research</span></span></span>

The Cwrt-y-Cadno tradition is less tourist-friendly but, in some ways, even more revealing. The Harrieses show how magical reputation worked in modern historical time. Their surviving papers turn the cunning man from a vague rural stereotype into a literate, controversial, professionally active figure. That makes them valuable for any Carmarthenshire folklore map: they show that belief culture was not confined to medieval legend, and that supernatural problem-solving continued in documented form into the nineteenth century.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.library.wales/discover-learn/digital-exhibitions/manuscripts/modern-period/john-harries-book-of-incantations" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: library.wales">[National Library of Wales]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">library.wales</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Library of Wales John Harries&#x27; Book of Incantations</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Library of WalesJohn Harries&#x27; Book of Incantations - National Library of Wales...</span></span></span>

## What Carmarthenshire magic meant when it meant medicine

The Physicians of Myddfai and the Cwrt-y-Cadno cunning folk should be read together, but not blurred into one story. Myddfai gives Carmarthenshire a medieval, manuscript-backed healing tradition with a fairy-lake origin legend. Cwrt-y-Cadno gives it a later, archive-backed cunning-folk tradition built from astrology, charms, medical practice and patient consultation.

Their shared lesson is that Carmarthenshire’s magical reputation often rested on knowledge rather than spectacle. The healer’s authority might come from a fairy mother, a family line, a manuscript preface, a locked book, a horoscope, a prescription ledger or a patient’s testimony. Some of that authority was legendary, some learned, some commercial and some deeply contested. The result is a county tradition in which medicine and magic do not sit in separate boxes. They meet in the places where people looked for help: at a lake, in a village, at a practitioner’s house, in a book, and in the fragile hope that someone knew what to do.
