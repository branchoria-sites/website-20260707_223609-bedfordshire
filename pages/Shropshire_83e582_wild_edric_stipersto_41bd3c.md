## Introduction

Wild Edric is the [Shropshire]({{ 'why-shropshires-hills-are-full-of/' | relative_url }}) border hero who would not stay neatly inside history. In the records he is an Anglo-Saxon landholder who resisted Norman power after 1066; in legend he becomes a hunter, rebel, fairy husband and spectral rider of the Stiperstones. The most memorable version says he married Lady Godda, a woman of the fairy company, lost her by breaking a forbidden promise, and now rides the Shropshire hills with horn, hounds and horsemen when England faces danger.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://htt.herefordshire.gov.uk/herefordshires-past/the-anglo-saxon-period/the-end-of-the-anglo-saxons/the-end-of-anglo-saxon-herefordshire/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: htt.herefordshire.gov.uk">[htt.herefordshire.gov.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">htt.herefordshire.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Herefordshire Through Time</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Herefordshire Through Time</span></span></span>

The legend matters because it gives the Stiperstones more than a dramatic skyline. Its jagged quartzite tors, old lead-mining ground, exposed weather and views towards Wales make the ridge feel like a place where conquest memory, Marches folklore and fairy belief could meet. Modern walking guides still present the ridge as a landscape of Wild Edric, witches, Roman miners and the Devil’s Chair, showing how firmly the tale remains attached to Shropshire’s public imagination.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.shropshiresgreatoutdoors.co.uk/site/stiperstones-national-nature-reserve-nnr/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: shropshiresgreatoutdoors.co.uk">[Shropshire\&#x27;s Great Outdoors]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">shropshiresgreatoutdoors.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Shropshire\&#x27;s Great Outdoors Stiperstones National Nature Reserve (NNR</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Shropshire\&#x27;s Great Outdoors Stiperstones National Nature Reserve (NNR</span></span></span>

## The rebel behind the legend

The historical Edric, more often written Eadric in modern history, belongs to the violent border world after the Norman Conquest. Herefordshire Through Time, an institutional county-history resource, notes that Eadric the Wild is mentioned in the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle for 1067, when he opposed the Normans around Hereford. It describes him as a large landowner in Herefordshire and Shropshire, with lands recorded in Domesday Book, and says he joined Welsh rulers Rhiwallon and Bleddyn in raids up to the River Lugg.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://htt.herefordshire.gov.uk/herefordshires-past/the-anglo-saxon-period/the-end-of-the-anglo-saxons/the-end-of-anglo-saxon-herefordshire/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: htt.herefordshire.gov.uk">[htt.herefordshire.gov.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">htt.herefordshire.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Herefordshire Through Time</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Herefordshire Through Time</span></span></span>

That border setting is crucial. Edric was not simply a picturesque outlaw invented by later storytellers. He belonged to the Welsh Marches, where English lordship, Welsh alliances, Norman castles and local landholding collided. The same Herefordshire account stresses that Norman castles were a new and highly visible form of control in the region, and that resistance took place in a county already shaped by Anglo-Welsh frontier politics.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://htt.herefordshire.gov.uk/herefordshires-past/the-anglo-saxon-period/the-end-of-the-anglo-saxons/the-end-of-anglo-saxon-herefordshire/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: htt.herefordshire.gov.uk">[htt.herefordshire.gov.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">htt.herefordshire.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Herefordshire Through Time</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Herefordshire Through Time</span></span></span>

Shropshire’s later legend takes that political memory and makes it mythic. The real struggle over land becomes a story about a dispossessed or restless lord who cannot fully die. The Norman Conquest becomes not only a date in national history, but a wound in the local landscape. This is why Wild Edric works so well as a Shropshire figure: he stands at the point where recorded resistance, lost inheritance and upland supernatural tradition overlap.

There is also a useful warning here. The historical Edric and the legendary Wild Edric are not the same sort of evidence. Records can show that an Eadric resisted Norman rule and held land in the region; they do not prove that he rode with fairies, lived under the Stiperstones, or appeared before wars. Those are traditions attached to him because his life left behind the right kind of memory: defiance, uncertainty, border violence and disappearance.

## Lady Godda and the fairy company

The fairy-wife story is older than many modern retellings suggest. Charlotte Sophia Burne’s nineteenth-century *Shropshire Folk-Lore*, drawing on Georgina F. Jackson’s collections and earlier literary material, says that Shropshire people had long associated Wild Edric with an elf-maiden wife. In the tale, Edric returns from hunting in the Forest of Clun, loses his way at night, and comes upon a brightly lit house where noble, beautiful women are dancing and singing.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/stream/shropshirefolkl00burngoog/shropshirefolkl00burngoog_djvu.txt" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Internet Archive Full text of &quot;Shropshire folk-lore; a sheaf of gleanings</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Internet Archive Full text of &quot;Shropshire folk-lore; a sheaf of gleanings</span></span></span>

Edric seizes or wins the most beautiful of them. In later versions she is Lady Godda, though Burne is careful enough to note uncertainty around the name. The marriage depends on a taboo: he must not reproach her for her fairy origin or mention her fairy sisters in anger. For a time they live happily and have a son, Alned, Alnodus or Aelfnoth depending on the version. Then Edric returns from hunting, finds her absent, and in irritation blames her sisters. The moment the forbidden words are spoken, she vanishes.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://sacred-texts.com/neu/eng/efft/efft08.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sacred-texts.com">[Internet Sacred Text Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sacred-texts.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on sacred-texts.com.</span></span></span>

This is a classic fairy-bride pattern, but the Shropshire version has its own local force. The wife is not merely a generic enchantress. She is bound into a named border hero’s story, and her loss helps explain why Edric becomes restless, sorrowful and spectral. Modern Shrewsbury retellings still keep the emotional structure intact: promise, marriage, breach, disappearance and endless riding.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://originalshrewsbury.co.uk/myths-legends-and-folktales-of-shropshire/wild-edric-and-lady-godda-a-promise-broken" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: originalshrewsbury.co.uk">[Original Shrewsbury]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">originalshrewsbury.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on originalshrewsbury.co.uk.</span></span></span>

Walter Map’s medieval *De nugis curialium*, or *Courtiers’ Trifles*, is especially important for the legend’s deep roots. A Cambridge University Press chapter summary on Map’s ghost stories identifies “The Wife of Edric Wilde” among stories of ghostly or otherworldly women who take physical form while honoured by mortal husbands, and it notes Edric’s heir Alnodus as part of that tradition. This places the Edric fairy-wife motif in a medieval learned text, not just in Victorian romantic folklore.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://resolve.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/DA505FC9560D8C3A962A748330263E4E/9781846154928c14_p86-95_CBO.pdf/the-courtiers-trifles-of-walter-map.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: resolve.cambridge.org">[Cambridge University Press &amp; Assessment]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">resolve.cambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">University Press &amp; Assessment The ‘Courtiers’ Trifles’ of Walter Map</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">University Press &amp; Assessment The ‘Courtiers’ Trifles’ of Walter Map</span></span></span>

That does not mean every detail now told at the Stiperstones is medieval. The named Lady Godda, the Wild Hunt colouring, the mining setting and the omen-of-war tradition have been shaped and reshaped through later Shropshire storytelling. The stronger conclusion is more interesting than a simple “true or false”: Wild Edric’s legend is layered. It has a historical rebel at the base, a medieval otherworld marriage near the middle, and a nineteenth-century county folklore record that preserves the form by which many readers know it today.

## The Wild Hunt on the Shropshire hills

Burne’s *Shropshire Folk-Lore* gives one of the richest accounts of Wild Edric as a spectral rider. In the passage, a girl is warned to cover her face and not speak as the company passes. Wild Edric rides at the head of the band on a white horse, with Lady Godda beside him, both dressed in vivid green details, while the hunt rides at full speed over the hills. The witness is told that Edric’s earlier appearance had preceded Napoleon’s time, and the account links such sightings with the coming of desperate war.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/stream/shropshirefolkl00burngoog/shropshirefolkl00burngoog_djvu.txt" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Internet Archive Full text of &quot;Shropshire folk-lore; a sheaf of gleanings</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Internet Archive Full text of &quot;Shropshire folk-lore; a sheaf of gleanings</span></span></span>

Burne treats the story with a mixture of belief-recording and cautious comparison. She says she did not obtain a second version and could not trace the woman who told it, but she still judged its details and parallels too striking to dismiss as mere invention. She also compares it with wider Wild Hunt and “enchanted hero” traditions, where sleeping or hidden champions wait in hills, caves or underground places until a national crisis calls them out.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/stream/shropshirefolkl00burngoog/shropshirefolkl00burngoog_djvu.txt" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Internet Archive Full text of &quot;Shropshire folk-lore; a sheaf of gleanings</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Internet Archive Full text of &quot;Shropshire folk-lore; a sheaf of gleanings</span></span></span>

For readers, that comparison helps explain why Wild Edric feels both local and familiar. The Wild Hunt is not unique to Shropshire, and neither is the idea of a sleeping army under a hill. What is distinctive is the way those motifs are fused with a named Marches rebel and placed against the Stiperstones’ harsh ridge. The story turns political defeat into suspended return: Edric is not simply dead, but withheld.

The omen-of-war element also explains why the legend has stayed memorable. A ghostly rider who appears at random is a haunting; a rider whose appearance warns of national peril becomes a public sign. The tale gives Shropshire a supernatural early-warning system, rooted not in court prophecy but in miners, hill people, rough weather and the old border country.

## Why the Stiperstones keep the story alive

The Stiperstones are almost made for legend. Shropshire’s Great Outdoors describes the National Nature Reserve as a wild, atmospheric ridge of shattered quartzite, formed more than 480 million years ago, with jagged tors including the Devil’s Chair. The same visitor resource points walkers towards the ridge, panoramic views across Shropshire and Wales, and an accessible route from the reserve car park.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.shropshiresgreatoutdoors.co.uk/site/stiperstones-national-nature-reserve-nnr/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: shropshiresgreatoutdoors.co.uk">[Shropshire\&#x27;s Great Outdoors]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">shropshiresgreatoutdoors.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Shropshire\&#x27;s Great Outdoors Stiperstones National Nature Reserve (NNR</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Shropshire\&#x27;s Great Outdoors Stiperstones National Nature Reserve (NNR</span></span></span>

That physical drama matters. The tors look like ruins, seats, teeth or broken battlements, and the ridge is exposed to weather that can make sound and visibility unreliable. A legend of horns, horsemen and hidden companies feels more plausible in such a place than it would in a tidy lowland field. The Stiperstones give the tale a stage.

The old mining landscape deepens the effect. The Shropshire Great Outdoors circular walk presents the area as a ridge steeped in history and myth, naming Bronze Age burial cairns, Wild Edric, Roman lead miners and witches in the same landscape. It also notes the Bog Centre and the long mining history around The Bog, including lead working and a Roman-marked lead ingot found nearby.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.shropshiresgreatoutdoors.co.uk/route/shropshire-way-circular-walk-2-stiperstones/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: shropshiresgreatoutdoors.co.uk">[Shropshire\&#x27;s Great Outdoors]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">shropshiresgreatoutdoors.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Shropshire\&#x27;s Great Outdoors Discover Shropshire Circular Walk 2</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Shropshire\&#x27;s Great Outdoors Discover Shropshire Circular Walk 2</span></span></span>

This is why stories of Edric and his company hidden underground have such power. Mines already make sound mysterious: knocks, echoes, shifting stone, darkness, tools and buried labour all feed the imagination. In folklore, such places readily become entrances to another world. At the Stiperstones, the idea that Edric’s retinue might be imprisoned, sleeping or waiting below the hill fits both the mining past and the enchanted-hero pattern Burne noticed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/stream/shropshirefolkl00burngoog/shropshirefolkl00burngoog_djvu.txt" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Internet Archive Full text of &quot;Shropshire folk-lore; a sheaf of gleanings</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Internet Archive Full text of &quot;Shropshire folk-lore; a sheaf of gleanings</span></span></span>

The Devil’s Chair adds another layer without needing to take over the page. It belongs to the wider Stiperstones folklore of witches, the Devil and uncanny weather. Wild Edric’s legend does not depend on the Devil’s Chair, but the named tor helps keep the ridge in the category of places where stories gather. For a visitor, the landscape reads as a chain of named supernatural landmarks rather than a blank upland walk.

## How old and well-attested is the tradition?

The answer depends on which part of the tradition is being tested. The historical Edric is well enough grounded to be treated as a real post-Conquest border figure. The Anglo-Saxon Chronicle reference, the Herefordshire and Shropshire land context, and Domesday-related discussion make him much firmer than a purely legendary hero.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://htt.herefordshire.gov.uk/herefordshires-past/the-anglo-saxon-period/the-end-of-the-anglo-saxons/the-end-of-anglo-saxon-herefordshire/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: htt.herefordshire.gov.uk">[htt.herefordshire.gov.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">htt.herefordshire.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Herefordshire Through Time</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Herefordshire Through Time</span></span></span>

The fairy-wife story is also old in literary terms, because Walter Map was writing in the later twelfth century, and modern scholarship still identifies “The Wife of Edric Wilde” as part of his otherworld-woman material. Burne’s county collection then preserves and popularises the story for Shropshire readers in the nineteenth century, explicitly connecting Wild Edric with an elf-maiden wife and with the Forest of Clun hunting episode.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://resolve.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/DA505FC9560D8C3A962A748330263E4E/9781846154928c14_p86-95_CBO.pdf/the-courtiers-trifles-of-walter-map.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: resolve.cambridge.org">[Cambridge University Press &amp; Assessment]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">resolve.cambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">University Press &amp; Assessment The ‘Courtiers’ Trifles’ of Walter Map</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">University Press &amp; Assessment The ‘Courtiers’ Trifles’ of Walter Map</span></span></span>

The Stiperstones Wild Hunt version is more difficult. Burne records a vivid account of Edric, Godda and the riding band, but she also admits that she could not get a second version or trace the original woman. That does not make it worthless; it makes it exactly the sort of evidence folklore often gives us: rich, local, memorable, but not capable of being checked like a court record.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/stream/shropshirefolkl00burngoog/shropshirefolkl00burngoog_djvu.txt" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Internet Archive Full text of &quot;Shropshire folk-lore; a sheaf of gleanings</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Internet Archive Full text of &quot;Shropshire folk-lore; a sheaf of gleanings</span></span></span>

Modern retellings often smooth out these differences. Tourist and local-history pages may present the full legend as one continuous story: Saxon rebel, fairy wife, broken promise, underground army, spectral hunt and wartime omen. That is useful for storytelling, but the evidence is layered. A careful reader should see four strands:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **Recorded history:** Eadric as a Shropshire and Herefordshire landholder resisting Norman power.
* **Medieval literary legend:** Edric’s otherworld wife and their son in Walter Map’s tradition.
* **Victorian folklore collection:** Burne’s Shropshire accounts of the fairy wife and the Wild Hunt.
* **Modern place-story:** Stiperstones walks, visitor material and local retellings that keep Edric active in the landscape.

</div>

This layered structure does not weaken the legend. It explains why it has lasted. Each period found a version of Wild Edric that met its own needs: rebel, fairy husband, sleeping champion, ghostly omen, local walking-route hero.

## What the legend says about Shropshire

Wild Edric’s story is one of Shropshire’s clearest examples of border folklore. It is not just a tale of fairies, and not just a tale of Norman conquest. It is a story about a county looking both east and west: English enough to remember 1066 as dispossession, Marcher enough to involve Welsh alliances and border raiding, and rural enough to place its supernatural life in forests, hills and mines.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://htt.herefordshire.gov.uk/herefordshires-past/the-anglo-saxon-period/the-end-of-the-anglo-saxons/the-end-of-anglo-saxon-herefordshire/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: htt.herefordshire.gov.uk">[htt.herefordshire.gov.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">htt.herefordshire.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Herefordshire Through Time</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Herefordshire Through Time</span></span></span>

The fairy element also changes the emotional meaning of the rebel. Without Godda, Wild Edric might be only a local resistance hero. With her, he becomes a figure of broken trust and permanent longing. His tragedy is not defeat in battle, but the loss of a relationship with the otherworld. That is why the story feels more like folklore than patriotic history: the turning point is a spoken taboo, not a military campaign.

The Stiperstones then make the memory visible. The ridge’s rough quartzite, old mining ground and exposed paths allow visitors to experience the legend as place rather than simply plot. A walker does not need to believe in Edric’s hunt to understand why the story belongs there. The landscape supplies the mood: height, wind, broken stone, distance, and the sense that something could be hidden just below or just beyond sight.

In the wider Shropshire folklore map, Wild Edric sits naturally beside Mitchell’s Fold, [the Wrekin]({{ 'the-wrekin/' | relative_url }}), the Devil’s Chair, black-dog traditions, witches and holy wells. But he remains distinctive because he carries historical memory into fairy legend. He is not an anonymous ghost. He has a name, a political wound, a lost fairy wife and a ridge that still keeps his story moving.

## How Wild Edric is retold today

Today, Wild Edric survives in several overlapping forms. Heritage and tourism pages retell the romance of Edric and Lady Godda for visitors; walking resources place him on Stiperstones routes; folklore websites connect him with the Wild Hunt; and local storytelling events continue to use him as an accessible way into Shropshire’s mythic landscape. Original Shrewsbury, for example, retells the broken-promise version in which Godda disappears after Edric blames her fairy family, leaving him to ride eternally in search of her.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://originalshrewsbury.co.uk/myths-legends-and-folktales-of-shropshire/wild-edric-and-lady-godda-a-promise-broken" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: originalshrewsbury.co.uk">[Original Shrewsbury]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">originalshrewsbury.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on originalshrewsbury.co.uk.</span></span></span>

The Stiperstones are also interpreted for modern outdoor audiences. Shropshire’s Great Outdoors presents the National Nature Reserve as open all year, with the 8 km Stiperstones Stomp, an all-ability trail, wildlife such as red grouse and skylarks, and the famous jagged tors. Its walking route explicitly frames the ridge as “steeped in history and myth”, naming Wild Edric among the figures that haunt the walk.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.shropshiresgreatoutdoors.co.uk/site/stiperstones-national-nature-reserve-nnr/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: shropshiresgreatoutdoors.co.uk">[Shropshire\&#x27;s Great Outdoors]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">shropshiresgreatoutdoors.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Shropshire\&#x27;s Great Outdoors Stiperstones National Nature Reserve (NNR</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Shropshire\&#x27;s Great Outdoors Stiperstones National Nature Reserve (NNR</span></span></span>

That modern presentation is not a betrayal of the old legend. Folklore often lives by being retold in the places people actually go: footpaths, visitor centres, town trails, school materials, local websites and guided walks. What changes is the emphasis. The medieval tale was concerned with an otherworld wife and the conditions of marriage; Burne’s account was interested in oral tradition, Wild Hunt parallels and local witnesses; today’s versions often emphasise romance, landscape and visitor experience.

The strongest modern retellings keep the uncertainty visible. They do not need to claim that Wild Edric literally rides the Stiperstones. They can say something more truthful and more durable: Shropshire has long remembered a rebel called Edric; medieval and later tradition gave him a fairy wife; local folklore set him riding with a spectral company; and the Stiperstones remain the landscape where that mixture of history and enchantment feels most at home.

      button.setAttribute('aria-selected', button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-option') === marketId ? 'true' : 'false');
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      var active = button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button') === marketId;
      button.setAttribute('aria-pressed', active ? 'true' : 'false');
    });
    section.setAttribute('data-ebay-selected-market', marketId);
    if (persist) {
      try { if (window.localStorage) localStorage.setItem('phoenix-ebay-market', marketId); } catch (err) {}
    }
    closeMenu(section);
  }
  sections.forEach(function (section) {
    if (section.getAttribute('data-ebay-localized-init') === '1') return;
    section.setAttribute('data-ebay-localized-init', '1');
    var available = availableMarkets(section);
    var marketId = defaultMarket;
    if (window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation && window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation.inferMarket) {
      marketId = window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation.inferMarket(available, {
        storageKey: 'phoenix-ebay-market',
        defaultMarket: defaultMarket
      });
    } else if (available.indexOf(defaultMarket)< 0) {
      marketId = available[0] || defaultMarket;
    }
    var select = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-select]');
    if (select) {
      select.addEventListener('change', function () {
        applyMarket(section, select.value, true);
      });
    }
    var trigger = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-trigger]');
    var menu = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-menu]');
    if (trigger && menu) {
      trigger.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
        event.stopPropagation();
        var expanded = trigger.getAttribute('aria-expanded') === 'true';
        menu.hidden = expanded;
        trigger.setAttribute('aria-expanded', expanded ? 'false' : 'true');
        var picker = trigger.closest('.fr-ebay-market-picker');
        if (picker) picker.classList.toggle('fr-ebay-market-picker--open', !expanded);
      });
      Array.prototype.slice.call(menu.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-option]')).forEach(function (button) {
        button.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
          event.stopPropagation();
          applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-option'), true);
        });
      });
      document.addEventListener('click', function () { closeMenu(section); });
    }
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      button.addEventListener('click', function () {
        applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button'), true);
      });
    });
    applyMarket(section, marketId, false);
  });
})();
