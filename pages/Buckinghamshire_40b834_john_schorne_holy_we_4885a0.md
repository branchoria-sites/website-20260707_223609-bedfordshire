## Introduction

John Schorne’s well at North Marston is [Buckinghamshire]({{ 'where-buckinghamshire-keeps-its-strange/' | relative_url }})’s most vivid holy-water legend: a medieval rector, a village drought, a spring struck from the ground, healing water for painful ailments, and the unforgettable story that he trapped the devil in a boot. The tradition belongs to a real place in north Buckinghamshire, on Schorne Lane near St Mary’s Church, but it also travelled far beyond the village through pilgrimage, badges, church imagery and later retellings. North Marston’s local history group describes Schorne as rector from 1282 to 1314, and the Buckinghamshire Heritage Portal records his well as a late 13th- or early 14th-century medieval holy well.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://northmarstonhistory.org.uk/john-schorne/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: northmarstonhistory.org.uk">[northmarstonhistory.org.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">northmarstonhistory.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">john schorne</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">12 Dec 2017 — John Schorne, a canon from Dunstable Priory, was the rector of North Marston church from 1282 to 1314. The story matters because it is not just a quaint village tale. Schorne became a “folk saint”: widely venerated by ordinary people, though never formally canonised. His well and shrine made North Marston one of the notable pilgrimage places of late medieval England, and his image — a holy man with a boot and a little devil — became one of Buckinghamshire’s strangest contributions to English religious folklore.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/H" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: britishmuseum.org">[British Museum]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">britishmuseum.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">British Museumpilgrim badgePilgrim-badge; lead alloy; standing figure, John Schorn the Buckinghamshire preacher, pushing the Devil into a...</span></span></span>

## The drought miracle at North Marston

The central North Marston story is simple enough to survive seven centuries of retelling. During a severe drought, John Schorne is said to have struck the ground with his staff and brought up a spring of fresh water. In local accounts, this did not remain an ordinary village water source: it became a holy well whose water was believed to heal. North Marston Parish Council presents the legend in this form, placing Schorne’s miracle in “one very hot summer” when the village was suffering from drought.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://northmarstonparishcouncil.gov.uk/the-well.php" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: northmarstonparishcouncil.gov.uk">[northmarstonparishcouncil.gov.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">northmarstonparishcouncil.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The well It all dates back to John Schorne</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The well It all dates back to John Schorne</span></span></span>

For folklore, the important point is not whether the spring appeared exactly as the story says. It is how the story joins landscape, need and sanctity. A community without water receives water through the action of a holy local figure. A practical crisis becomes a remembered miracle. A spring in a lane becomes a sacred landmark. That pattern is found in many British holy-well traditions, but North Marston is unusually distinctive because the well is tied to a named medieval rector and a later pilgrimage cult with surviving material evidence.

The well’s physical setting keeps the legend grounded. It is not a vague “somewhere in Buckinghamshire” tale, but a marked local site in Schorne Lane. Buckinghamshire’s Heritage Portal identifies John Schorne’s or Shorne’s Well as a medieval holy well dating to the late 13th or early 14th century, while local accounts record restoration work in the modern period.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://heritageportal.buckinghamshire.gov.uk/monuments/search?pn=1164&amp;ps=10" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: heritageportal.buckinghamshire.gov.uk">[heritageportal.buckinghamshire.gov.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">heritageportal.buckinghamshire.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Record Search</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">This medieval holy well is supposed to have derived its...Read more...</span></span></span>

The spring’s healing reputation also developed in a very bodily way. It was especially linked with painful conditions of the limbs and feet, including gout. That matters for the boot legend: in one reading, the “devil in the boot” is not only a supernatural prisoner but a vivid image for agony in the foot. North Marston Parish Council explains the local interpretation directly: gout was painful and was associated with the phrase “the devil in the boot”, so the holy water’s cure could be imagined as drawing the devil out.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://northmarstonparishcouncil.gov.uk/the-well.php" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: northmarstonparishcouncil.gov.uk">[northmarstonparishcouncil.gov.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">northmarstonparishcouncil.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The well It all dates back to John Schorne</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The well It all dates back to John Schorne</span></span></span>

## Why pilgrims came for water, relics and hope

John Schorne’s reputation grew after his death. He was buried at North Marston, and the village became associated with two linked sources of help: the well, whose water was believed to cure, and the shrine, where pilgrims sought aid through Schorne’s holiness. Buckinghamshire’s Heritage Portal summarises the tradition plainly: Schorne’s well was said to cure those who drank its water, and there was a shrine to him in St Mary’s Church before it was later moved to St George’s Chapel, Windsor.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://heritageportal.buckinghamshire.gov.uk/Theme/TBC749" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: heritageportal.buckinghamshire.gov.uk">[heritageportal.buckinghamshire.gov.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">heritageportal.buckinghamshire.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on buckinghamshire.gov.uk.</span></span></span>

The strongest version of the local claim is that North Marston became the third most popular pilgrimage destination in England after Canterbury and Walsingham. This ranking appears in local and folklore-facing accounts, including the Folklore Society’s event description for a 2024 talk on Schorne. It should be read as a traditional or interpretive claim rather than a neat medieval league table, but it does capture the scale of Schorne’s reputation in later medieval devotion.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.folklore-society.com/event/sir-john-schorn-the-man-who-conjured-the-devil-into-a-boot/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: folklore-society.com">[Folklore Society]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">folklore-society.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on folklore-society.com.</span></span></span>

A striking sign of that fame is the involvement of Windsor. The College of St George notes that Master John Schorn was among the saintly figures whose burial places and relics helped draw pilgrims to St George’s Chapel in the Middle Ages. The London Museum’s catalogue says that Schorn’s remains were moved to Windsor in 1478, and that badges from his shrine may therefore belong either to North Marston or to Windsor depending on date and type.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.stgeorges-windsor.org/a-host-of-relics/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: stgeorges-windsor.org">[College of St George]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">stgeorges-windsor.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">College of St George A host of relics</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">College of St George A host of relics</span></span></span>

That move changes how the legend should be understood within Buckinghamshire. Schorne begins as a village rector attached to North Marston, but his cult does not remain village-sized. It becomes part of a wider sacred economy: pilgrimage, relics, badges, chapel-building and royal attention. The Folklore Society’s account notes that the income from pilgrims was significant enough for his relics to be translated to Windsor to help finance St George’s Chapel.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.folklore-society.com/event/sir-john-schorn-the-man-who-conjured-the-devil-into-a-boot/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: folklore-society.com">[Folklore Society]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">folklore-society.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on folklore-society.com.</span></span></span>

The story also brushes against Tudor history. Modern scholarship on Henry VIII has noted that Henry went twice to the shrine of John Schorn at North Marston, in July 1511 and May 1521. That detail is useful because it shows Schorne’s cult still mattered in the early 16th century, before the Reformation transformed the religious world that had supported shrines, relics and pilgrimage.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.jstor.org/stable/24809778" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: jstor.org">[JSTOR]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">jstor.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Henry VIII: &#x27;Catholicism without the Pope?</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Henry VIII: &#x27;Catholicism without the Pope?</span></span></span>

## The devil in a boot

Schorne’s most memorable legend is the one that made his image instantly recognisable: he was said to have conjured, trapped or forced the devil into a boot. The British Museum has a 15th-century lead-alloy pilgrim badge showing “John Schorn the Buckinghamshire preacher” pushing the Devil into a boot. Its findspot near London Bridge also shows how far souvenirs of the cult could travel from North Marston.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/H" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: britishmuseum.org">[British Museum]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">britishmuseum.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">British Museumpilgrim badgePilgrim-badge; lead alloy; standing figure, John Schorn the Buckinghamshire preacher, pushing the Devil into a...</span></span></span>

The London Museum records several John Schorn pilgrim badges with boot-and-devil imagery. One depicts him in a long robe holding a tall boot, with the Devil’s head and bat-like wings peering from the top; another shows him holding the Devil under his arm with a scroll reading “Beware! Beware!” These are not late fantasy illustrations but late medieval devotional objects, probably bought or worn by pilgrims.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.londonmuseum.org.uk/collections/v/object-776167/john-schorn/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: londonmuseum.org.uk">[London Museum]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">londonmuseum.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on londonmuseum.org.uk.</span></span></span>

There are two main ways to read the boot. One is exorcism: Schorne was remembered as a holy man powerful enough to defeat a demonic force. The other is healing symbolism: the boot represents gout, foot pain or bodily affliction, and the devil is the suffering driven out by sacred power. The Folklore Society notes that opinion varies between these interpretations, with Schorne seen either as an exorcist or as a healer whose imagery made pain visible.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.folklore-society.com/event/sir-john-schorn-the-man-who-conjured-the-devil-into-a-boot/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: folklore-society.com">[Folklore Society]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">folklore-society.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on folklore-society.com.</span></span></span>

The boot legend also belongs to a wider world of medieval religious images that were bold, funny, frightening and easy to recognise. A tiny devil trapped in footwear sounds comic now, but it made a serious point in a form ordinary people could remember. The image compressed several promises into one scene: the saintly rector had power over evil, pain could be expelled, and a local Buckinghamshire holy place could offer help when ordinary medicine could not.

The oft-repeated claim that Schorne’s boot inspired the jack-in-the-box toy should be treated carefully. North Marston Parish Council says it is widely believed to be the origin of the toy, and St George’s Chapel repeats the association in its 700th-anniversary material. But the safer wording is that Schorne’s image has been linked in tradition with the jack-in-the-box, not that a direct line of invention can be proved.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://northmarstonparishcouncil.gov.uk/the-well.php" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: northmarstonparishcouncil.gov.uk">[northmarstonparishcouncil.gov.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">northmarstonparishcouncil.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The well It all dates back to John Schorne</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The well It all dates back to John Schorne</span></span></span>

## Pilgrim badges made the legend portable

Pilgrim badges are one of the reasons Schorne’s story is unusually well anchored. They were small, wearable souvenirs, usually cast in inexpensive metal, and they allowed a pilgrimage story to move with the pilgrim. In Schorne’s case, the badge did not need much explanation: a holy man, a boot and a devil were enough to identify the cult.

The British Museum’s 15th-century badge is especially valuable because it identifies Schorn as a Buckinghamshire preacher and shows the boot miracle as the badge’s central subject. The London Museum examples add detail: robe, halo, book, rod, scroll, boot, demon’s head, bat-like wings. Together they show that Schorne’s legend had become a standard visual type, not merely a local anecdote remembered in prose.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/H" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: britishmuseum.org">[britishmuseum.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">britishmuseum.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">British Museumpilgrim badgePilgrim-badge; lead alloy; standing figure, John Schorn the Buckinghamshire preacher, pushing the Devil into a...</span></span></span>

This material evidence also helps separate different layers of the tradition. The drought miracle explains the well. The healing reputation explains why sufferers might come. The shrine explains pilgrimage. The badge explains how the story was sold, worn and recognised. Later folklore then folds these pieces together into a single memorable character: “Sir John Schorne, gentleman born, conjured the devil into a boot.”

Schorne’s badges also connect North Marston to London and the Thames. Finds from London Bridge or the River Thames suggest that pilgrims carried such objects into the capital, lost them, discarded them, or deposited them in watery places. That does not prove every badge was bought in Buckinghamshire, especially after the relics moved to Windsor, but it does show how a village cult could circulate through the wider religious landscape of southern England.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/H" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: britishmuseum.org">[British Museum]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">britishmuseum.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">British Museumpilgrim badgePilgrim-badge; lead alloy; standing figure, John Schorn the Buckinghamshire preacher, pushing the Devil into a...</span></span></span>

## What changed when the cult moved to Windsor?

The translation of Schorne’s remains to Windsor is one of the most important turns in the story. It did not erase North Marston’s claim, because the well remained in the village and the origin legend still belonged there. But it did split the cult between two places: the Buckinghamshire village of the holy rector and the royal chapel that acquired his relics.

The College of St George describes medieval St George’s Chapel as a place with a “spectacular array of relics” that drew pilgrims, including the burial places of King Henry VI and Master John Schorn. Another St George’s Chapel account says that only Henry VI’s tomb remains of the early pilgrim sites in that corner of the chapel, while Schorn’s remains are presumed still to rest there, in a space later reconstructed as the tomb of Edward Clinton, 1st Earl of Lincoln.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.stgeorges-windsor.org/a-host-of-relics/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: stgeorges-windsor.org">[College of St George]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">stgeorges-windsor.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">College of St George A host of relics</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">College of St George A host of relics</span></span></span>

For Buckinghamshire folklore, this creates a useful tension. North Marston keeps the landscape legend: the drought, the spring, the lane, the parish church, the water. Windsor keeps the high-status relic story: translation, chapel, pilgrimage revenue and royal religious life. Schorne therefore sits between local folk memory and institutional medieval religion.

The Reformation later weakened or destroyed many such cults. Shrines, relics and miracle claims came under attack in 16th-century religious reform, and Schorne’s public cult no longer functioned as it had in the late Middle Ages. Yet the story survived because it had several carriers: the well, village memory, antiquarian writing, museum objects, church history, badges and the irresistibly odd phrase about the devil in a boot.

## Bierton, Buckingham and other sacred waters

Schorne’s well is Buckinghamshire’s best-known sacred-water story, but it is not the county’s only one. Nearby holy wells show how North Marston fits into a broader local pattern in which springs were attached to saints, healing and community memory.

At Bierton, St Osyth’s Well — also known as Uptown Well — is associated with St James’ Church and local tradition. The village history page places it a short distance from the Aylesbury Road, while the Bierton Neighbourhood Plan says the spring was reputed to have healing properties after St Osyth’s body was believed to have rested near the spot on its way to Aylesbury.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.biertonvillage.org.uk/bierton-and-broughton/stosyths-well/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: biertonvillage.org.uk">[Bierton Parish Council]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">biertonvillage.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on biertonvillage.org.uk.</span></span></span>

At Buckingham, St Rumbold’s Well preserves another type of saintly water tradition. Buckinghamshire’s Heritage Portal records it as the site of a medieval holy well, rebuilt in 1623 as a conduit house and restored in 2002. Historic England describes the scheduled monument as including the medieval well, early 17th-century conduit-house remains, an outflow leat and nearby medieval ridge and furrow.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://heritageportal.buckinghamshire.gov.uk/Monument/MBC5728" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: heritageportal.buckinghamshire.gov.uk">[heritageportal.buckinghamshire.gov.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">heritageportal.buckinghamshire.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on buckinghamshire.gov.uk.</span></span></span>

These sites are quieter than North Marston because they lack Schorne’s combination of named rector, dramatic miracle, shrine, relic translation and mass-produced badges. But they help readers see the county pattern. Buckinghamshire’s sacred waters are not all one thing. Some are village springs with saintly associations; some become restored heritage sites; Schorne’s well became the centre of a full pilgrimage legend with national reach.

## How old and well-attested is the tradition?

The Schorne tradition is unusually well-attested for a local healing legend, but not every detail has the same evidential weight. The existence of a medieval holy well at North Marston is recorded by Buckinghamshire’s Historic Environment Record. The existence of late medieval Schorne pilgrim badges is supported by museum collections. The translation to Windsor is reflected in St George’s Chapel history and museum catalogues.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://heritageportal.buckinghamshire.gov.uk/monuments/search?pn=1164&amp;ps=10" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: heritageportal.buckinghamshire.gov.uk">[buckinghamshire.gov.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">heritageportal.buckinghamshire.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Record Search</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">This medieval holy well is supposed to have derived its...Read more...</span></span></span>

The exact miracle stories are a different kind of evidence. There are also small variations in dates and names. Sources use Schorn, Schorne, Shorn or Shorne; he is called Sir, Master or Maister; and his death is variously given as 1314 or 1315 in different summaries. ## Why Schorne still belongs to Buckinghamshire folklore

John Schorne’s legend has lasted because it is unusually complete. It has a place to visit, a saintly figure, a crisis story, a healing promise, a memorable monster image, museum objects, institutional history and modern village presentation. Many local legends survive only as a sentence in a folklore collection. Schorne’s survives as a small landscape of belief.

For Buckinghamshire, the story is especially valuable because it gives the county a major medieval folklore figure without turning him into a fantasy hero. Schorne is not a dragon-slayer or a giant. He is a parish rector remembered through water, pain, pilgrimage and a boot. That makes the legend feel strongly rooted in the everyday concerns of medieval villagers and pilgrims: drought, illness, travel, money, prayer and hope.

The modern well at North Marston also shows how folklore becomes heritage. The site no longer functions as a medieval pilgrimage shrine in the old sense, but it remains a place where visitors can encounter the story physically. North Marston Parish Council still presents the well as a local landmark, complete with the devil-in-the-boot mechanism, while local history pages continue to tell Schorne’s story as part of the village’s identity.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://northmarstonparishcouncil.gov.uk/the-well.php" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: northmarstonparishcouncil.gov.uk">[northmarstonparishcouncil.gov.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">northmarstonparishcouncil.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The well It all dates back to John Schorne</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The well It all dates back to John Schorne</span></span></span>

The result is one of Buckinghamshire’s clearest examples of belief layered into landscape. A spring becomes a holy well. A rector becomes a folk saint. A painful foot becomes a devil in a boot. A village in the Vale of Aylesbury becomes part of the pilgrimage map of medieval England.

    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-domain-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = domainLabel(marketId);
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-option]')).forEach(function (button) {
      button.setAttribute('aria-selected', button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-option') === marketId ? 'true' : 'false');
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      var active = button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button') === marketId;
      button.setAttribute('aria-pressed', active ? 'true' : 'false');
    });
    section.setAttribute('data-ebay-selected-market', marketId);
    if (persist) {
      try { if (window.localStorage) localStorage.setItem('phoenix-ebay-market', marketId); } catch (err) {}
    }
    closeMenu(section);
  }
  sections.forEach(function (section) {
    if (section.getAttribute('data-ebay-localized-init') === '1') return;
    section.setAttribute('data-ebay-localized-init', '1');
    var available = availableMarkets(section);
    var marketId = defaultMarket;
    if (window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation && window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation.inferMarket) {
      marketId = window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation.inferMarket(available, {
        storageKey: 'phoenix-ebay-market',
        defaultMarket: defaultMarket
      });
    } else if (available.indexOf(defaultMarket)< 0) {
      marketId = available[0] || defaultMarket;
    }
    var select = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-select]');
    if (select) {
      select.addEventListener('change', function () {
        applyMarket(section, select.value, true);
      });
    }
    var trigger = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-trigger]');
    var menu = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-menu]');
    if (trigger && menu) {
      trigger.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
        event.stopPropagation();
        var expanded = trigger.getAttribute('aria-expanded') === 'true';
        menu.hidden = expanded;
        trigger.setAttribute('aria-expanded', expanded ? 'false' : 'true');
        var picker = trigger.closest('.fr-ebay-market-picker');
        if (picker) picker.classList.toggle('fr-ebay-market-picker--open', !expanded);
      });
      Array.prototype.slice.call(menu.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-option]')).forEach(function (button) {
        button.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
          event.stopPropagation();
          applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-option'), true);
        });
      });
      document.addEventListener('click', function () { closeMenu(section); });
    }
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      button.addEventListener('click', function () {
        applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button'), true);
      });
    });
    applyMarket(section, marketId, false);
  });
})();
