## Introduction

The Oakley witch ordeal was a public “testing” of an elderly [Bedfordshire]({{ 'where-bedfordshire-keeps-its-oldest/' | relative_url }}) woman in 1735, close to Oakley Mill on the River Great Ouse. She had lived for some time under the accusation that she was a witch, and, according to the account preserved by Bedfordshire Archives, she agreed to be ducked in the hope of clearing both her own name and her children’s. Instead, the ordeal turned into a village spectacle: she was bound, searched for pins, thrown into the river, declared suspicious because she floated, and then weighed against the parish Bible. It matters because it shows Bedfordshire witch fear at the point where official law and popular belief were parting company. Parliament was about to repeal the old witchcraft laws, but at Oakley a local crowd still acted as though supernatural guilt could be proved by water, rumour and ritual humiliation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://bedsarchives.bedford.gov.uk/CommunityHistories/Oakley/WitchTestingAtOakleyIn1735.aspx" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: bedsarchives.bedford.gov.uk">[Bedfordshire Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">bedsarchives.bedford.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Hosted By Bedford Borough Council: Witch Testing at Oakley in 1735...</span></span></span>

## The 1735 River Ouse ducking

The fullest accessible account places the episode at Oakley, about three miles from Bedford, in 1735. Bedfordshire Archives reproduces a report printed in the *Bedfordshire Times* in 1892, itself quoting W. C. Sydney’s *England and the English in the Eighteenth Century*, which in turn drew on *London Magazine* for 1735. That chain of transmission matters: this is not a surviving parish minute book calmly recording the event, but a later printed retelling of an eighteenth-century magazine account. Even so, the detail is sharp enough to make Oakley one of Bedfordshire’s most memorable witchcraft stories.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://bedsarchives.bedford.gov.uk/CommunityHistories/Oakley/WitchTestingAtOakleyIn1735.aspx" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: bedsarchives.bedford.gov.uk">[Bedfordshire Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">bedsarchives.bedford.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Hosted By Bedford Borough Council: Witch Testing at Oakley in 1735...</span></span></span>

The woman is not named in the archive account. She is described as married, over sixty, and resident in Oakley. She had “long lain under an imputation of witchcraft”, meaning that suspicion had already stuck to her before the ordeal began. The story’s grimest detail is that she consented to the test because she wanted to escape the accusation, not because a court had convicted her. In folklore terms, that is crucial: the Oakley case is about social pressure and communal belief as much as law.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://bedsarchives.bedford.gov.uk/CommunityHistories/Oakley/WitchTestingAtOakleyIn1735.aspx" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: bedsarchives.bedford.gov.uk">[Bedfordshire Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">bedsarchives.bedford.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Hosted By Bedford Borough Council: Witch Testing at Oakley in 1735...</span></span></span>

The chosen place was the river near the mill on the banks of the Ouse. The ritual followed a cruel folk logic. She was tied in a wet sheet, with her face uncovered, her hands and feet bound, and a rope around her body. The bystanders searched her for pins because pins were thought to interfere with the “swimming” of a witch. She was then thrown into the water. When she floated, with her head under water, the crowd took this not as a sign of the binding or the river’s behaviour, but as evidence that she was a witch. The cry reportedly went up: “A witch! Drown her! Hang her!”<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://bedsarchives.bedford.gov.uk/CommunityHistories/Oakley/WitchTestingAtOakleyIn1735.aspx" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: bedsarchives.bedford.gov.uk">[Bedfordshire Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">bedsarchives.bedford.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Hosted By Bedford Borough Council: Witch Testing at Oakley in 1735...</span></span></span>

The ordeal did not stop after one near-drowning. She was pulled out “more dead than alive”, allowed to recover her breath, and placed in the river again. She again floated, and was again treated as guilty. The report then says she was hauled out, laid on the grassy bank, speechless and close to death, while the people around her abused rather than pitied her. It is this mixture of public entertainment, fear and moral certainty that makes the Oakley story so disturbing. The crowd believed it was revealing hidden guilt; from a modern viewpoint, it was manufacturing danger and then interpreting the victim’s survival as proof against her.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://bedsarchives.bedford.gov.uk/CommunityHistories/Oakley/WitchTestingAtOakleyIn1735.aspx" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: bedsarchives.bedford.gov.uk">[Bedfordshire Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">bedsarchives.bedford.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Hosted By Bedford Borough Council: Witch Testing at Oakley in 1735...</span></span></span>

## Why the Bible weighing mattered

The second test sounds almost absurd until it is read as part of the same belief-world. After the ducking, some of the crowd proposed weighing the woman against the large Bible from Oakley church. The idea was that the Bible, as the word of God, would outweigh or overcome the works of the Devil. According to the preserved account, the Bible weighed about twelve pounds, and the woman outweighed it. That result convinced some spectators of her innocence, or at least made them hesitate.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://bedsarchives.bedford.gov.uk/CommunityHistories/Oakley/WitchTestingAtOakleyIn1735.aspx" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: bedsarchives.bedford.gov.uk">[Bedfordshire Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">bedsarchives.bedford.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Hosted By Bedford Borough Council: Witch Testing at Oakley in 1735...</span></span></span>

Yet the parish clergyman is said to have left still convinced she was a witch and tried to persuade others to agree. This is one of the story’s most revealing details. The test was presented as a way to settle the matter, but belief did not depend on the outcome. Floating proved guilt; outweighing the Bible did not reliably prove innocence. The ordeal worked less like evidence and more like a public performance in which suspicion had already decided what it wanted to see.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://bedsarchives.bedford.gov.uk/CommunityHistories/Oakley/WitchTestingAtOakleyIn1735.aspx" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: bedsarchives.bedford.gov.uk">[Bedfordshire Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">bedsarchives.bedford.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Hosted By Bedford Borough Council: Witch Testing at Oakley in 1735...</span></span></span>

For a folklore reader, the Bible weighing also shows how local witch-belief could mix Christian objects, village authority and older ordeal customs. The Bible was not being used as a text to be read, but as a sacred weight. The parish setting gave the test a kind of moral theatre: river, mill, church book, officers, crowd, clergyman. Oakley’s story therefore belongs not just to “witch trials” in the courtroom sense, but to the wider history of communal rituals used to manage fear, blame and misfortune.

## Witch belief after formal trials faded

Oakley happened at a turning point. UK Parliament’s own historical summary explains that English witchcraft prosecutions had peaked much earlier, especially in south-east England between 1560 and 1700, and that the last English witch trials were held in Leicester in 1717. In 1736, Parliament repealed the laws against witchcraft and replaced them with penalties for people who claimed magical powers. In other words, the law was moving away from punishing witches as real supernatural criminals and towards treating magical claims as fraud or imposture.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.parliament.uk/about/living-heritage/transformingsociety/private-lives/religion/overview/witchcraft/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: parliament.uk">[Parliament UK]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">parliament.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Witchcraft - UK Parliament...</span></span></span>

That is why the Oakley date matters. The ordeal is recorded for 1735, just before the 1736 legal change took effect. It sits in the awkward space between old and new mental worlds. Formal executions and assize trials were fading, but popular suspicion had not vanished. A person could no longer be easily imagined as a lawful candidate for execution in the old way, yet neighbours might still believe that illness, bad luck, cattle deaths or family misfortune had a human supernatural cause.

Bedfordshire’s own records show that such beliefs had deeper county roots. Bedfordshire Archives’ women’s history source guide lists several witchcraft references: Agnes Jeffes hanged for witchcraft in 1594, Emma Saunders of Little Staughton accused in 1653, Elizabeth Pratt of Dunstable in 1667, Mary Chamberlain in 1693, and the Oakley testing in 1735. It also lists later traces, including a Maulden woman ducked at Flitton mill dam in 1780 and an 1848 Harrold case involving a cat’s heart stuck with pins. The pattern is not one continuous “witch panic”, but a long afterlife of witch-thinking in records, rumours, punishments and local memory.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://bedsarchives.bedford.gov.uk/Resources/PDF-Documents/ArchivesAndRecordOffice/Womens%20History%20Sources.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: bedsarchives.bedford.gov.uk">[Bedfordshire Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">bedsarchives.bedford.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Bedfordshire Archives Womens History Sources</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Bedfordshire Archives Womens History Sources</span></span></span>

## Bedfordshire’s wider fear of witches

Oakley should not be treated as an isolated oddity. Earlier Bedfordshire cases show the kinds of accusations that made witch-belief feel plausible to communities. At Little Staughton in 1653, Emma Saunders, wife of Robert Saunders, was accused at Bedford Quarter Sessions of “feloniously bewitching William Holland”. The quarter sessions could not deal with the matter, so she was sent on to the assizes; parish records later show that she survived and died in Little Staughton in 1670.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://bedsarchives.bedford.gov.uk/CommunityHistories/Little-Staughton/Witchcraft-in-Little-Staughton.aspx" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: bedsarchives.bedford.gov.uk">[Bedfordshire Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">bedsarchives.bedford.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Hosted By Bedford Borough Council: Witchcraft in Little Staughton...</span></span></span>

The Dunstable case of 1667 is even more revealing because it shows accusation spreading from one woman to others. Elizabeth Pratt was examined for supposed witch marks, then accused Mary Poole, Ursula Clarke and Mary Hudson of bewitchment. Later accusations connected Pratt with the deaths of children in the Settle family. Neighbours reportedly brought her to the sick boy and made him scratch her with a pin to see whether she would bleed, because a witch was believed not to bleed under such treatment. Pratt was sent to the county gaol and died before the assizes could take place.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://virtual-library.culturalservices.net/bedfordshire/vlib/0.digitised_resources/dunstable_digitisation_history_witches.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: virtual-library.culturalservices.net">[virtual-library.culturalservices.net]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">virtual-library.culturalservices.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Dunstable Witches</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Digitised Resources - The Virtual Library...</span></span></span>

These earlier stories help explain Oakley without excusing it. Bedfordshire witch fear often focused on vulnerable or socially exposed people, especially women, and on everyday disasters: illness, dead children, dead cattle, household conflict and begging at the door. The suspected witch was not a fairy-tale sorceress in a distant wood. She was a neighbour, a widow, a poor woman, an elderly woman, or somebody already resented. Oakley’s unnamed woman fits that pattern: she had a family to protect, a reputation to lose, and no safe way to disprove what others had decided.

## How suspicion became local folklore

The Oakley ordeal became folklore because it survived as a story attached to a specific place and a specific action. The River Great Ouse was not just scenery; it was the instrument of the test. Oakley Mill was not just an economic feature of the village; it became the remembered riverside setting where fear was performed in public. Bedfordshire Archives’ Oakley timeline reduces the event to a stark entry: in 1735 an infirm woman over sixty was accused of witchcraft, ducked three times, and because she floated was thought to be in league with the Devil.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://bedsarchives.bedford.gov.uk/CommunityHistories/Oakley/Timeline-of-Events-in-Oakley.aspx" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: bedsarchives.bedford.gov.uk">[Bedfordshire Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">bedsarchives.bedford.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Bedfordshire Archives Timeline of Events in Oakley</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Bedfordshire Archives Timeline of Events in Oakley</span></span></span>

That compressed local-memory version shows how folklore changes shape. The longer account includes the woman’s consent, the search for pins, the crowd’s shouting, the repeated ducking, the Bible weighing and the clergyman’s reaction. The timeline version keeps the core moral image: an old woman, a river, a test, a conclusion already waiting. It is shorter, easier to retell, and more portable as local legend.

There is also a warning here about how “witch stories” are remembered. Some modern retellings can make witchcraft cases feel quaint, spooky or picturesque. The Oakley case resists that treatment. Its interest lies not in proving that Bedfordshire had witches, but in showing how a community could turn fear into action and then preserve that action as a story. The folklore is not evidence of magic. It is evidence of belief under pressure.

## Why Oakley still matters in Bedfordshire folklore

Oakley matters because it gives Bedfordshire a witchcraft story with a precise landscape and a precise historical tension. It is not as famous as the witch-hunts of Essex or East Anglia, and it does not have the theatrical invented quality of later “witch” legends. Its power is quieter and harsher: a village near Bedford, a woman already marked by rumour, a river ordeal, a Bible used as a test, and a community struggling to decide whether misfortune had a human supernatural cause.

Within the county’s folklore, the story belongs beside other traditions of moral memory: haunted gallows places, black dog legends, sacred springs, old earthworks and village customs whose meanings have shifted over time. It also connects naturally with Bedfordshire’s Dunstable and Little Staughton witchcraft records, which show that accusations were not rare flashes of eccentricity but part of a wider county history of suspicion.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://virtual-library.culturalservices.net/bedfordshire/vlib/0.digitised_resources/dunstable_digitisation_history_witches.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: virtual-library.culturalservices.net">[culturalservices.net]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">virtual-library.culturalservices.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Dunstable Witches</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Digitised Resources - The Virtual Library...</span></span></span>

The most important thing to remember is that the Oakley ordeal took place when official England was stepping away from witch prosecution, but local fear had not caught up. That makes it one of Bedfordshire’s clearest examples of folklore as social history: a tale of the supernatural that reveals very human anxieties about age, gender, poverty, reputation, illness and blame.
