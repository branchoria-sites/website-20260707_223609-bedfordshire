## Introduction

Carlops is a small village at the southern edge of the Pentlands, just outside the modern [Midlothian]({{ 'where-midlothians-legends-meet-the/' | relative_url }}) council area but tightly bound to Midlothian’s older route-based folklore. Its best-known legend explains the place-name through the rocks called the Carlin’s Lowp or Witches’ Leap: two crags where “carlins”, an old Scots word associated with old women and witches, were said to leap across the gap for sport. The story matters because it shows how Midlothian folklore often clings to thresholds: road passes, county edges, rocky outcrops, rivers and old approaches to Edinburgh. Carlops is not simply a “witch village”; it is a boundary place where language, landscape and travelling tales have fused into one memorable local tradition.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://scotlandstartshere.com/point-of-interest/carlops/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: scotlandstartshere.com">[scotlandstartshere.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">scotlandstartshere.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on scotlandstartshere.com.</span></span></span>

The evidence is strongest for a place-name legend and modern local retelling, rather than for a proven historic execution site. That distinction makes the rocks more interesting, not less. Carlops preserves the way a striking landscape feature can attract witch-belief, literary memory, road folklore and county-border identity all at once.

## The Carlin’s Lowp place-name story

The usual explanation of Carlops begins with the phrase “Carlins Lowp”, commonly translated as “Witches’ Leap”. Local and tourism accounts describe two exposed rock faces near the south of the village, roughly 20 metres high and about the same distance apart, where witches were said to jump from one face to the other in the evening for amusement. Scotland Starts Here, a tourism initiative for the south of Scotland, gives this as the village’s name-origin story and links it directly to the two rock faces and the old tale of witches leaping the gap.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://scotlandstartshere.com/point-of-interest/carlops/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: scotlandstartshere.com">[Scotland Starts Here]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">scotlandstartshere.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on scotlandstartshere.com.</span></span></span>

The Scots word behind the legend is important. The Dictionaries of the Scots Language define “carline” as a woman, especially an old woman, often in a disparaging sense; Scots Language Centre material also glosses “carlin” or “carline” as a contemptuous term for an old woman and a witch. This does not mean every “carlin” place-name was originally about a witch trial. It means the word carried a strong enough charge of age, femaleness, uncanniness and ridicule to make it useful in naming strange rocks and wild places.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.dsl.ac.uk/entry/snd/carline" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: dsl.ac.uk">[Dictionaries of the Scots Language]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">dsl.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on dsl.ac.uk.</span></span></span>

That helps explain why Carlops feels like folklore even before the full story is told. The name itself performs the legend. A visitor does not need a long narrative of covens, curses or trials: “Witches’ Leap” is already a miniature story, with a character, an action and a dangerous landscape feature. The rocks become memorable because the place-name tells people how to imagine them.

There are two main versions in circulation. The lighter version says witches jumped between the rocks for entertainment. Friends of the Pentlands gives a more localised form, naming the two witches as Jenny Barry and Mause, and describing the Carlin’s Loup as the first thing seen above the car park when approaching Carlops from West Linton. The darker version, repeated in some modern accounts, says accused witches were thrown from the rocks as an ordeal: if they flew, they were guilty; if they fell, they died innocent. The first version is the better-attested place-name folklore; the second should be treated cautiously unless supported by stronger local records.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pentlandfriends.org.uk/carlops/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pentlandfriends.org.uk">[pentlandfriends.org.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pentlandfriends.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on pentlandfriends.org.uk.</span></span></span>

The rocks have also been affected by road history. Modern visitor accounts note that one of the two facing outcrops was largely blasted away or reduced during work on the Edinburgh road, leaving the surviving rock and the name to carry more of the story than the original physical pairing. That is a useful reminder that folklore sites are not frozen: roads, quarrying, traffic and village development can reshape the very features that gave rise to the legend.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.tripadvisor.co.uk/Attraction_Review-g1026905-d21349351-Reviews-The_Carlins_Lowp_At_Carlops-Carlops_Scottish_Borders_Scotland.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: tripadvisor.co.uk">[Tripadvisor]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">tripadvisor.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Tripadvisor THE CARLINS LOWP AT CARLOPS</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Tripadvisor THE CARLINS LOWP AT CARLOPS</span></span></span>

## Witch legends on the Pentland edge

Carlops sits in a landscape made for boundary stories. It lies between West Linton and Penicuik, on the Pentland edge, where upland routes lead north towards Edinburgh and south-west towards Tweeddale and the upper Clyde country. Vision of Britain places Carlops in Linton parish, north-west Peeblesshire, on the North Esk at the boundary with Edinburghshire, while Wikishire describes the village as being in Peeblesshire but “butted up” against Midlothian, with the North Esk marking the border.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.visionofbritain.org.uk/place/22364" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: visionofbritain.org.uk">[Vision of Britain]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">visionofbritain.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on visionofbritain.org.uk.</span></span></span>

That border position is central to the legend’s feel. A witch-leap is a crossing story: from rock to rock, from one side of a gap to another, from ordinary ground into uncanny space. Carlops itself sits at a similar crossing point in county terms. It is not in the modern Midlothian council area, yet it belongs naturally to the folklore of Midlothian’s southern edge because the North Esk, Penicuik approaches, Pentland passes and old Edinburgh road bind it to the older county story.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.visionofbritain.org.uk/place/22364" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: visionofbritain.org.uk">[Vision of Britain]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">visionofbritain.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on visionofbritain.org.uk.</span></span></span>

The A702 adds another layer. Wikishire describes Carlops as lying on the route from Upper Clydesdale to Edinburgh and notes the older road significance of the crossing point. Whether or not every detail of the “Roman road” claim is pressed too far, the broader pattern is clear: this was a place people passed through. Travelling routes are good carriers of folklore because names, warnings and jokes move with walkers, drovers, carters, soldiers, miners and later tourists.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://wikishire.co.uk/wiki/Carlops" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wikishire.co.uk">[Wikishire]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wikishire.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wikishire.co.uk.</span></span></span>

The landscape around Carlops is also full of story-ready features: Habbie’s Howe, Peggy’s Pool, the North Esk, the reservoir road, the exposed crags and the Pentland slopes above. Modern walking guides still frame the area through a mixture of route, scenery and legend, often starting at Witches’ Leap before moving through Habbie’s Howe and the wider Allan Ramsay landscape.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://sobt.co.uk/walk-carlops-circular/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sobt.co.uk">[Scotland Off the Beaten Track]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sobt.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on sobt.co.uk.</span></span></span>

This is why the Carlops rocks should not be read only as a spooky isolated attraction. They are part of a wider Pentland pattern in which dramatic landforms and old routes generate names that feel half-topographical and half-magical. The crags give the story a body; the road gives it an audience.

## How old is the tradition?

The safest answer is that the Carlops witch-rock tradition is older than most online retellings, but its exact age is hard to pin down. The modern village itself is usually dated to the late eighteenth century. Scotland Starts Here gives 1788, while other gazetteer-style accounts give 1784 and connect the village’s early development with weaving, coal and limestone work.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://scotlandstartshere.com/point-of-interest/carlops/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: scotlandstartshere.com">[Scotland Starts Here]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">scotlandstartshere.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on scotlandstartshere.com.</span></span></span>

That matters because the village name may preserve or formalise an older landscape-name rather than prove that a fully developed witch legend existed in its current form before the village. Many place-name legends work like that: a striking name survives, and stories gather around it, sometimes becoming more detailed as visitors ask what the name means.

There is a further complication. Scotland’s witch-hunting period was real and brutal. The University of Edinburgh’s Survey of Scottish Witchcraft covers the years 1563 to 1736 and identifies 3,837 people accused of witchcraft in Scotland. Historic Environment Scotland explains that the 1563 Witchcraft Act helped create a century and a half of witch-hunts, while Scotland’s People notes that both practising witchcraft and consulting witches were capital offences under the Act until repeal in 1736.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://witches.hca.ed.ac.uk/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: witches.hca.ed.ac.uk">[ed.ac.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">witches.hca.ed.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on ed.ac.uk.</span></span></span>

But a real national history of witch persecution does not automatically prove that every witch-named rock was an execution site. For Carlops, the best-publicised tale of accused women being thrown from the cliff is a modern dark retelling repeated in blogs and tourism-adjacent material. It may reflect a genuine local belief, or it may be a later moralising explanation attached to a place already called Witches’ Leap. Without clear court, kirk-session or parish evidence tied specifically to Carlops, it is better presented as a tradition or claim, not as established legal history.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.badwitch.co.uk/2018/08/scottish-folklore-witches-leap-at.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: badwitch.co.uk">[badwitch.co.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">badwitch.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on badwitch.co.uk.</span></span></span>

The lighter jumping-witches version is more stable across local descriptions. It appears in travel, walking and local-history accounts: witches leap between the two rocks, often in the evening, for pleasure or display. That version fits the name cleanly and does not require an undocumented execution practice. It also belongs to a wider Scottish habit of attaching “carlin” names to striking stones, crags and natural features associated with old hags, witches or the Cailleach-like supernatural old woman of tradition.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://scotlandstartshere.com/point-of-interest/carlops/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: scotlandstartshere.com">[scotlandstartshere.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">scotlandstartshere.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on scotlandstartshere.com.</span></span></span>

## Mause, Allan Ramsay and the literary afterlife of Carlops

Carlops folklore is not only oral and topographical; it is also literary. The village and its surrounding glens are closely associated with Allan Ramsay’s eighteenth-century pastoral comedy *The Gentle Shepherd*, first published in 1725. The area around Carlops, Newhall and Habbie’s Howe became strongly identified with the play, and modern local walking material still treats Habbie’s Howe as part of that Ramsay landscape.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://sobt.co.uk/walk-carlops-circular/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sobt.co.uk">[Scotland Off the Beaten Track]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sobt.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on sobt.co.uk.</span></span></span>

This matters for the witch-rocks because one of the named carlins in modern local retellings is Mause, a figure from *The Gentle Shepherd*. In the play, Mause is repeatedly treated as a woman suspected of witchcraft. In the Project Gutenberg text, Bauldy complains of “the witch ca’d Mause” who lives above the mill, while the plot later undercuts the accusation rather than presenting her as a simple supernatural villain.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/files/40639/40639-h/40639-h.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on gutenberg.org.</span></span></span>

That gives Carlops a particularly Scottish kind of folklore layering. A local landscape-name about carlins becomes associated with named figures; one of those figures is also a literary “witch” from Ramsay’s pastoral world; and the surrounding countryside becomes a place where readers, walkers and villagers can move through both real topography and literary memory. Friends of the Pentlands explicitly links Carlops to Ramsay, noting that many surrounding feature-names derive from characters and scenes in *The Gentle Shepherd*.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pentlandfriends.org.uk/carlops/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pentlandfriends.org.uk">[pentlandfriends.org.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pentlandfriends.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on pentlandfriends.org.uk.</span></span></span>

The result is not pure oral folklore, nor pure literary invention. It is a hybrid. The rocks and the Scots word “carlin” give the tradition its local root. Ramsay’s fame and the later heritage trail around Habbie’s Howe help keep the witchy vocabulary alive for visitors. Carlops therefore shows how a place can become legendary through repeated acts of naming: by villagers, map-makers, walkers, poets, innkeepers and tourism writers.

## Historic county borders and travelling tales

For a Midlothian folklore project, Carlops needs careful handling because it sits on a boundary. Administratively, the village is now in the Scottish Borders, and historically it is commonly placed in Peeblesshire or Tweeddale. Yet it lies immediately beside Midlothian’s southern edge, on routes leading to Penicuik and Edinburgh, and its river and hill context belong to the same Pentland-Esk story that shapes southern Midlothian folklore.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://wikishire.co.uk/wiki/Carlops" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wikishire.co.uk">[wikishire.co.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wikishire.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wikishire.co.uk.</span></span></span>

This is exactly the kind of place where historic county folklore can be more useful than modern council mapping. Legends rarely stop at administrative lines. A tale told at Carlops could travel north with workers and walkers towards Penicuik, Roslin and Edinburgh; it could travel south towards West Linton; it could be reinforced by inns, roads, quarrying, weaving settlements and later leisure walking. The rocks are local, but the story’s circulation is regional.

The Pentland Hills themselves encourage that wider reading. The modern Regional Park covers land across Edinburgh, Midlothian and West Lothian, while the hill range extends towards the Scottish Borders and South Lanarkshire. Official visitor information emphasises access through Midlothian, Edinburgh and West Lothian, while wider descriptions of the range include settlements such as Penicuik, Silverburn, Carlops and West Linton.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.pentlandhills.org/plan-visit/find-us" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pentlandhills.org">[pentlandhills.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pentlandhills.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on pentlandhills.org.</span></span></span>

Carlops therefore belongs in a Midlothian folklore branch not because the modern council boundary absorbs it, but because Midlothian’s legendary geography has always been porous at this edge. The North Esk, the A702 corridor, the Pentland passes and the literary landscape of Ramsay all make Carlops a southern threshold of the Midlothian imagination.

## What visitors are really seeing today

A visitor to Carlops today is not seeing a preserved witch-trial monument in the strict historical sense. They are seeing a surviving folklore site: a crag, a road, a village name and a cluster of stories that explain why the place feels charged. The Carlin’s Lowp is still visible as a roadside landmark, and modern walking routes use it as a start or feature before heading into Habbie’s Howe, Peggy’s Pool and the North Esk landscape.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://sobt.co.uk/walk-carlops-circular/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sobt.co.uk">[Scotland Off the Beaten Track]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sobt.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on sobt.co.uk.</span></span></span>

The most grounded way to read the site is in three layers:

**The landscape layer:** two crags and a gap, partly altered by road development, made memorable by their shape and position at the village edge.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.tripadvisor.co.uk/Attraction_Review-g1026905-d21349351-Reviews-The_Carlins_Lowp_At_Carlops-Carlops_Scottish_Borders_Scotland.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: tripadvisor.co.uk">[Tripadvisor]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">tripadvisor.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Tripadvisor THE CARLINS LOWP AT CARLOPS</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Tripadvisor THE CARLINS LOWP AT CARLOPS</span></span></span>

**The language layer:** the Scots word “carlin”, carrying meanings of old woman, crone and witch, turns the rocks into a supernatural place-name.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.dsl.ac.uk/entry/snd/carline" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: dsl.ac.uk">[Dictionaries of the Scots Language]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">dsl.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on dsl.ac.uk.</span></span></span>

**The storytelling layer:** local tradition, walking culture and literary memory add witches who leap for sport, named figures such as Jenny Barry and Mause, and darker modern versions about witch ordeals.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pentlandfriends.org.uk/carlops/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pentlandfriends.org.uk">[pentlandfriends.org.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pentlandfriends.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on pentlandfriends.org.uk.</span></span></span>

Seen this way, Carlops is not weakened by uncertainty. The uncertainty is part of what makes the site useful for understanding folklore. It shows the difference between a documented witch prosecution, a place-name tradition, a literary association and a modern heritage retelling. All four can be meaningful, but they should not be collapsed into one claim.

## Why Carlops matters to Midlothian folklore

Carlops gives Midlothian’s folklore map a southern edge. Roslin and [Rosslyn]({{ 'rosslyn/' | relative_url }}) Chapel may dominate the county’s mystery-tour imagination, but Carlops offers a smaller, sharper example of how local legend works: a single word, a pair of rocks, an old road and a border river create a story that people continue to repeat.

Its importance lies in the way it concentrates several themes common across Midlothian and the Lothians. It is a witch-place without needing a grand witch-hunt narrative. It is a rock legend without requiring prehistoric monumentality. It is a boundary tale that makes sense only when old counties, modern councils and travel routes are all held in view. And it is a literary-folkloric landscape where Allan Ramsay’s pastoral world overlaps with older naming habits and later walking culture.

The best way to tell the Carlops story, then, is neither to dismiss it as “just a name” nor to inflate it into a proven execution site. It is a place where Scots language, Pentland topography and county-edge memory have made a gap in the rocks into a leap of imagination. That is exactly the kind of modest but durable legend that gives Midlothian’s folklore its texture.
