## Introduction

Wemyss Caves feel legendary because they gather several kinds of Fife tradition into one short stretch of coast: Pictish symbols, early Christian crosses, a holy well, seasonal custom, medieval castle memory, cave names, lost chambers and modern rescue work. They are not simply “mysterious caves” in a loose romantic sense. They are a nationally protected archaeological landscape at East Wemyss, on the Fife shore of the Firth of Forth, where real carvings and real local practices have encouraged later stories to cluster around the cliffs. Historic Environment Scotland describes the scheduled monument as nine caves and associated coastal areas containing nationally important abstract and figurative rock art, including Pictish symbols, dating broadly from the Iron Age into the early medieval period.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://portal.historicenvironment.scot/apex/f?p=1505%3A300%3A%3A%3A%3A%3AVIEWTYPE%2CVIEWREF%3Adesignation%2CSM817" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: portal.historicenvironment.scot">[Historic Environment Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">portal.historicenvironment.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Historic Environment ScotlandWemyss Caves, East Wemyss (SM817)6 Dec 2022 — The caves contain a nationally important collection of abstrac...</span></span></span>



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/Fife_ef689b_wemyss_caves_180898-overview.webp" | relative_url }}" alt="Overview image for Wemyss Caves" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

For folklore readers, the value of Wemyss is the layering. Some things can be pointed to on the rock: birds, beasts, symbols, crosses, names, holdfasts and later graffiti. Some can be traced through custom: the Handsel Monday visits to St Margaret’s Well in Well Cave. Some sit closer to legend: secret tunnels, cave names, court traditions and stories of what may once have happened beneath Macduff’s Castle. The result is one of Fife’s clearest examples of a sacred carved landscape: not a single tale, but a place where archaeology, ritual memory and local storytelling keep interpreting one another.

## Why this Fife coast is more than a cave site

East Wemyss belongs to the older coastal Fife world of villages, castles, mining communities, fishing routes and sea-worn cliffs. The caves themselves were formed by sea action thousands of years ago, and Save Wemyss Ancient Caves Society says the surviving named caves east of the village include Court Cave, Doo Cave, Well Cave, Jonathan’s Cave, Sliding Cave and Gas Works Cave, while earlier caves such as Glassworks Cave and Michael Cave no longer survive.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://wemysscaves.org/the-caves/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wemysscaves.org">[wemysscaves.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wemysscaves.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">the caves</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">SWACSThe Wemyss Caves, which are believed to have been the product of sea action some 8000-5000 years ago, are situated on the north shor...</span></span></span>

That loss matters. Wemyss is a legendary landscape partly because it is incomplete. Some caves have collapsed, some carvings have disappeared, and some evidence now survives only in records, casts, drawings, photographs or digital models. The place therefore invites a very Fife kind of attention: looking at what remains, listening to what local memory says, and staying honest about what cannot now be recovered.

The site also sits below and around Macduff’s Castle, which anchors the caves in medieval Fife as well as in earlier Pictish and Christian history. Historic Environment Scotland notes that the caves have close associations with Macduff’s Castle during the medieval period, while the scheduled monument description singles out Court Cave, Jonathan’s Cave and Sliding Cave as especially important for surviving carved material.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://portal.historicenvironment.scot/apex/f?p=1505%3A300%3A%3A%3A%3A%3AVIEWTYPE%2CVIEWREF%3Adesignation%2CSM817" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: portal.historicenvironment.scot">[Historic Environment Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">portal.historicenvironment.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Historic Environment ScotlandWemyss Caves, East Wemyss (SM817)6 Dec 2022 — The caves contain a nationally important collection of abstrac...</span></span></span> This is one reason Wemyss can feel like a whole story-world rather than a single monument: the shore, castle, caves and village traditions all sit within walking distance of one another.

The name “Wemyss” itself helps the atmosphere. It is commonly linked to a word for caves, so the caves are not an incidental local feature but part of the identity of the place. In folklore terms, this matters because a name can keep a landscape feature alive in public memory long after individual stories fade. Wemyss is, in effect, a cave-place whose history kept being re-read by different communities.

## Pictish symbols and early Christian marks

The most famous reason to visit Wemyss is the Pictish carving. The caves contain one of the most important concentrations of Pictish cave art in Scotland, with symbols and animal figures carved directly into the sandstone walls. Historic Environment Scotland records 26 known surviving Pictish symbols in Court Cave, Jonathan’s Cave and Sliding Cave out of roughly 50 originally identified, including geese or swans, a salmon, a horse or lion, a wolf or dog, Pictish beasts, a boat with a human figure, and abstract symbols such as the double disc, crescent, mirror and arch.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://portal.historicenvironment.scot/apex/f?p=1505%3A300%3A%3A%3A%3A%3AVIEWTYPE%2CVIEWREF%3Adesignation%2CSM817" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: portal.historicenvironment.scot">[Historic Environment Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">portal.historicenvironment.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Historic Environment ScotlandWemyss Caves, East Wemyss (SM817)6 Dec 2022 — The caves contain a nationally important collection of abstrac...</span></span></span>

These carvings matter for Fife folklore because they are visible but not fully readable. The Picts left no easy written explanation of what such symbols meant, and modern interpretation has to stop short of pretending certainty. SCAPE, the coastal archaeology project associated with Wemyss recording work, explains that Pictish symbols clearly carried meaning for the people who made them, but that meaning is now lost to us; the carvings are recognisably Pictish because their forms match symbols on stones and objects across early medieval Scotland.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://scapetrust.org/picts-and-the-wemyss-caves/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: scapetrust.org">[scapetrust.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">scapetrust.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">picts and the wemyss caves</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">picts and the wemyss caves</span></span></span>

That uncertainty is not a weakness. It is exactly what gives Wemyss its pull. A visitor can see a bird, beast, fish or abstract mark and know it is not modern fantasy, while still having to accept that its original purpose is not recoverable in a neat sentence. For a folklore page, that distinction is crucial: the carvings are evidence of early medieval symbolic activity, not proof of any later supernatural claim. Yet they are also the kind of ancient, unexplained marks that naturally become magnets for sacred feeling, local legend and imaginative retelling.

Jonathan’s Cave is especially important. The 4D Wemyss Caves project records 11 Pictish carvings there, plus five birds that probably date to the same period, and notes the remarkable boat carving: if Pictish, it may be the earliest depiction of a boat in Scotland. The same cave also contains 19 early Christian crosses, ranging across different forms, raising the possibility that the cave later had Christian religious significance or was understood as a devotional place.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.4dwemysscaves.org/cave/index.php?ccode=jc" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: 4dwemysscaves.org">[4dwemysscaves.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">4dwemysscaves.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on 4dwemysscaves.org.</span></span></span>

This layering changes how the caves should be read. Wemyss is not simply “Pictish” and then later “Christian”, as if one story cleanly replaced another. The same physical spaces seem to have gathered different marks and uses over time. Birds, beasts, abstract symbols, crosses, names and later graffiti all occupy the same coastal sandstone. The sacred landscape here is cumulative: each period left marks that later people had to live with, explain, ignore, protect or reinterpret.

## Saints, wells and Handsel Monday customs

Well Cave gives Wemyss its clearest link between carved landscape and living custom. Historic Environment Scotland records that Well Cave was the focus of a local folk practice until the 19th century: on Handsel Monday, the first Monday after 12 January, people visited St Margaret’s Well in the cave, drank the water, gave gifts and often carved their names into the wall, leaving a substantial body of historic graffiti.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://portal.historicenvironment.scot/apex/f?p=1505%3A300%3A%3A%3A%3A%3AVIEWTYPE%2CVIEWREF%3Adesignation%2CSM817" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: portal.historicenvironment.scot">[Historic Environment Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">portal.historicenvironment.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Historic Environment ScotlandWemyss Caves, East Wemyss (SM817)6 Dec 2022 — The caves contain a nationally important collection of abstrac...</span></span></span>

This is where Wemyss moves from ancient carving into recognisable seasonal folklore. Handsel Monday was a New Year custom associated with giving small gifts, tips or tokens, often to servants, children, beggars or people to whom thanks were owed. Historic Environment Scotland’s own blog explains that the date could vary, with some places observing an “old style” version after 11 January because of the calendar change from Julian to Gregorian reckoning in 1752.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://blog.historicenvironment.scot/2017/01/just-thought-comes-hansel-monday/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: blog.historicenvironment.scot">[Historic Environment Scotland Blog]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">blog.historicenvironment.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Historic Environment Scotland Blog What is Hansel Monday?</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Historic Environment Scotland Blog What is Hansel Monday?</span></span></span>

At East Wemyss, the custom became site-specific. The 4D Wemyss Caves account says that up to the end of the 19th century young people from the village visited Well Cave on Handsel Monday to drink from St Margaret’s Well, sing songs and exchange gifts. SWACS describes the local version more vividly as a torch-lit procession into the cave, with wine, cakes, hymns and a social gathering in which the older people eventually went home and the younger ones continued the evening.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.4dwemysscaves.org/cave/index.php?ccode=wc" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: 4dwemysscaves.org">[4dwemysscaves.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">4dwemysscaves.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on 4dwemysscaves.org.</span></span></span>

That image explains why the custom has survived in memory: torches in a dark cave, water from a saint’s well, gifts at the turning of the year, names cut into the wall. It is easy to see why later writers would be tempted to push the practice far back into pre-Christian religion. SCAPE notes that drinking water from holy wells for curative or purifying purposes has pre-Christian parallels and suggests that an older sacred-spring tradition may lie behind the Wemyss custom, but careful wording matters: “may” is doing important work here.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://scapetrust.org/handsel-monday-and-the-wemyss-caves/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: scapetrust.org">[scapetrust.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">scapetrust.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Handsel Monday and the Wemyss Caves</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Handsel Monday and the Wemyss Caves</span></span></span>

The safest interpretation is that Well Cave preserves a Christianised, localised New Year water custom with possible older roots. The dedication to St Margaret, the singing of hymns and the Handsel Monday gift-giving make it historically legible in Christian and post-medieval Scottish terms. The dark cave, spring water, torchlight and calendar timing give it the deeper feel of seasonal folk practice. Wemyss is powerful because both readings can be held together without pretending that the evidence proves more than it does.

The well itself has changed. Modern visitor material notes that the water source associated with St Margaret’s Well has been diverted and that the tradition has died out.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.welcometofife.com/inspire-me-post/wemyss-caves" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: welcometofife.com">[Welcome to Fife - Visit]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">welcometofife.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Welcome to Fife</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Welcome to Fife</span></span></span> That loss is part of the story. The custom is no longer a living annual village rite in the old sense, but it is not forgotten: it is interpreted through SWACS talks, open days, digital resources and the visible graffiti left by generations of local people.

## Tunnel tales, cave names and what archaeology can verify

Wemyss has exactly the kind of setting that produces tunnel stories: a ruined castle above, caves below, blocked spaces, collapsed chambers and a coastline altered by erosion, mining and masonry. Modern event descriptions for Well Cave still refer to the question of whether there was “really” a secret tunnel connecting the cave to the castle above, showing that the tradition remains part of how visitors imagine the site.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.tickettailor.com/events/savewemyssancientcavessociety/1386757" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: tickettailor.com">[Ticket Tailor]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">tickettailor.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on tickettailor.com.</span></span></span>

The important point is not to treat every tunnel tale as fact, but not to dismiss it as meaningless either. Tunnel legends often grow around castles, caves, abbeys and old houses because they offer a simple explanation for hidden access, danger and elite secrecy. At Wemyss, the geography makes the story plausible enough to be memorable, but archaeology is more cautious. The National Record details for Well Cave note dressed blocks of local stone suggesting that steps once ascended from the cave towards the castle above, but that is not the same as proof of a long secret passage.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.trove.scot/place/53953" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: trove.scot">[Trove Scot]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">trove.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on trove.scot.</span></span></span>

The cave names themselves are a guide to the mixed nature of the landscape. Jonathan’s Cave is also known as Factor’s Cave and Cat Cave; local historical writing says Jonathan’s Cave was named after a poor man who lived there with his family in the late 1700s, while Court Cave is associated with a local baronial court in the Middle Ages and Doo Cave, or Dovecote Cave, with pigeons kept in carved nesting places.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.trove.scot/place/53979" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: trove.scot">[Trove Scot]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">trove.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on trove.scot.</span></span></span>

Those names are not all “folklore” in the same way. Some are functional, some social, some historical, some perhaps explanatory after the event. But together they show how local people used the caves: as shelter, court space, storage, dovecote, ritual site, curiosity and landmark. This is why Wemyss should not be reduced to Pictish symbols alone. The legendary quality comes from repeated human use, not from one isolated ancient mystery.

Archaeology confirms that the caves were not merely decorative backdrops. Wessex Archaeology’s account of the 2005 work for Time Team says five trenches were excavated inside Jonathan’s Cave, Well Cave and Sliding Cave, with further trenches outside Well Cave, alongside recording of an eroding coastal section.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://wessexarchaeologylibrary.org/library/repository/view/301/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wessexarchaeologylibrary.org">[Wessex Archaeology Open Library]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wessexarchaeologylibrary.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Wessex Archaeology Open Library Time Team</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wessex Archaeology Open Library Time Team</span></span></span> Earlier work around Jonathan’s Cave also found evidence for Iron Age and early historic occupation, including midden material, showing that human activity around the caves long pre-dated modern heritage interest.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.tafac.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2018/11/V13_p91-99_GibsonStevens.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: tafac.org.uk">[Tayside Fife Archaeology Committee]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">tafac.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on tafac.org.uk.</span></span></span>

The balance, then, is clear. Archaeology can verify carved symbols, crosses, occupation deposits, some structural modifications and the physical relationship between caves and castle. It can also identify uncertainty: what the symbols meant, whether a carved boat is definitely Pictish, how different cave uses related to one another, and how far tunnel stories reflect lost structures rather than later imagination. For folklore, those uncertainties are not empty gaps. They are where local storytelling does its work.

## How loss, damage and digital recording shape the modern legend

Wemyss is not a frozen ancient place. It is a fragile coastal site under active threat. SWACS describes the caves as a nationally important scheduled monument threatened by coastal erosion, structural instability and vandalism.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://wemysscaves.org/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wemysscaves.org">[wemysscaves.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wemysscaves.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wemysscaves.org.</span></span></span> SCAPE similarly identifies the coastline between East Wemyss and Buckhaven as rapidly eroding from the mid-20th century onwards, with no sign of that pressure simply disappearing.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://scapetrust.org/4d-wemyss-caves/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: scapetrust.org">[scapetrust.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">scapetrust.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">4d wemyss caves</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">4d wemyss caves</span></span></span>

That threat has already changed what can be seen. Historic Environment Scotland’s record notes that 26 Pictish symbols are now known to survive from around 50 originally identified, while SWACS records a particularly destructive modern episode in 1986, when a car was driven into Jonathan’s Cave and set alight, causing irreversible damage to carvings including Pictish beasts and a swan.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://portal.historicenvironment.scot/apex/f?p=1505%3A300%3A%3A%3A%3A%3AVIEWTYPE%2CVIEWREF%3Adesignation%2CSM817" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: portal.historicenvironment.scot">[Historic Environment Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">portal.historicenvironment.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Historic Environment ScotlandWemyss Caves, East Wemyss (SM817)6 Dec 2022 — The caves contain a nationally important collection of abstrac...</span></span></span>

This gives Wemyss a modern moral edge. The caves are legendary not because they are safely locked in the past, but because they are vulnerable. Every drawing, scan, guided tour and conservation decision now affects how future people will know the place. The 4D Wemyss Caves project and associated digital recording work respond to exactly that problem: the carvings and cave forms are being recorded in high detail so that scholars, local people and visitors can still study features that may be hard to see, unsafe to approach or eventually lost. SCAPE says its digital work has recorded the carvings and surrounding landscape in high-quality detail and has even helped show that Pictish carvings tend to appear on brightly lit walls.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://wemysscaves.org/about-us/archaeology/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wemysscaves.org">[wemysscaves.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wemysscaves.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wemysscaves.org.</span></span></span>

That last detail is especially useful for readers. It gently corrects the idea of Pictish symbols as purely hidden, occult marks buried in darkness. At least some were placed where light mattered. The caves may feel secret now, and some interiors are dark, but the carvings were not necessarily intended as invisible mysteries. They belonged to a lived coastal landscape of entrances, thresholds, light, sound and movement.

Modern access also shapes the story. SWACS currently explains that, because of ongoing damage, gated areas are closed except through guided tours, which help visitors see carvings that are otherwise difficult to identify and reduce the risk of further harm.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://wemysscaves.org/visiting-and-tours/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wemysscaves.org">[wemysscaves.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wemysscaves.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wemysscaves.org.</span></span></span> This is a good example of responsible folklore tourism. The point is not to make the caves feel less magical, but to make sure the magic is not bought at the cost of destroying the evidence.

## Why Wemyss matters in Fife folklore

Wemyss Caves matter because they show how Fife’s story culture often grows from place rather than from a single neat legend. The caves are not a fairy hill, a ghost road or a saint’s shrine in isolation. They are allusive rather than simple: Pictish animals and symbols, Christian crosses, a saint’s well, New Year gifts, castle associations, cave names, tunnel questions, vanished chambers and modern conservation all share the same shore.

That makes Wemyss one of the county’s strongest examples of a sacred carved landscape. “Sacred” here does not mean that every carving had a religious meaning that can now be decoded. It means that the place gathered special attention across time: symbolic carving, Christian marking, water custom, seasonal gathering and later reverence for antiquity. It is a landscape where people repeatedly behaved as though the caves mattered.

For readers exploring Fife folklore, Wemyss also helps separate evidence from atmosphere. The evidence is strong for the carvings, the crosses, the protected status, the Well Cave custom, historic graffiti, damage, loss and digital recording. The atmosphere comes from how those facts sit together: a dark cave under a castle; a spring named for a saint; torches on Handsel Monday; birds and beasts cut into stone; a boat whose date and meaning are still debated; local names that preserve past uses; and the knowledge that some marks have already vanished.

That is why Wemyss should be retold carefully. Calling it merely “mysterious” makes it smaller than it is. Treating every local story as proven history goes too far. The richer reading is also the truer one: Wemyss Caves are a Fife meeting place of archaeology, sacred memory and folklore, where the most haunting thing is not a single ghost or monster, but the survival of human marks whose full meanings are just out of reach.
