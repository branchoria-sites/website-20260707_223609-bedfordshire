## Introduction

For this project, Hertfordshire is treated as the historic county. That matters because county folklore often follows parish boundaries, old roads, estates and older county identities rather than today’s administrative lines. Historic county mapping projects treat Hertfordshire as one of the United Kingdom’s historic counties, while modern council and statistical maps may divide the area differently or relate it to London-facing administrative systems.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File%3ABritish_Isles_map_showing_UK%2C_Republic_of_Ireland%2C_and_historic_counties.svg" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: commons.wikimedia.org">[wikimedia.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">commons.wikimedia.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wikimedia.org.</span></span></span>

## Where Hertfordshire folklore lives on the map

Hertfordshire is an inland Home Counties shire, historically bordered by Middlesex to the south, Buckinghamshire to the west, Bedfordshire and Cambridgeshire to the north, and Essex to the east. Its folklore is shaped by that position. It is close enough to London for urban legends, highwaymen and moral panics to travel easily, but rural enough for village giants, field devils, harvest customs and holy wells to hold their ground.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://wikishire.co.uk/wiki/Hertfordshire" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wikishire.co.uk">[Wikishire]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wikishire.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wikishire.co.uk.</span></span></span>

The county’s legendary geography clusters around several kinds of place. St Albans carries saintly and pilgrimage traditions; Tring and Walkern are tied to late witchcraft history; Brent Pelham and Weston preserve heroic monster-slaying and giant lore; Royston is known for a mysterious carved cave; Hexton and Braughing preserve distinctive calendar customs. This is not a county where folklore belongs only to remote moorland or ancient stone circles. Much of it sits in parish churches, market routes, workhouses, pubs, lanes, wells and village greens.

Historic-county framing also helps prevent a common confusion. Some places now discussed in Greater London or neighbouring county contexts have older links with Hertfordshire or its borders, while folklore motifs themselves often cross boundaries. Black dogs, witches, Spring-heeled Jack, highwaywomen and holy wells are not uniquely Hertfordshire motifs. What makes them Hertfordshire traditions is the way they attach to particular local cases, documents and landscapes.

## The dragon-slayer of Brent Pelham

The most vivid Hertfordshire monster legend is the story of Piers Shonks of Brent Pelham. The tale says that Shonks, lord of the manor, killed a dragon that had settled near the village and was ruining the countryside. In local retellings the dragon lurks beneath an ancient yew tree between fields, while Shonks rides from his moated home, known as Shonkes, to destroy it. His tomb is traditionally identified inside St Mary’s Church at Brent Pelham.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.hertsmemories.org.uk/content/herts-history/towns-and-villages/brent_pelham/piers-shonks-hertfordshire-dragon-slayer" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: hertsmemories.org.uk">[Herts Memories]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">hertsmemories.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">piers shonks hertfordshire dragon slayer</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">piers shonks hertfordshire dragon slayer</span></span></span>

The story has the classic structure of an English dragon legend: a rural community under threat, a local champion, a lair in a rooted or hollow place, and a final complication involving the Devil. In the Piers Shonks version, the Devil is said to have claimed the dragon as his own and to have tried to take Shonks’s soul. The hero escapes by being buried in the church wall, neither fully inside nor outside the churchyard, so the Devil cannot seize him according to the terms of the bargain.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.hertsmemories.org.uk/content/herts-history/towns-and-villages/brent_pelham/piers-shonks-hertfordshire-dragon-slayer" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: hertsmemories.org.uk">[Herts Memories]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">hertsmemories.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">piers shonks hertfordshire dragon slayer</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">piers shonks hertfordshire dragon slayer</span></span></span>

What makes the tale valuable is not whether a dragon was ever believed to be a literal animal. It is the way the legend binds together visible local features: the church, the tomb, the moated manor earthworks, field names and a remembered boundary landscape. In that sense, Piers Shonks is a classic place-legend. It turns a village topography into a story that can be walked, pointed at and retold.

The legend has also attracted modern literary attention. Christopher Hadley’s work on the Brent Pelham dragon has treated the tale as a serious local mystery of memory, manuscript traces and landscape tradition, rather than simply a children’s monster story.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://rhakotis.com/2020/07/28/dragons/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: rhakotis.com">[Rhakotis]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">rhakotis.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Hollow Places by Christopher Hadley</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Hollow Places by Christopher Hadley</span></span></span>

## Jack O’Legs and the giant as local hero

Hertfordshire’s best-known giant is Jack O’Legs, associated with Weston, Baldock and the Great North Road. The legend presents him not simply as a monster but as a rough champion of the poor. In the common version, Jack lives in a cave or woodland near Weston. When bakers at Baldock raise prices during a shortage, he steals flour or bread and redistributes it to hungry people. The bakers capture, blind and execute him, but grant him a final wish: he shoots an arrow towards Weston and asks to be buried where it lands. Two stones in Weston churchyard are said to mark his grave.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://baldockhistory.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2022/08/ARTICLE-JACK-OLEGS.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: baldockhistory.org.uk">[Baldock History]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">baldockhistory.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ARTICLE JACK OLEGS</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ARTICLE JACK OLEGS</span></span></span>

The appeal is obvious. Jack is a Hertfordshire cousin of Robin Hood: an outlaw giant whose body is excessive, whose justice is rough, and whose memory is tied to a physical grave. The story also gives a social edge to the landscape around Baldock. Roads, markets, food prices and punishment all become part of the legend. The bakers are not incidental villains; they represent the power of those who control food in a hungry year.

The earliest substantial antiquarian notice often cited for the legend is Nathaniel Salmon’s eighteenth-century account of Hertfordshire, which records the Weston churchyard stones and the local belief that they marked a giant’s grave. Later retellings added or sharpened the Robin Hood-like details.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://baldockhistory.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2022/08/ARTICLE-JACK-OLEGS.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: baldockhistory.org.uk">[Baldock History]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">baldockhistory.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ARTICLE JACK OLEGS</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ARTICLE JACK OLEGS</span></span></span>

Jack O’Legs remains a strong example of how Hertfordshire folklore is preserved through signs, pubs, breweries, local history groups and village identity. A legend that may once have circulated orally now survives as heritage, branding and place-making, as well as story.

## Witchcraft: from pamphlet fear to public warning

Hertfordshire has unusually important witchcraft traditions because several cases are well documented, not merely rumoured. These stories should be handled carefully. They are not proof of witchcraft; they are evidence of accusation, fear, gendered suspicion, legal process, mob violence and changing belief.

One of the earliest notable cases is that of Johane Harrison and her daughter Anne of Royston. A London pamphlet of 1606 described their conviction and execution at Hertford for murder by witchcraft. The account includes material that seventeenth-century readers would have understood as damning magical evidence, but modern readers should recognise the pamphlet as a product of a culture that expected witchcraft to leave narrative and physical signs.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.hertsmemories.org.uk/content/herts-history/topics/witchcraft/johane-and-anne-harrison-witches-of-royston" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: hertsmemories.org.uk">[Herts Memories]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">hertsmemories.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">johane and anne harrison witches of royston</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">johane and anne harrison witches of royston</span></span></span>

The case of Jane Wenham of Walkern in 1712 is even more famous. She was tried at Hertford and convicted of witchcraft, but the conviction was set aside and she was pardoned. The case became a national argument between those who still defended witch prosecutions and those who treated such claims as irrational or legally unsafe. Contemporary pamphlets show the dispute in print, with one side presenting the accusation and another examining the “Hertfordshire witchcraft” case sceptically.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/details/b30508162" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[archive.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on archive.org.</span></span></span>

The darkest Hertfordshire witchcraft story is Ruth Osborne of Tring in 1751. Ruth and her husband John were accused by local people and seized by a mob. Ruth was ducked at Long Marston near Tring and died. Thomas Colley, a chimney sweep involved in the attack, was convicted of her murder and executed; Hertfordshire Archives preserve material relating to his confession.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.hertsmemories.org.uk/content/herts-history/towns-and-villages/tring/the-confession-of-thomas-colley-to-the-murder-of-ruth-osborne-a-suspected-witch-1751" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: hertsmemories.org.uk">[Herts Memories]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">hertsmemories.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on hertsmemories.org.uk.</span></span></span>

This case is often retold as one of the last violent outbreaks of witch-belief in England. Its power lies in the clash between popular belief and state authority. Colley’s punishment was meant to make clear that killing a suspected witch was murder, not community justice. Yet accounts of the crowd’s sympathy for him show that official scepticism did not instantly erase local belief.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://tringlocalhistorymuseum.org.uk/society/2024-4.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: tringlocalhistorymuseum.org.uk">[Tring Local History Museum]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">tringlocalhistorymuseum.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on tringlocalhistorymuseum.org.uk.</span></span></span>

## Black dogs, ghosts and death omens

Hertfordshire has its share of spectral animals and death omens. The black dog is part of a much wider English tradition, where a large dark hound may haunt roads, churchyards, bridges or lonely places and appear as an omen of death or misfortune. In Hertfordshire, the Tring black dog tradition becomes tangled with the Ruth Osborne story and later supernatural interpretation. Herts Memories notes that guardian souls were sometimes imagined as black dogs with fiery eyes, while at Tring the spirit was said in one account to be that of Mother Shipton.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.hertsmemories.org.uk/content/herts-history/topics/traditional-hertfordshire-project-2017/hertfordshire-archives-local-studies/ghosts-hauntings-hertfordshire" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: hertsmemories.org.uk">[Herts Memories]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">hertsmemories.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ghosts hauntings hertfordshire</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ghosts hauntings hertfordshire</span></span></span>

The county’s ghost lore is not limited to dogs. Hertfordshire traditions include death coaches, plague carts, shining boys and haunted houses. Herts Memories records, for example, a hearse with black horses and a headless coachman on the road towards St Paul’s Walden Bury, a plague cart said to drive around Datchworth Green, and a “shining boy” at Knebworth House connected with deaths in the Lytton family.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.hertsmemories.org.uk/content/herts-history/topics/traditional-hertfordshire-project-2017/hertfordshire-archives-local-studies/funeral-mourning-customs-hertfordshire" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: hertsmemories.org.uk">[Herts Memories]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">hertsmemories.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">funeral mourning customs hertfordshire</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">funeral mourning customs hertfordshire</span></span></span>

These stories are best read as social folklore rather than ghost-hunting evidence. They teach people where danger, memory or grief is imagined to gather. A road becomes ominous because of repeated telling; a family house gains a death-token; a village green remembers plague; an animal’s cry becomes a warning. In a county of old estates and busy roads, the supernatural often appears as movement: a coach passing, a hound padding, a cart returning.

Hertfordshire also has funeral and mourning customs that show how belief worked in everyday life. Omens included howling dogs, unusual animal behaviour, unlucky flowers and the timing of church bells. Customs such as closing curtains, tolling passing bells, providing food for bearers, and telling the bees about a death linked the household, parish and natural world in one ritual order.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.hertsmemories.org.uk/content/herts-history/topics/traditional-hertfordshire-project-2017/hertfordshire-archives-local-studies/funeral-mourning-customs-hertfordshire" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: hertsmemories.org.uk">[Herts Memories]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">hertsmemories.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">funeral mourning customs hertfordshire</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">funeral mourning customs hertfordshire</span></span></span>

## Holy wells, saints and sacred water

St Albans gives Hertfordshire one of its deepest sacred landscapes. The city is associated with Alban, widely honoured as Britain’s first Christian martyr, and St Albans Cathedral presents itself as standing on the oldest site of continual Christian worship in Britain.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.stalbanscathedral.org/the-story-of-alban" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: stalbanscathedral.org">[St Albans Cathedral]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">stalbanscathedral.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">the story of alban</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">the story of alban</span></span></span>

The Holy Well at St Albans belongs to the legend of Alban’s martyrdom. In the traditional story, Alban was thirsty on the way to execution on what is now Holywell Hill, and a spring burst forth so that he could drink. Herts Memories describes this as perhaps the most celebrated of Hertfordshire’s sacred springs.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.hertsmemories.org.uk/content/herts-history/places/rivers-and-canals/sacred-wells-and-springs-of-hertfordshire" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: hertsmemories.org.uk">[Herts Memories]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">hertsmemories.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">sacred wells and springs of hertfordshire</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">sacred wells and springs of hertfordshire</span></span></span>

Holy wells often sit at the meeting-point of Christian devotion, older water reverence, healing belief and local memory. Hertfordshire’s wells and springs are no exception. Some are attached to saints, some to healing, and some to stories of the Devil or magical practice. Herts Memories notes, for example, that St John’s Well was said to relieve ague, an old term often used for feverish illness, and that Hertfordshire sacred springs could be folded into both pilgrimage and folk-healing traditions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.hertsmemories.org.uk/content/herts-history/towns-and-villages/st-albans/the-devils-work-in-hertfordshire" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: hertsmemories.org.uk">[Herts Memories]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">hertsmemories.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">the devils work in hertfordshire</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">the devils work in hertfordshire</span></span></span>

The important point for readers is that a holy well is not just a water source with a story added. It is a place where landscape, body and belief meet. People visited such places because water could heal, bless, mark a saint’s presence, or preserve an older sense that springs were unusually powerful.

## Strange stones, caves and hidden places

Hertfordshire does not have the same national reputation for megalithic monuments as Wiltshire or Orkney, but it has its own stone folklore. The most distinctive is Hertfordshire puddingstone, a hard conglomerate rock made of rounded pebbles cemented into a pale matrix. The Hertfordshire Geological Society notes that it is found mainly in Hertfordshire, with related sources in east Buckinghamshire and west Essex, and that much of it has been moved by ice or by human use.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.hertsgeolsoc.org.uk/puddingstone/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: hertsgeolsoc.org.uk">[hertsgeolsoc.org.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">hertsgeolsoc.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Hertfordshire Puddingstone</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Hertfordshire Puddingstone</span></span></span>

Puddingstone attracted folk belief because it looks uncanny: like a natural cake or mass of pebbles fused into one object. In Hertfordshire folklore it was credited with protective power against witchcraft. One widely repeated tradition says that in Aldenham in 1662, a suspected witch was buried with a piece of puddingstone on her coffin to prevent her return after death.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ztevetevans.wordpress.com/2020/04/08/folkore-superstitions-and-customs-around-hertforshire-puddingstone/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ztevetevans.wordpress.com">[Under the influence!]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ztevetevans.wordpress.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Under the influence!Folklore, Superstitions and Customs around Hertfordshire</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Under the influence!Folklore, Superstitions and Customs around Hertfordshire</span></span></span>

Royston Cave is another Hertfordshire place where the line between history and speculation must be kept clear. It is a man-made chalk chamber beneath Royston, rediscovered in 1742, with extensive carvings including Christian imagery such as the Crucifixion, the Holy Family and saints. The cave has been linked in popular theories to the Knights Templar, Freemasons, hermits and secret worship, but its origin and function remain uncertain.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.hertsmemories.org.uk/content/herts-history/towns-and-villages/royston/royston-cave/royston-cave" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: hertsmemories.org.uk">[Herts Memories]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">hertsmemories.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">royston cave</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">royston cave</span></span></span>

The cave is exactly the kind of place that generates folklore because it invites explanation but resists certainty. Its underground setting, carvings and lack of a simple documentary origin make it a magnet for imaginative retelling. A grounded account can enjoy that mystery without pretending that the most exciting theory is the best-supported one.

## Devils in fields and on the road

The Devil appears often in Hertfordshire lore, usually as a moral figure who punishes pride, greed, oath-breaking or impiety. The most famous example is *The Mowing-Devil: or, Strange News out of Hartford-shire*, a 1678 pamphlet about a farmer who refuses to pay a mower’s price and says he would rather the Devil cut the field. According to the pamphlet, the field appears to burn by night and is found perfectly mown the next morning.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.hertsmemories.org.uk/content/herts-history/topics/traditional-hertfordshire-project-2017/hertfordshire-archives-local-studies/hertfordshire-mowing-devil" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: hertsmemories.org.uk">[hertsmemories.org.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">hertsmemories.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">hertfordshire mowing devil</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">hertfordshire mowing devil</span></span></span>

Modern readers often encounter the Mowing Devil through crop-circle discussions, because the woodcut seems to show a demonic figure cutting a circular pattern. That is a modern reframing. The older pamphlet is better understood as a moralising “strange news” tale about labour, bargaining, blasphemous speech and divine or diabolical judgement. Herts Memories points out that the pamphlet does not name the exact place in Hertfordshire where the event supposedly happened.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.hertsmemories.org.uk/content/herts-history/topics/traditional-hertfordshire-project-2017/hertfordshire-archives-local-studies/hertfordshire-mowing-devil" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: hertsmemories.org.uk">[Herts Memories]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">hertsmemories.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">hertfordshire mowing devil</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">hertfordshire mowing devil</span></span></span>

The Devil also belongs to the Piers Shonks legend, where he is outwitted by burial in a liminal church-wall position. That motif is important: in Hertfordshire stories, the Devil is dangerous, but he can be tricked through legalistic, spatial or ritual cleverness. The church wall, the field edge and the oath all become places where words and boundaries matter.

## Highwaywomen, leaping terrors and modern legend

Hertfordshire folklore did not stop in the medieval or early modern period. The county also absorbed and reshaped more modern legends, including highway robbery and Victorian fright stories.

The Wicked Lady of Markyate is the best-known example. The legend identifies Katherine Ferrers, a seventeenth-century gentlewoman, as a masked highwaywoman who robbed travellers by night. Herts Memories retells the local association with Markyate, while other historical discussions caution that the dramatic highwaywoman identity is difficult to prove and may have grown through later confusion, fiction and film.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.hertsmemories.org.uk/content/herts-history/towns-and-villages/sandridge/wicked-lady-of-markyate" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: hertsmemories.org.uk">[hertsmemories.org.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">hertsmemories.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">wicked lady of markyate</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">wicked lady of markyate</span></span></span>

This distinction matters. The Wicked Lady is part historical person, part local legend and part popular entertainment. The 1944 novel *The Life and Death of the Wicked Lady Skelton* and the 1945 film *The Wicked Lady* helped turn a Hertfordshire-associated story into national popular culture.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Katherine_Ferrers" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Katherine Ferrers</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Katherine Ferrers</span></span></span>

Spring-heeled Jack also reached Hertfordshire. The wider legend began in the nineteenth century as a frightening urban figure who leapt, attacked and vanished; Herts Memories records reports in various parts of Hertfordshire, with particular concern in Bushey.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.hertsmemories.org.uk/content/herts-history/towns-and-villages/hitchin/spring-heeled-jack-in-hertfordshire" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: hertsmemories.org.uk">[Herts Memories]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">hertsmemories.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Herts Memories Spring-heeled Jack in Hertfordshire</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Herts Memories Spring-heeled Jack in Hertfordshire</span></span></span>

These modern legends show Hertfordshire’s closeness to London’s print culture and rumour networks. A tale could move by newspaper, penny dreadful, gossip and local alarm, then settle into county memory. Hertfordshire’s folklore is therefore not only rural survivals; it includes media-shaped legends that were already modern when they arrived.

## Calendar customs and village memory

Hertfordshire’s customs are often quieter than its monster legends, but they reveal how communities kept time. Hock-Tide at Hexton is one of the most striking. Hocktide was the Monday and Tuesday after Easter, once associated in England with games, dues, collections and festive disorder. In Hexton, Francis Taverner recorded that local celebrations continued for some years into Elizabeth I’s reign, remembered through older villagers’ accounts.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.efdss.org/learning/resources/beginners-guides/48-british-folk-customs-from-plough-monday-to-hocktide/3389-hocktide" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: efdss.org">[English Folk Dance and Song Society]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">efdss.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">English Folk Dance and Song Society Hocktide</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">English Folk Dance and Song Society Hocktide</span></span></span>

Old Man’s Day at Braughing is more unusual because it is still locally recognisable as a named custom. It takes place on 2 October and commemorates Matthew Wall, who according to the story was being carried to burial in 1571 when a pallbearer stumbled, the coffin was jolted, and Wall revived. The custom involves telling the story and the symbolic sweeping of Fleece Lane, because the obstruction in the lane is what supposedly saved him from premature burial.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.hertsmemories.org.uk/content/herts-history/towns-and-villages/braughing/old-mans-day-famous-braughing-tradition" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: hertsmemories.org.uk">[hertsmemories.org.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">hertsmemories.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Herts Memories Old Man&#x27;s Day</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Herts Memories Old Man&#x27;s Day</span></span></span>

Marriage, funeral, May Day and harvest customs also appear strongly in Hertfordshire collections. Wedding beliefs included unlucky times of year, walking weddings, churchyard refreshments and local drinking customs; death customs included omens, closed curtains, passing bells, food for coffin-bearers and telling the bees.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.hertsmemories.org.uk/content/herts-history/topics/traditional-hertfordshire-project-2017/hertfordshire-archives-local-studies/marriage-customs-hertfordshire" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: hertsmemories.org.uk">[Herts Memories]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">hertsmemories.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">marriage customs hertfordshire</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">marriage customs hertfordshire</span></span></span>

These customs matter because they show folklore as behaviour, not just story. A legend can be told; a custom has to be done. When a village repeats a ceremony, even playfully, it turns memory into public action.

## Who collected and preserved Hertfordshire folklore?

Much of what survives has passed through antiquarian and local-history collecting. William Blyth Gerish is especially important for Hertfordshire folklore. His *Hertfordshire Folk Lore* and related reprints gathered beliefs, charms, customs, [witchcraft cases]({{ 'witchcraft-cases/' | relative_url }}), strange pamphlets and local traditions in the early twentieth century, and later writers repeatedly drew on his work. Catalogue records and Herts Memories both point to Gerish as a key preserver of county material.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://catalog.hathitrust.org/Record/011255505" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: catalog.hathitrust.org">[HathiTrust]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">catalog.hathitrust.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on hathitrust.org.</span></span></span>

Doris Jones-Baker’s *The Folklore of Hertfordshire*, published in 1977, is another major county collection, frequently referenced by Hertfordshire local-history pages on customs, death traditions and village belief.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.hertsmemories.org.uk/content/herts-history/topics/traditional-hertfordshire-project-2017/hertfordshire-archives-local-studies/funeral-mourning-customs-hertfordshire" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: hertsmemories.org.uk">[Herts Memories]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">hertsmemories.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">funeral mourning customs hertfordshire</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">funeral mourning customs hertfordshire</span></span></span>

Hertfordshire Archives and Local Studies, through the Herts Memories community archive, now plays a central public role. Its Traditional Hertfordshire material brings together pages on ghosts, wells, witchcraft, customs, Piers Shonks, Jack O’Legs, the Mowing Devil, the Wicked Lady, Spring-heeled Jack and many other local traditions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.hertsmemories.org.uk/content/category/herts-history/topics/traditional-hertfordshire-project-2017/hertfordshire-archives-local-studies/page/2" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: hertsmemories.org.uk">[Herts Memories]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">hertsmemories.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on hertsmemories.org.uk.</span></span></span>

This collecting history affects how the folklore should be read. Some items are old oral traditions written down late. Some are early printed pamphlets with moral or commercial motives. Some are antiquarian interpretations. Some are modern heritage retellings. A good Hertfordshire folklore page should preserve the pleasure of the tales while making those layers visible.

## What Hertfordshire folklore says about the county

Hertfordshire folklore is unusually good at showing how the ordinary becomes strange. A field of oats becomes the Mowing Devil’s field. A village coffin becomes Old Man’s Day. A church wall becomes a legal loophole against the Devil. A lump of puddingstone becomes protection against witchcraft. A market road becomes the route of a giant outlaw’s justice.

The county’s strongest traditions also reveal recurring concerns:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **Boundaries:** church walls, county edges, roads, wells, caves and field margins are where the strange often appears.
* **Justice and injustice:** Jack O’Legs punishes greedy bakers; Ruth Osborne is killed by a mob; Jane Wenham’s case exposes the tension between belief and law.
* **Food and labour:** bread, mowing, harvest, feasting and funeral hospitality recur again and again.
* **Memory in objects:** stones, tombs, wells, carved caves and parish records carry stories forward when oral tradition thins.
* **Modern retelling:** the Wicked Lady, Spring-heeled Jack and even the Mowing Devil show how print, tourism and popular culture reshape older material.

</div>

The result is a county folklore that feels less like a remote mythic world and more like a set of charged local places. Hertfordshire’s legends are at their best when read with a map in one hand and a sceptical but generous imagination in the other.

    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-selected-market-flag], [data-ebay-trigger-market-flag]')).forEach(function (flag) {
      flag.className = flagClass(marketId);
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-trigger-market-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = selectedText;
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-domain-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = domainLabel(marketId);
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-option]')).forEach(function (button) {
      button.setAttribute('aria-selected', button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-option') === marketId ? 'true' : 'false');
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      var active = button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button') === marketId;
      button.setAttribute('aria-pressed', active ? 'true' : 'false');
    });
    section.setAttribute('data-ebay-selected-market', marketId);
    if (persist) {
      try { if (window.localStorage) localStorage.setItem('phoenix-ebay-market', marketId); } catch (err) {}
    }
    closeMenu(section);
  }
  sections.forEach(function (section) {
    if (section.getAttribute('data-ebay-localized-init') === '1') return;
    section.setAttribute('data-ebay-localized-init', '1');
    var available = availableMarkets(section);
    var marketId = defaultMarket;
    if (window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation && window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation.inferMarket) {
      marketId = window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation.inferMarket(available, {
        storageKey: 'phoenix-ebay-market',
        defaultMarket: defaultMarket
      });
    } else if (available.indexOf(defaultMarket)< 0) {
      marketId = available[0] || defaultMarket;
    }
    var select = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-select]');
    if (select) {
      select.addEventListener('change', function () {
        applyMarket(section, select.value, true);
      });
    }
    var trigger = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-trigger]');
    var menu = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-menu]');
    if (trigger && menu) {
      trigger.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
        event.stopPropagation();
        var expanded = trigger.getAttribute('aria-expanded') === 'true';
        menu.hidden = expanded;
        trigger.setAttribute('aria-expanded', expanded ? 'false' : 'true');
        var picker = trigger.closest('.fr-ebay-market-picker');
        if (picker) picker.classList.toggle('fr-ebay-market-picker--open', !expanded);
      });
      Array.prototype.slice.call(menu.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-option]')).forEach(function (button) {
        button.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
          event.stopPropagation();
          applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-option'), true);
        });
      });
      document.addEventListener('click', function () { closeMenu(section); });
    }
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      button.addEventListener('click', function () {
        applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button'), true);
      });
    });
    applyMarket(section, marketId, false);
  });
