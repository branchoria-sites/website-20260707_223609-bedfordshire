## Introduction

For this project, “County Londonderry” means the historic county: one of the six historic counties of Northern Ireland, now divided for local government purposes mainly across Derry and Strabane, Causeway Coast and Glens, and Mid Ulster. That distinction matters because folklore rarely obeys modern council boundaries. Traditions along Lough Foyle, the Bann, the Sperrins, and the Donegal border often cross present-day administrative lines, while the county remains a useful historic frame for local legend and identity.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikivoyage.org/wiki/County_Londonderry" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: en.wikivoyage.org">[wikivoyage.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">en.wikivoyage.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wikivoyage.org.</span></span></span>

## Where the county’s folklore lives

County Londonderry’s folklore is strongly tied to landscape. The Foyle gives the city its dramatic setting; the Bann and Roe carry older settlement and travel routes; the Sperrins and Benbradagh create upland story-country; and Magilligan and Lough Foyle open the county towards Donegal and the north coast. Archaeology adds another layer. Northern Ireland’s Historic Environment Record holds information on around 17,000 archaeological sites and monuments, and County Londonderry includes raths, standing stones, holy wells, crannogs, tombs, bullaun stones and “Giant’s Grave” place names that often attract folklore even when the monument itself is much older than the tale attached to it.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.communities-ni.gov.uk/services/sites-and-monuments-record" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: communities-ni.gov.uk">[Department for Communities]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">communities-ni.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Department for Communities Sites and Monuments Record</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Department for Communities Sites and Monuments Record</span></span></span>

The old county is also unusually marked by a double memory: early Christian Derry, associated with St Columba, and the seventeenth-century walled city built during the Plantation period. The name Derry comes from an oak-grove place name, while Londonderry reflects the early modern role of the London livery companies and the Honourable the Irish Society. That tension is not just political history; it affects how stories are told, named and claimed. A saint’s well, a siege wall, a family song, and a fairy fort may belong to different communities of memory, yet they all sit within the same county landscape.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nationalchurchestrust.org/explore/story/walled-city" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nationalchurchestrust.org">[nationalchurchestrust.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nationalchurchestrust.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nationalchurchestrust.org.</span></span></span>

## St Columba, oak leaves and holy wells

The most important saintly folklore in County Londonderry centres on St Columba, also known as Columcille, patron of Derry. Church and heritage sources present Derry as an early Christian site associated with the saint, and the city’s name is linked to an oak grove. That oak memory survives in modern custom around St Columba’s Well: Slí Cholmcille describes a tradition in which the well is decorated with oak leaves on 9 June, St Columba’s feast day, and pilgrims wear an oak leaf on their clothing.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nationalchurchestrust.org/explore/story/walled-city" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nationalchurchestrust.org">[National Churches Trust]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nationalchurchestrust.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nationalchurchestrust.org.</span></span></span>

The well’s legend is a classic saintly miracle story. According to the tradition preserved on the St Columba Way, a child was brought to Columba for baptism, but no water was nearby; the saint made the sign of the cross over a stone and water sprang from it. This is exactly the kind of story that turns a practical local feature into a sacred place: the well is not merely water, but a sign that the saint’s presence altered the landscape.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.colmcille.org/en/map-marker/4-5-st-columbs-well/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: colmcille.org">[colmcille.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">colmcille.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on colmcille.org.</span></span></span>

There is also evidence that Derry’s well tradition was once more complex than a single surviving site. Local heritage accounts refer to St Columb’s Wells in the plural, and note that they appeared on early maps, including Docwra’s 1600 map and Neville’s 1689 map. Later antiquarian discussion describes a group of three wells and connects them with a nearby bullaun stone, a hollowed stone often linked in folk practice with healing or ritual use. The details should be treated carefully, because some of the older physical setting has changed or disappeared, but the pattern is clear: saint, stone, water, annual day and local devotion were once closely bound together in Derry’s sacred geography.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://nimc.co.uk/members/tower-museum/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nimc.co.uk">[nimc.co.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nimc.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">tower museum</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">tower museum</span></span></span>

## Fairy forts, raths and the rules of respect

The strongest everyday folklore pattern in County Londonderry is not a single dramatic monster story but the wider Ulster and Irish belief-world of fairy forts, raths, lone trees and dangerous interference with old places. The Schools’ Collection, gathered by schoolchildren in 1937–39 under the Irish Folklore Commission, is central here because it preserves local memories in the voices of pupils, families and older neighbours rather than in polished literary form.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ucd.ie/irishfolklore/en/collections/schoolscollectionduchas/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ucd.ie">[University College Dublin]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ucd.ie</span><span class="citation-popover-title">University College Dublin Schools&#x27; Collection (Dúchas</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">University College Dublin Schools&#x27; Collection (Dúchas</span></span></span>

County Londonderry’s archaeological record gives this belief-world plenty of physical anchors. Raths and enclosures are recorded across the county, and local heritage profiles for parts of the county show standing stones, enclosures and raths among the most common recorded historic site types in some rural wards. A rath could be explained by archaeology as an early medieval enclosed farmstead, yet in folk speech it might become a fairy fort: a place not to dig, level, cut through, or treat casually.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.communities-ni.gov.uk/services/sites-and-monuments-record" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: communities-ni.gov.uk">[communities-ni.gov.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">communities-ni.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Department for Communities Sites and Monuments Record</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Department for Communities Sites and Monuments Record</span></span></span>

The warning stories are consistent across Ireland: treasure may be hidden in a fort, but those who dig may be frightened away; a lone thorn or rowan may mark a fairy place; illness or misfortune may follow disrespect. A Schools’ Collection item from Cor na Muclach tells of a fort with a mountain ash tree said to have grown in one night, with a dream of gold under the tree and a warning voice heard by the would-be seeker. The source is not in County Londonderry, but it records the same type of rural belief found throughout Ulster and helps explain how local people read forts, trees and hidden treasure stories.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.duchas.ie/en/cbes/4605945/4604668/4645083" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: duchas.ie">[Dúchas]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">duchas.ie</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on duchas.ie.</span></span></span>

For County Londonderry, the key point is not that every rath has a surviving named fairy tale. It is that the county’s old earthworks and field monuments belonged to a wider culture of caution. These places were useful in story because they were visible, old, unexplained to many local people, and often left alone. The folklore protected them through fear, reverence and habit long before modern heritage law described them as scheduled monuments or archaeological sites.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.communities-ni.gov.uk/services/sites-and-monuments-record" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: communities-ni.gov.uk">[Department for Communities]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">communities-ni.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Department for Communities Sites and Monuments Record</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Department for Communities Sites and Monuments Record</span></span></span>

## Limavady and the Leap of the Dog

Limavady has one of County Londonderry’s clearest place-name legends. The town’s name is commonly explained from “Leap of the Dog”, and local tourism and history sources link it to the Dog Leap in Roe Valley Country Park, near the former O’Cahan stronghold. The best-known version tells of a faithful wolfhound that leapt across the River Roe gorge to warn its master of approaching enemies. Another version has the chief himself escaping on horseback, with pursuers unable to follow.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.causewaycoastalroute.com/limavady-history" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: causewaycoastalroute.com">[Causeway Coastal Route]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">causewaycoastalroute.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on causewaycoastalroute.com.</span></span></span>

The legend is powerful because it does several things at once. It explains a place name, ties the town to the O’Cahan lords, turns the Roe gorge into a story-stage, and gives Limavady an animal hero whose loyalty is easier to remember than a date or battle. The physical Dog Leap is also a real geological location in Roe Valley Country Park; geological descriptions place it about 3 km south-south-west of Limavady and describe the complex schist rocks of the gorge. Folklore and geology therefore meet neatly: the rock formation makes the leap imaginable, and the tale makes the rock memorable.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.habitas.org.uk/escr/site.asp?Item=151" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: habitas.org.uk">[Habitas]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">habitas.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on habitas.org.uk.</span></span></span>

The story is still actively retold. A sculpture trail in Limavady commemorates the famous legend through Maurice Harron’s “Leap of the Dog” sculpture, showing how a local oral tradition can become civic art and tourism identity. That does not prove the event happened; it shows that the story continues to carry meaning for the town.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://storymaps.arcgis.com/stories/bc18ab0435b94bdc929d5e76d77f14a0" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: storymaps.arcgis.com">[ArcGIS StoryMaps]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">storymaps.arcgis.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Arc GIS Story Maps Limavady Explore See Do Sculpture Trail!</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Arc GIS Story Maps Limavady Explore See Do Sculpture Trail!</span></span></span>

## The Londonderry Air: song, memory and disputed origins

County Londonderry’s folklore is not only about supernatural beings. It also includes collected song and musical memory, above all the melody known as the “Londonderry Air”, later famous worldwide through “Danny Boy”. Jane Ross, a Limavady-born collector, sent local airs to George Petrie, who published the melody in 1855. The Dictionary of Irish Biography identifies Ross as a folksong collector from Limavady, while local accounts link the air to a fiddler heard near Ross’s home on Main Street.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.dib.ie/biography/ross-jane-a7805" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: dib.ie">[DIB]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">dib.ie</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on dib.ie.</span></span></span>

This is a good example of the difference between folklore, collection and later invention. The tune was collected in County Londonderry, but the words of “Danny Boy” were written later by Fred Weatherly and are not an old County Londonderry lyric. Scholars and music writers have debated the exact origin of the air, the role of Ross, and whether the melody was altered in transmission. A careful view is that Ross’s contribution as collector is secure, while the tune’s earlier history remains uncertain.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://journalofmusic.com/opinion/long-drive-limavady" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journalofmusic.com">[Journal of Music]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journalofmusic.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">long drive limavady</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">long drive limavady</span></span></span>

For readers interested in county folklore, the important point is that the “Londonderry Air” shows how local tradition can travel. A melody associated with a market town and a collector’s notebook became an international song of exile, longing and return. That journey is folklore in the modern sense: not a fixed relic, but a tradition repeatedly remade by performers, emigrants, families and audiences.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.causewaycoastalroute.com/danny-boy-legacy" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: causewaycoastalroute.com">[Causeway Coastal Route]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">causewaycoastalroute.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on causewaycoastalroute.com.</span></span></span>

## Giants, graves and named stones

Giant traditions in County Londonderry are more scattered than the famous Giant’s Causeway legend in neighbouring County Antrim, but the county still has giant-language embedded in its archaeological landscape. Lists of archaeological sites in County Londonderry include names such as “Giant’s Grave” at Knockoneill and Slaghtneill, and “Giant’s Sconce” at Sconce, Belgarrow and Knockmult. These names do not mean that giants were historically believed in at every site in the same way; they show how unusually large stones, tombs, forts and earthworks invited giant explanations in local naming.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_archaeological_sites_in_County_Londonderry" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">List of archaeological sites in County Londonderry</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">List of archaeological sites in County Londonderry</span></span></span>

The Schools’ Collection also preserves the wider Irish habit of explaining large stones through giant action. One Donegal school entry, referring partly to a family connection in County Derry, tells of a “giant’s stone” said to have been thrown from a mountain several miles away. That is a classic giant motif: a massive object becomes legible because a superhuman thrower accounts for its size and odd position.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.duchas.ie/en/cbes/4428275/4390792/4459115?Route=stories" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: duchas.ie">[Dúchas]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">duchas.ie</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on duchas.ie.</span></span></span>

In County Londonderry, these giant names are best read beside the county’s prehistoric monuments: wedge tombs, standing stones, cairns, bullaun stones and burial sites. Folklore often arrives after the monument, not before it. People encountered old stones without modern archaeological explanation, then supplied stories of giants, saints, hidden treasure, burials, battles or enchanted beings.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_archaeological_sites_in_County_Londonderry" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">List of archaeological sites in County Londonderry</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">List of archaeological sites in County Londonderry</span></span></span>

## Witches, hares and harmful magic

Witchcraft traditions in County Londonderry are harder to pin to famous named cases than the Leap of the Dog or St Columba’s Well, but the belief pattern is familiar from Ulster rural folklore: witches were often imagined through butter theft, milk magic, animal transformation, harmful charms and unexplained illness. The Schools’ Collection contains many Irish examples of witch hares, enchanted animals and local “pishoges”, a term often used for harmful folk magic or superstitious practices.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.duchas.ie/en/cbes/4613719/4612450/4644612" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: duchas.ie">[Dúchas]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">duchas.ie</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on duchas.ie.</span></span></span>

A useful comparison from the Schools’ Collection describes a hare in Moneydarragh that no one could catch and that was “supposed to be a witch hare”. While that particular entry is from Donegal rather than County Londonderry, it reflects a north-western rural story type that helps interpret similar local beliefs across the county border. In such tales the hare is not just wildlife; it is a suspicious form, often linked to a woman believed to be stealing milk or evading harm.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://duchas.ie/en/cbes/4541001/4421984/4541189" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: duchas.ie">[Dúchas]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">duchas.ie</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on duchas.ie.</span></span></span>

For County Londonderry, this matters because rural folklore was practical as well as entertaining. Witchcraft stories explained bad luck in dairying, illness in animals, failed crops, sudden weakness, quarrels between neighbours, and the anxiety of households dependent on milk, butter and seasonal work. These stories should not be read as evidence of actual magic, but as evidence of how communities made sense of risk, envy and misfortune.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ucd.ie/irishfolklore/en/collections/schoolscollectionduchas/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ucd.ie">[University College Dublin]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ucd.ie</span><span class="citation-popover-title">University College Dublin Schools&#x27; Collection (Dúchas</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">University College Dublin Schools&#x27; Collection (Dúchas</span></span></span>

## Ghosts, banshees and haunted memory

County Londonderry has ghostly traditions, but many are local and fragmentary rather than nationally famous. In practice, ghost stories often attach to ruins, old houses, roads, graveyards, wells, battle memories and family deaths. This makes them difficult to separate neatly from ordinary local history. A haunted room, a cry heard near a death, a figure by water, or a warning apparition may be preserved in family memory even when it never becomes a county-wide legend.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.duchas.ie/en/cbes/4493792/4421117/4536881" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: duchas.ie">[Dúchas]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">duchas.ie</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on duchas.ie.</span></span></span>

The banshee belongs to this wider Irish and Ulster context. Folklife scholarship describes the banshee as a liminal figure, standing between woman, fairy being and death messenger. In County Londonderry, banshee lore is best treated as part of a shared Irish death-warning tradition rather than a story unique to one parish, although local families and places may have had their own versions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://folklife.si.edu/magazine/irish-banshee" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: folklife.si.edu">[Smithsonian Folklife Center]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">folklife.si.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">irish banshee</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">irish banshee</span></span></span>

Benbradagh and the Roe Valley are sometimes associated in modern walking and local-interest writing with Finvola, “the Gem of the Roe”, and banshee tradition. That material is evocative, but it should be handled cautiously because popular retellings often blend legend, romance, place-name lore and later literary embellishment. The more reliable conclusion is that the Roe Valley has become a strong container for romantic and supernatural memory, especially around O’Cahan associations, Dungiven, Limavady and the mountain landscape.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.belfasttelegraph.co.uk/life/weekend/walk-of-the-week-benbradagh/a/118551228.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: belfasttelegraph.co.uk">[Belfast Telegraph]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">belfasttelegraph.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on belfasttelegraph.co.uk.</span></span></span>

## The walled city as folklore landscape

Derry’s walls are not “folklore” in the narrow fairy-tale sense, but they are central to County Londonderry’s story culture. The Department for Communities describes the Derry Walls as the largest upstanding monument in State Care in Northern Ireland, maintained by the state since 1955, with the Honourable the Irish Society remaining the owners. Friends of the Derry Walls describe them as the largest ancient monument in State Care in Northern Ireland and the longest complete circuit of ramparts among Ireland’s remaining walled towns.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.communities-ni.gov.uk/articles/derry-walls" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: communities-ni.gov.uk">[Department for Communities]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">communities-ni.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on communities-ni.gov.uk.</span></span></span>

The walls generate memory, ritual and identity: the “Maiden City” story of unbreached walls, the Siege of Derry, gates, processions, monuments, and contested viewpoints over the Bogside. These are not neutral tourist details. They are civic legends in the sense that they are repeatedly narrated to explain what the city is, who defended it, who felt excluded by its symbolism, and why the same stones can mean pride, pain or endurance to different communities.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.communities-ni.gov.uk/articles/derry-walls" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: communities-ni.gov.uk">[Department for Communities]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">communities-ni.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on communities-ni.gov.uk.</span></span></span>

For folklore readers, the useful distinction is this: a fairy fort warns people not to disturb an old rural place; the Derry Walls organise public memory around endurance, siege and identity. Both are story-bearing structures. One belongs mainly to field and hearth tradition, the other to civic ritual and historical commemoration.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://thederrywalls.com/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: thederrywalls.com">[The Derry Walls]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">thederrywalls.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on thederrywalls.com.</span></span></span>

## What is old tradition, and what is modern retelling?

County Londonderry’s folklore comes through several different channels, and readers should not treat them all as the same kind of evidence. The Schools’ Collection is especially valuable because it records material gathered from local people in the late 1930s, often preserving beliefs about cures, forts, customs, weather lore, holy wells and supernatural encounters that might otherwise have vanished. It is still a mediated source, shaped by school exercises and transcription, but it is much closer to oral tradition than a modern tourism brochure.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ucd.ie/irishfolklore/en/collections/schoolscollectionduchas/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ucd.ie">[University College Dublin]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ucd.ie</span><span class="citation-popover-title">University College Dublin Schools&#x27; Collection (Dúchas</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">University College Dublin Schools&#x27; Collection (Dúchas</span></span></span>

Antiquarian and heritage sources are useful for wells, stones, churches and maps, especially where they preserve older references or identify vanished features. They can also overstate continuity or speculate about pre-Christian origins, so their claims need care. Modern tourism sources, by contrast, are excellent for showing what is publicly retold now: the Leap of the Dog sculpture, St Columba trails, the Tower Museum, Derry Walls interpretation, and Limavady’s “Danny Boy” identity.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.thenorthernantiquarian.org/2021/08/20/columbas-well-derry/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: thenorthernantiquarian.org">[thenorthernantiquarian.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">thenorthernantiquarian.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">columbas well derry</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">columbas well derry</span></span></span>

A good rule for County Londonderry is to ask four questions of any legend: is it attached to a named place; does it appear in older oral or antiquarian records; has it been reshaped by song, tourism or civic commemoration; and does the landscape still help the story make sense? The strongest traditions usually answer yes to more than one of these. The Leap of the Dog, St Columba’s Well, the Londonderry Air, fairy forts and the Derry Walls all survive because they are not merely stories: they are linked to places people can still visit, name, argue over, sing about, or pass on.
