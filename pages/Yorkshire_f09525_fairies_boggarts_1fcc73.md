## Introduction

[Yorkshire]({{ 'the-legends-behind-yorkshires-wild/' | relative_url }})’s fairies and boggarts belong to a tradition of “hidden folk” who were imagined as living close to ordinary rural life: in farmhouses, fields, caves, cloughs, streams and lonely corners of the landscape. These beings were not simply monsters or magical characters; they helped explain everyday uncertainties, from unexplained noises in the home to unlucky encounters on the moors. In Yorkshire stories, a boggart might disturb a household, while fairy beings could inhabit unusual rocks, hills or remote places where the human and supernatural worlds seemed to meet.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.yorkshiredales.org.uk/folklore-of-the-dales/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: yorkshiredales.org.uk">[Yorkshire Dales National Park]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">yorkshiredales.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">folklore of the dales</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Yorkshire Dales National ParkFolklore of the DalesJul 18, 2021 — An angered hob is known as a boggart and is most commonly heard of in Yo...</span></span></span>

The evidence for these traditions comes mainly from oral storytelling, dialect records, nineteenth-century folklore collecting and later local retellings rather than from a single ancient Yorkshire mythology. The importance of the stories lies in what they reveal about rural communities: how people understood the landscape, warned children, explained misfortune and gave personality to the places around them.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.yorkshiredales.org.uk/folklore-of-the-dales/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: yorkshiredales.org.uk">[Yorkshire Dales National Park]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">yorkshiredales.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">folklore of the dales</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Yorkshire Dales National ParkFolklore of the DalesJul 18, 2021 — An angered hob is known as a boggart and is most commonly heard of in Yo...</span></span></span>

## Boggarts around homes and farms

Boggarts are among Yorkshire’s best-known examples of domestic supernatural folklore. Unlike the elegant fairy figures often found in later literature, the Yorkshire boggart was usually unpredictable: sometimes mischievous, sometimes frightening, and often connected with a particular place. Folklore from the Yorkshire Dales describes the boggart as a transformed or troublesome hob, with traditions placing such beings in valleys, potholes and other landscape features as well as near human settlements.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.yorkshiredales.org.uk/folklore-of-the-dales/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: yorkshiredales.org.uk">[Yorkshire Dales National Park]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">yorkshiredales.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">folklore of the dales</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Yorkshire Dales National ParkFolklore of the DalesJul 18, 2021 — An angered hob is known as a boggart and is most commonly heard of in Yo...</span></span></span>

A famous Yorkshire tale tells of a boggart that moved into the home of farmer George Gilbertson. The creature was said to cause disturbances within the household, particularly troubling the children, while remaining invisible to most people. The story reflects a common pattern in northern English folklore: a supernatural being becomes almost another member of the household, occupying a familiar place but disrupting the expected order of family life.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://yorkshirefolktales.home.blog/2019/02/25/the-boggart-and-the-knot-hole/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: yorkshirefolktales.home.blog">[Yorkshire Folk Tales]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">yorkshirefolktales.home.blog</span><span class="citation-popover-title">the boggart and the knot hole</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Yorkshire Folk TalesThe Boggart and the knot-hole25 Feb 2019 — One day, a Boggart took up residence (why and how, I never discovered) in...</span></span></span>

These tales often carried practical meanings. A noisy house, frightened animals, damaged belongings or unexplained events could be given a story-based explanation through the idea of a resident spirit. The boggart also represented a warning about the relationship between people and their surroundings: a home was not always completely controlled by its owners, and the landscape around a farm could be imagined as having its own inhabitants and rules.

The distinction between helpful and harmful spirits was not always fixed. Northern traditions included hobs, household spirits that might assist farmers if treated properly, but a neglected or angered spirit could become a boggart. This uncertainty made the creatures useful storytelling figures because they reflected everyday concerns about respect, behaviour and good fortune.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.yorkshiredales.org.uk/folklore-of-the-dales/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: yorkshiredales.org.uk">[Yorkshire Dales National Park]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">yorkshiredales.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">folklore of the dales</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Yorkshire Dales National ParkFolklore of the DalesJul 18, 2021 — An angered hob is known as a boggart and is most commonly heard of in Yo...</span></span></span>

## Fairy traditions of the Yorkshire Dales

Fairy traditions in Yorkshire often connect supernatural beings with distinctive landscapes rather than distant fantasy worlds. Hills, caves, unusual rock formations and remote valleys could become places where people imagined hidden communities existing alongside their own. The Yorkshire Dales, with its limestone scenery, caves and isolated settlements, provided many settings for stories about mysterious inhabitants beneath or beyond the visible landscape.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.yorkshiredales.org.uk/folklore-of-the-dales/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: yorkshiredales.org.uk">[Yorkshire Dales National Park]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">yorkshiredales.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">folklore of the dales</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Yorkshire Dales National ParkFolklore of the DalesJul 18, 2021 — An angered hob is known as a boggart and is most commonly heard of in Yo...</span></span></span>

One example linked to Yorkshire’s landscape tradition is Brimham Rocks near Ripon, a dramatic collection of natural rock formations that has attracted stories and imaginative interpretations over generations. Such places often became associated with fairies and other hidden beings because their unusual shapes seemed to invite explanations beyond ordinary geology.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://britishfairies.wordpress.com/tag/boggart/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: britishfairies.wordpress.com">[British Fairies]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">britishfairies.wordpress.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">British Fairiesboggart &#124; British Fairies</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">remarkable geological formation know as Brimham Rocks, near Ripon in the Yorkshire Dales, is the site of a curious faery incident: “Edwin...</span></span></span>

Fairy stories in Yorkshire were not always cheerful tales of tiny winged creatures. Older English traditions used “fairy” as a broad term covering many kinds of supernatural beings, including dangerous or morally ambiguous figures. A fairy encounter could involve gifts, warnings, tricks or the risk of being taken away from the human world. The boundary between fairies, hobs, goblins and boggarts was often flexible in local speech rather than organised into strict categories.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boggart" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">BoggartA boggart is a supernatural being from English folklore. The landscape connection is one of the most important features of Yorkshire’s hidden-folk traditions. A story about a fairy place preserved a memory of that location: a strange hill, a cave entrance, an old track or a patch of countryside that felt different from ordinary farmland. Folklore turned geography into a narrative map, where certain places carried warnings, histories and mysteries.

## Why rural communities told these stories

Fairies and boggarts helped rural communities give meaning to events that were difficult to explain. Before modern understandings of many natural processes, stories about hidden beings offered a shared language for discussing fear, luck, danger and unusual experiences.

The stories also reinforced social values. A boggart tale might discourage careless behaviour, especially among children, by placing consequences into a memorable supernatural form. A fairy warning might teach respect for certain places or encourage people not to disturb mysterious parts of the countryside. These were not simply entertainment; they were part of a wider tradition of passing knowledge between generations.

For historians and folklorists, the value of these tales is not that they provide evidence for supernatural creatures, but that they preserve the thoughts and experiences of the communities that told them. The surviving records show how Yorkshire people attached stories to farms, valleys and landmarks, creating a sense that the countryside contained layers of memory as well as physical features.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.yorkshiredales.org.uk/folklore-of-the-dales/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: yorkshiredales.org.uk">[Yorkshire Dales National Park]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">yorkshiredales.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">folklore of the dales</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Yorkshire Dales National ParkFolklore of the DalesJul 18, 2021 — An angered hob is known as a boggart and is most commonly heard of in Yo...</span></span></span>

## From oral tales to modern retellings

Many Yorkshire fairy and boggart traditions survived because collectors, local historians and storytellers recorded them during periods when rural oral culture was changing. Later publications helped preserve stories that might otherwise have disappeared, although each retelling could reshape details to suit new audiences.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.jstor.org/stable/jj.28460426" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: jstor.org">[JSTOR]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">jstor.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Boggart: Folklore, History, Place-names and DialectThe story takes place some two hundred years ago on the wild edges of a tiny...</span></span></span>

Modern interpretations often present boggarts and fairies as charming fantasy creatures, but the older Yorkshire traditions are more complex. A boggart was not simply a friendly household helper, and a fairy was not always a harmless figure. They belonged to a world where the countryside was both familiar and uncertain, full of places that demanded caution and respect.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boggart" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">BoggartA boggart is a supernatural being from English folklore. Today these stories remain part of Yorkshire’s cultural identity through local history projects, walking routes, museums, tourism writing and new folklore collections. Their survival shows how rural legends can continue to connect people with landscapes, even after the original beliefs that shaped them have changed.
