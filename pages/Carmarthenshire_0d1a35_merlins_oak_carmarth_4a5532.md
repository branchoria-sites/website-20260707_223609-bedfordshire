## Introduction

Carmarthen’s Merlin’s Oak legend is one of [Carmarthenshire]({{ 'why-does-carmarthenshire-feel-so/' | relative_url }})’s clearest examples of prophecy becoming local identity. The tradition says that an old oak in the town was bound to Carmarthen’s fate: when the tree fell, the town would fall too, or in another version, would be drowned by flood. The tree itself once stood at the corner of Oak Lane and Priory Street, and fragments are still associated with local museum and civic display. The story became especially memorable after the remaining stump was removed in 1978 and Carmarthen suffered serious flooding soon afterwards. That does not prove the prophecy; it shows how folklore works. A landmark, a rhyme, a public memory and a dramatic coincidence were folded into one story that still helps Carmarthen explain itself as Merlin’s town.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.landoflegends.wales/location/carmarthenshire-county-museum" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: landoflegends.wales">[landoflegends.wales]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">landoflegends.wales</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Land of Legends Carmarthenshire County Museum</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Land of LegendsCarmarthenshire County Museum - King ArthurA well-known Carmarthen belief refers to an old oak which used to grow in the t...</span></span></span>

## The oak, the saying and the flood legend

The core saying is usually given in English as: “When Merlin’s Oak shall tumble down, then shall fall Carmarthen town.” Local retellings also preserve a water-focused version in which Carmarthen would flood or drown if the oak fell. That small change matters. “Fall” could mean political ruin, civic decline, disaster or physical collapse; “drown” ties the prophecy directly to Carmarthen’s river landscape and to the remembered flood after the stump’s removal.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.landoflegends.wales/location/carmarthenshire-county-museum" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: landoflegends.wales">[Land of Legends]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">landoflegends.wales</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Land of Legends Carmarthenshire County Museum</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Land of LegendsCarmarthenshire County Museum - King ArthurA well-known Carmarthen belief refers to an old oak which used to grow in the t...</span></span></span>

The tree was not hidden in a remote sacred grove. It stood in the working town, at the corner of Oak Lane and Priory Street, where streets, traffic and everyday movement made the legend visible. Accounts describe it as the Old Oak, Priory Oak or Merlin’s Oak. One tradition says it may have been planted in 1659 or 1660 by a local schoolmaster to mark the Restoration of Charles II, while another links it more loosely to the medieval Carmarthen Priory area. This is a useful warning against treating the story as a single ancient fact: the tree’s civic, royalist, priory and Merlin associations seem to have gathered around the same object over time.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.atlasobscura.com/places/merlins-oak-carmarthen" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: atlasobscura.com">[Atlas Obscura]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">atlasobscura.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Atlas Obscura Merlin&#x27;s Oak in Carmarthen</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Atlas Obscura Merlin&#x27;s Oak in Carmarthen</span></span></span>

By the nineteenth century, the oak had reportedly become troublesome as well as treasured. Later accounts say it was poisoned by a local man who disliked people gathering beneath it, and that its dead trunk was then protected with railings and concrete. In folklore terms, that is a striking detail. The oak was already dead, or nearly dead, yet it remained socially alive. Carmarthen did not merely preserve timber; it preserved a warning.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.atlasobscura.com/places/merlins-oak-carmarthen" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: atlasobscura.com">[Atlas Obscura]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">atlasobscura.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Atlas Obscura Merlin&#x27;s Oak in Carmarthen</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Atlas Obscura Merlin&#x27;s Oak in Carmarthen</span></span></span>

The decisive modern moment came in 1978, when the remaining stump was removed. Local heritage accounts connect that removal with Carmarthen’s worst floods in living memory, while the Tree Register’s regional notes also record the legend warning of floods if the oak fell and say fragments remained on display at the county museum. The important point is not that the flood was supernaturally caused, but that the timing gave the prophecy a powerful afterlife. A practical traffic or street-management decision became a legendary turning point.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.landoflegends.wales/location/carmarthenshire-county-museum" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: landoflegends.wales">[Land of Legends]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">landoflegends.wales</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Land of Legends Carmarthenshire County Museum</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Land of LegendsCarmarthenshire County Museum - King ArthurA well-known Carmarthen belief refers to an old oak which used to grow in the t...</span></span></span>

Today the oak survives as fragments, replacement planting and story. Atlas Obscura notes that one piece can be seen at Carmarthenshire County Museum and another final fragment is associated with St Peter’s Civic Hall in Nott Square, while a replacement tree grows at the original site. Historic UK likewise records that fragments are preserved in the local civic hall. In other words, the “fallen” tree has not disappeared. It has been redistributed through the town’s heritage geography.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.atlasobscura.com/places/merlins-oak-carmarthen" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: atlasobscura.com">[Atlas Obscura]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">atlasobscura.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Atlas Obscura Merlin&#x27;s Oak in Carmarthen</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Atlas Obscura Merlin&#x27;s Oak in Carmarthen</span></span></span>

## Carmarthen, Merlin and the Black Book tradition

Carmarthen’s oak prophecy carries more weight because the town already has one of the strongest Merlin associations in Wales. The Welsh place-name is often interpreted through Myrddin, the Welsh Merlin figure, although historians and writers also discuss more complicated possibilities involving the Roman name Moridunum and later legendary etymology. The point for folklore is that the name made the town unusually ready to receive Merlin stories: once a place can be heard as “Merlin’s fort”, local landmarks become easier to read as Merlin’s landscape.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.theguardian.com/travel/2024/nov/13/where-tourists-seldom-tread-british-towns-with-tales-to-tellwakefield-carmarthen-colne" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theguardian.com">[The Guardian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theguardian.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on theguardian.com.</span></span></span>

The medieval anchor is the Black Book of Carmarthen, catalogued by the National Library of Wales as Peniarth MS 1. The Library describes it as the earliest surviving complete manuscript written in Welsh, associated with the Priory of St John the Evangelist and Teulyddog at Carmarthen, and now understood as the work of a single scribe writing before and around 1250. Its connection with Carmarthen Priory is part of the town’s cultural claim, even though the surviving manuscript now belongs to the National Library’s collections in Aberystwyth.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.library.wales/discover-learn/digital-exhibitions/manuscripts/the-middle-ages/the-black-book-of-carmarthen" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: library.wales">[National Library of Wales]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">library.wales</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Library of Wales The Black Book of Carmarthen</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Library of Wales The Black Book of Carmarthen</span></span></span>

The Black Book does not give us the modern Merlin’s Oak legend. Its importance is deeper and older: it preserves medieval poetic material in which the prophetic Merlin tradition is already taking shape. The National Library identifies three poems definitely relating to the Myrddin legend and explains that in two of them a solitary figure in a forest addresses an apple tree and a pig while prophesying Welsh success or failure in conflicts with the Normans in south Wales. The Library also notes that some prophetic lines were composed after the events they appear to foretell, a reminder that medieval prophecy often worked by presenting history as prediction.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.library.wales/discover-learn/digital-exhibitions/manuscripts/the-middle-ages/the-black-book-of-carmarthen" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: library.wales">[National Library of Wales]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">library.wales</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Library of Wales The Black Book of Carmarthen</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Library of Wales The Black Book of Carmarthen</span></span></span>

That matters for Merlin’s Oak because it places Carmarthen’s later tree legend in a wider prophetic habit. Merlin is not only a wand-waving court wizard of modern popular culture. In early Welsh material he is a poet, wild man and prophet, tied to landscape, trees, animals, war memory and national fate. Recent reporting on the Myrddin Poetry Project, involving scholars from Welsh universities, stresses that early Merlin poems challenge the modern “wizard” image and present him as a prophet deeply concerned with the natural world.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.theguardian.com/uk-news/2025/feb/25/early-poems-about-merlin-portray-him-as-environmentalist-say-scholars" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theguardian.com">[The Guardian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theguardian.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on theguardian.com.</span></span></span>

Carmarthen’s oak therefore looks like a local, civic version of an older pattern. Medieval poetry has Merlin speaking through trees and prophecy; the town legend has Merlin’s warning attached to a specific urban oak. The scale changes from battles and kingdoms to streets, floods and a county town, but the imaginative mechanism is similar: the future of a community is made readable through a living landmark.

## How town landmarks become living folklore

Merlin’s Oak is powerful because it made prophecy practical. A tree in the way of traffic is an everyday nuisance; a tree believed to carry the fate of the town is a public responsibility. That tension explains why the story stayed vivid. It was not simply told beside a fireplace; it could be pointed to in the street, protected, argued over, damaged, removed, displayed and replanted.

Three layers made the legend durable:

**A visible object.** The oak gave Carmarthen a prophecy people could see. Unlike a vague tale of a hidden cave or distant battlefield, this legend had a street-corner location. Even after the tree died, its trunk and fragments let the story remain physically present.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.atlasobscura.com/places/merlins-oak-carmarthen" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: atlasobscura.com">[Atlas Obscura]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">atlasobscura.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Atlas Obscura Merlin&#x27;s Oak in Carmarthen</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Atlas Obscura Merlin&#x27;s Oak in Carmarthen</span></span></span>

**A memorable rhyme.** The saying is short, balanced and easy to repeat. “Tree falls, town falls” is exactly the kind of structure that survives in oral tradition because it is simple enough for children, visitors, councillors and journalists to remember.

**A confirming event.** The 1978 flood gave the legend its modern dramatic hook. The town did not fall in any literal final sense, but the flood was close enough to the wording of the drowned-town version that it renewed belief, curiosity and tourist retelling.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.landoflegends.wales/location/carmarthenshire-county-museum" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: landoflegends.wales">[Land of Legends]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">landoflegends.wales</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Land of Legends Carmarthenshire County Museum</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Land of LegendsCarmarthenshire County Museum - King ArthurA well-known Carmarthen belief refers to an old oak which used to grow in the t...</span></span></span>

This is why Merlin’s Oak should be read as folklore rather than as failed or successful prediction. Folklore is not only about whether a claim is factually true. It is about how communities attach meaning to events. A flood after the removal of an old tree becomes more than bad weather and river behaviour; it becomes a story about memory, caution, civic identity and the danger of ignoring inherited warnings.

The legend also shows how Carmarthenshire’s folklore often works through place rather than through abstract myth. Carmarthen’s Merlin tradition is not just “Arthurian legend in Wales”. It is tied to a town name, a priory manuscript, a street-corner oak, a civic hall, a museum fragment and the River Tywi’s floodplain setting. The Guardian’s recent travel writing on Carmarthen makes the same landscape point in non-folkloric terms, noting the town’s Roman depth, its floodplain and the Black Book’s local significance.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.theguardian.com/travel/2024/nov/13/where-tourists-seldom-tread-british-towns-with-tales-to-tellwakefield-carmarthen-colne" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theguardian.com">[The Guardian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theguardian.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on theguardian.com.</span></span></span>

## What is old, what is later, and what is still alive?

The safest way to understand Merlin’s Oak is to separate the layers without stripping away the charm.

The medieval layer belongs to the Black Book of Carmarthen and the older Welsh Merlin tradition. This gives Carmarthen a serious literary and manuscript connection to prophetic Merlin material, but it does not by itself prove that the oak rhyme is medieval. The National Library’s account places the manuscript around 1250 and discusses prophetic Myrddin poems, while a later scholarly article describes the Black Book as a mid-thirteenth-century collection and notes evidence supporting a South Wales origin.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.library.wales/discover-learn/digital-exhibitions/manuscripts/the-middle-ages/the-black-book-of-carmarthen" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: library.wales">[National Library of Wales]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">library.wales</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Library of Wales The Black Book of Carmarthen</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Library of Wales The Black Book of Carmarthen</span></span></span>

The early modern or civic layer may belong to the tree’s reported planting in 1659 or 1660 to mark the return of Charles II. That tradition, if accurate, would make the oak a Restoration-era civic tree later absorbed into Merlin lore. It is also possible that the “Priory Oak” name reflects the older religious geography of Carmarthen rather than an unbroken medieval sacred-tree tradition.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.atlasobscura.com/places/merlins-oak-carmarthen" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: atlasobscura.com">[Atlas Obscura]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">atlasobscura.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Atlas Obscura Merlin&#x27;s Oak in Carmarthen</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Atlas Obscura Merlin&#x27;s Oak in Carmarthen</span></span></span>

The nineteenth- and twentieth-century layer is the survival of the dead or damaged tree as a protected landmark. Reports of poisoning, railings, concrete, fire damage and eventual removal show a legend interacting with modern urban life. This is the period in which the oak becomes not just a tree but a municipal problem: how do you manage traffic around a prophecy?<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.atlasobscura.com/places/merlins-oak-carmarthen" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: atlasobscura.com">[Atlas Obscura]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">atlasobscura.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Atlas Obscura Merlin&#x27;s Oak in Carmarthen</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Atlas Obscura Merlin&#x27;s Oak in Carmarthen</span></span></span>

The living layer is today’s heritage retelling. Visitors can encounter the story through Carmarthenshire County Museum, St Peter’s Civic Hall, the replacement tree and modern articles about Carmarthen’s Merlin connections. ## Why Merlin’s Oak still matters to Carmarthenshire folklore

Merlin’s Oak matters because it brings several kinds of Carmarthenshire tradition together in one object. It is a tree legend, a town prophecy, a Merlin story, a flood memory and a heritage landmark. It also sits at the meeting point between written culture and oral culture: the Black Book gives Carmarthen a medieval prophetic background, while the oak rhyme gives the town a popular legend that ordinary residents and visitors can repeat.

The story is also unusually honest about how folklore changes. The Merlin of the oak is not exactly the Merlin of medieval Welsh poetry, and neither is exactly the Merlin of later Arthurian romance. Carmarthen’s tradition has blended them. Geoffrey of Monmouth helped connect Merlin with Carmarthen in medieval literary culture; the Black Book preserved Welsh prophetic material; later local tradition fastened Merlin’s warning to a town tree; modern heritage keeps the fragments and the story in public view.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.library.wales/discover-learn/digital-exhibitions/manuscripts/the-middle-ages/the-black-book-of-carmarthen" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: library.wales">[library.wales]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">library.wales</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Library of Wales The Black Book of Carmarthen</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Library of Wales The Black Book of Carmarthen</span></span></span>

For readers exploring Carmarthenshire folklore, the oak is a useful doorway into the county’s wider pattern. Like the lake legend of Llyn y Fan Fach or the healing traditions of Myddfai, it is not merely a strange tale pasted onto a place. It explains how people in this part of Wales have used landscape, memory and story to think about risk, identity and belonging. In Carmarthen, even a traffic obstruction could become a prophecy — and even after the tree fell, the legend kept growing.
