## Introduction

Kent’s folk customs are at their most distinctive when they come out of work, food and parish life rather than from grand myth. Hoodening, hop-picking memory and the Biddenden Easter dole all belong to that world. They are customs of farm labourers, winter visiting, harvest migration, village charity, hard biscuits, oast houses and local performance. Hoodening centres on the hooden horse: a wooden horse’s head on a pole, usually carried by a hidden performer, once taken round East Kent by farm workers at Christmas and later revived as a folk-play tradition. Hop customs attach Kent’s “Garden of England” image to real seasonal labour in hop gardens. Biddenden’s Easter dole turns a village charity into one of the county’s most memorable food legends. Together they show why Kentish folklore often feels local, practical and socially rooted rather than remote or purely supernatural.

## Why Kent’s customs grew from working landscapes

Kent’s old rural identity is not just a slogan. Hop gardens, orchards, mixed farms and village charities made seasonal rhythm visible in everyday life: winter slack time, spring Easter giving, late-summer hop harvest, autumn plenty, and the public house or parish church as gathering place. The Kentish Stour Countryside Partnership notes that hop gardens began appearing in Kent in the seventeenth century and became strongly associated with the “Garden of England”; British Hops, the trade body for British hop growers, gives the older tradition that the first English hop garden was planted near Canterbury in 1520, reflecting Kent’s early importance in hop culture.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://kentishstour.org.uk/about-the-stour-valley/action-to-help-the-stour-valley/countryside-products/old-orchards-nut-plats-and-hop-gardens/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: kentishstour.org.uk">[Kentish Stour]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">kentishstour.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Kentish StourOld Orchards, Nut Plats and Hop GardensHop Gardens started to appear in Kent in the 17th century and have been strongly asso...</span></span></span>

That agricultural background matters because these customs are not simply “quaint survivals”. They are ways communities managed the hard edges of rural life: uncertain work, seasonal poverty, dependence on landowners, and the need for neighbourly recognition. Hoodening asked households and publicans for drink, food or money in exchange for performance. Hop-picking brought outside families into Kent’s fields for weeks at a time. The Biddenden dole redistributed bread, cheese and later tea through a charity tied to named pieces of land. Each custom made a social relationship public.

The result is a kind of folklore that feels different from a ghost story or saint’s legend. It is theatrical, edible, noisy, sometimes unruly, and often attached to a precise local mechanism: a horse’s clacking jaw, a hop-picker’s hut, a biscuit mould, a window at the old workhouse. Kent’s customs survive in memory partly because they can be handled, tasted, performed or watched.

## Hooden horses in winter and harvest

Hoodening is the best-known Kentish animal-disguise custom. In its classic East Kent form, it used a “hooden horse”: a carved wooden horse’s head, fixed to a pole, with a movable jaw that could snap or clatter. The performer was hidden under cloth, making the horse both comic and unsettling. Modern summaries sometimes describe it as a British “hooded animal” custom, but its strongest historic association is with East Kent, especially Thanet, Deal, Walmer, Sarre, St Nicholas-at-Wade and the Canterbury area.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hoodening" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wikipedia.org.</span></span></span>

The older pattern was a Christmas visiting custom. Groups of farm labourers travelled with the horse, music and comic characters, often including a leader and a cross-dressed “Mollie” figure. They entered houses, inns and farm settings, performed, joked, sang, caused controlled disorder and received refreshment or money. This was not a polished stage tradition in origin. It belonged to a winter economy in which agricultural work was reduced and farm workers used performance to renew bonds with employers, neighbours and publicans.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hoodening" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wikipedia.org.</span></span></span>

Percy Maylam is central to how hoodening is known today. A Canterbury solicitor and local historian, he recorded surviving examples and published *The Hooden Horse: An East Kent Christmas Custom* in 1909. Later writers have treated Maylam’s work as unusually careful because he tried to separate evidence from speculation at a time when many folk customs were being explained too confidently as pagan survivals. Kent Archaeological Society material notes both the importance and rarity of Maylam’s original book, while modern hoodening histories continue to identify it as the key early study.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.kentarchaeology.org.uk/magazine/119/14-the-hooden-horse-a-kent-christmas-custom" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: kentarchaeology.org.uk">[Kent History &amp; Archaeology]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">kentarchaeology.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Kent History &amp; Archaeology The Hooden Horse: A Kent Christmas Custom</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Kent History &amp; Archaeology The Hooden Horse: A Kent Christmas Custom</span></span></span>

The strongest reading of hoodening is therefore not “this proves an ancient horse cult”, but “this is a well-attested East Kent seasonal performance with older roots, later decline and modern revival”. The custom has attracted theories about pre-Christian ritual, fertility, animal spirits and the symbolic power of the horse, but the evidence is clearest from the eighteenth century onwards and especially from nineteenth- and early twentieth-century accounts. That makes it no less folkloric. Its power lies in the way a farm-worker entertainment became a county emblem.

Modern hoodening is also a revival tradition, not a frozen survival. The St Nicholas-at-Wade Hoodeners describe the present form as a small group touring pubs and private gatherings before Christmas, performing a humorous rhymed play with death-and-resurrection themes, topical jokes and charity collecting. The “Celebrating the Kentish Hooden Horse” project similarly frames hoodening as a living East Kent tradition connected to folk theatre, working-class custom, revival culture and contemporary folk activism.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://hoodening.org.uk/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: hoodening.org.uk">[Hoodening]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">hoodening.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on hoodening.org.uk.</span></span></span>

That revival matters for how the custom should be understood. Today’s hoodening is not simply an antiquarian reconstruction, but neither is it an invented tourist gimmick. It is a modern performance built from recorded local practice, family memory, folk scholarship, pub culture and the pleasure of seeing a strange wooden horse clack its way through a Kentish winter.

## Hop gardens, farms and the Garden of England

Hop-picking is not always treated as “folklore” in the narrow sense, but in Kent it carries many of the features that make customs endure: a seasonal journey, repeated family participation, specialised vocabulary, songs and stories, temporary homes, shared hardship, and a strong sense of place. Before mechanisation, picking hops was intensely labour-hungry. London Museum describes how, until the 1960s, thousands of east and south-east Londoners travelled into Kent at the end of summer to help with the hop harvest, often returning to the same farms year after year.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.londonmuseum.org.uk/collections/london-stories/hop-picking-londoners-working-holiday/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: londonmuseum.org.uk">[London Museum]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">londonmuseum.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">hop picking londoners working holiday</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">hop picking londoners working holiday</span></span></span>

This was paid work, not a rural holiday in the simple nostalgic sense. Families stayed in basic hoppers’ huts, cooked outdoors, lived close together and worked long days. Yet many remembered it as the only affordable break from the city, a time of outdoor life, kinship and annual reunion. The National Trust’s Scotney Castle account preserves precisely this double character: hop-picking as hard work and as a cherished family memory, with Little Scotney Farm described as the Trust’s only working hop farm and one of the few remaining hop farms in Kent.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nationaltrust.org.uk/visit/kent/scotney-castle/memories-of-the-kent-hop-fields" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nationaltrust.org.uk">[National Trust]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nationaltrust.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Trust Memories of hop picking&#124; Kent</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Trust Memories of hop picking&#124; Kent</span></span></span>

Kent’s hop landscape also left visible architecture. Oast houses, with their roundels and cowls, became one of the county’s most recognisable rural forms because hops had to be dried after picking. Kent Archives notes the relationship between hop growers and brewers, including small farms and larger commercial growers supplying breweries such as Shepherd Neame; this places hop customs inside a wider rural economy rather than a decorative postcard scene.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.kentarchives.org.uk/hops-in-kent/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: kentarchives.org.uk">[kentarchives.org.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">kentarchives.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">hops in kent</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">hops in kent</span></span></span>

The public-facing revival of hop memory now often happens through heritage events. Kent Life’s “Hops n Harvest” event, for example, recreates hand-picking, bushel filling, oast-house work and hoppers’ huts for visitors, presenting the hop harvest as recent social history rather than distant legend.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://kentlife.org.uk/2024/08/09/hops-n-harvest/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: kentlife.org.uk">[kentlife.org.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">kentlife.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">hops n harvest</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">hops n harvest</span></span></span>

This is where hop customs connect naturally with hoodening. Both belong to Kent’s farm calendar, but they sit at different points in the year and social order. Hop-picking gathered temporary labour for a crop that tied Kent to London families, brewers and oast houses. Hoodening came in the quieter winter season, when local farm workers transformed themselves into comic performers and carried a strange horse from door to door. One is harvest labour remembered as community; the other is winter performance remembered as custom.

Hop hoodening makes that link explicit. Wantsum Morris traces the modern Hop Hoodening event in East Kent to 1957, after the renaming of a Wickhambreaux public house as The Hooden Horse in 1956. It is therefore a modern folk festival rather than a direct medieval harvest rite, but it deliberately joins the hooden horse image to the hop harvest and to East Kent’s rural identity.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://wantsum-morris.org.uk/hop-hoodening/hop-hoodening-history/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wantsum-morris.org.uk">[Wantsum Morris Men]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wantsum-morris.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wantsum-morris.org.uk.</span></span></span>

## Biddenden Maids and Easter dole traditions

Biddenden gives Kent one of its most unusual blends of charity, food custom and legend. The core practice is the Easter dole: bread, cheese and other gifts distributed to local recipients through a village charity. The folklore attached to it says that Mary and Eliza Chulkhurst, known as the Biddenden Maids, were conjoined sisters born in 1100 who left land to support the poor of the parish. The souvenir object is the Biddenden cake or biscuit, a very hard flour-and-water cake stamped with two joined female figures.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cultureoncall.com/the-biddenden-plaque-and-biscuit/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cultureoncall.com">[Culture on Call]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cultureoncall.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Culture on Call The Biddenden Maids Plaque and Biscuit</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Culture on Call The Biddenden Maids Plaque and Biscuit</span></span></span>

The historically secure part is the charity and the land. Edward Hasted’s eighteenth-century county history records “Twenty Acres of Land, called the Bread and Cheese Land”, lying in five pieces, with yearly rents distributed among the poor of Biddenden parish. The modern parish council states that the dole is now managed by the trustees of the Consolidated Charities and is given out on Easter Monday morning from the old workhouse on Sissinghurst Road.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.british-history.ac.uk/survey-kent/vol7/pp130-141" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: british-history.ac.uk">[British History Online]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">british-history.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on british-history.ac.uk.</span></span></span>

The legendary part is more complicated. Hasted doubted the famous conjoined-twins explanation and suggested that the figures on the cakes may originally have represented two poor widows, the kind of people who received the charity, rather than medieval sisters who founded it. HistoryExtra’s 2025 discussion of Biddenden cakes also highlights this scepticism, noting that the charity is older and better attested than the fully developed story of Mary and Eliza.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.historyextra.com/period/medieval/easter-treats-what-are-biddenden-cakes/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: historyextra.com">[History Extra]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">historyextra.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">easter treats what are biddenden cakes</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">easter treats what are biddenden cakes</span></span></span>

That uncertainty is exactly why Biddenden matters as folklore. The custom does not depend on proving that the sisters lived in the form later tradition describes. Instead, it shows how a charitable land endowment, an annual food distribution and a striking biscuit image generated a memorable origin story. Once visitors could hold a cake stamped with two joined figures, the legend had a physical object to travel with.

The Pitt Rivers Museum’s “Imaging of the Biddenden Maids” page treats the biscuit as a memorial food item and records examples in the museum’s collections, including nineteenth- and early twentieth-century specimens. Folkestone Museum likewise describes a Biddenden cake and notes how the legend became especially popular in the Victorian period, when large numbers of visitors came to the village and kept the cakes as souvenirs.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://web.prm.ox.ac.uk/england/englishness-imaging-biddenden-maids.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: web.prm.ox.ac.uk">[Oxford PRM]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">web.prm.ox.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on ox.ac.uk.</span></span></span>

The dole also reveals the unruly side of charitable custom. Accounts of the 1605 Easter distribution mention bread, cheese, cakes and barrels of beer, and explain that the giving was suspended during an archdeacon’s visit because previous occasions had produced disorder. That detail is important: village charity was not always quiet benevolence. It could be festive, crowded, contested and difficult to control.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.historyextra.com/period/medieval/easter-treats-what-are-biddenden-cakes/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: historyextra.com">[History Extra]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">historyextra.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">easter treats what are biddenden cakes</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">easter treats what are biddenden cakes</span></span></span>

Biddenden therefore belongs beside hoodening, even though one is an Easter charity and the other a winter visiting performance. Both customs turn social obligation into public ritual. Both involve food or drink. Both have a comic or disturbing visual emblem: the snapping hooden horse in one case, the hard biscuit with joined women in the other. Both also show how Kentish tradition is preserved not only by storytellers but by trustees, local historians, museums, parish notices, performers and visitors.

## What these customs tell us about Kent

Kent’s hoodening, hop customs and Biddenden dole are not isolated curiosities. They form a small map of how county identity works at ground level. East Kent’s hooden horse belongs to farms, pubs, winter roads and revival performance. Hop-picking belongs to gardens, oast houses, London migration and remembered summer labour. Biddenden belongs to parish land, Easter food, local poor relief and a legend made durable by a biscuit.

They also show why “old” is not a single category. Hoodening is old in record, but its modern performance has been consciously revived. Hop-picking memory is recent enough to sit within family history, yet it now feels like a vanished rural world because machines and changing labour patterns ended the mass seasonal migration. Biddenden’s charity is firmly historical, while its most famous origin story remains disputed. The folklore is not weakened by these distinctions; it becomes more interesting because the reader can see how tradition is made, challenged and remade.

For Kent, these customs keep the “Garden of England” from becoming a bland phrase. They put people back into the landscape: farm hands with a wooden horse, London families in hop gardens, pensioners receiving bread and cheese, visitors buying a biscuit too hard to eat. The supernatural is almost absent, but the sense of strangeness remains. A horse with a clacking jaw appears in a pub. A harvest becomes an annual migration. A charity cake turns two mysterious women into village memory. That is Kentish folklore at its most local: practical, social, seasonal and impossible to separate from the land that produced it.
