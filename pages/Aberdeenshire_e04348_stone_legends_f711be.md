## Introduction

[Aberdeenshire]({{ 'aberdeenshire/' | relative_url }})’s ancient stones are more than prehistoric remains: they are places where archaeology and local storytelling have met for centuries. Across the county, recumbent stone circles, standing stones and carved monuments acquired later explanations involving giants, witches, devils, enchanted transformations and memories of extraordinary events. These legends do not usually preserve the original meaning of the monuments, but they reveal how later communities understood a landscape filled with mysterious and ancient features.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://journals.socantscot.org/index.php/psas/article/view/7678" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journals.socantscot.org">[Society of Antiquaries of Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journals.socantscot.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Society of Antiquaries of ScotlandFolklore of the Aberdeenshire Stone Circles and Standing-...by J Ritchie · 1926 · Cited by 6 — The leg...</span></span></span>

The strongest examples show two histories existing side by side. Archaeologists can identify a Bronze Age stone circle or an early medieval carved stone, while folklore records explain the same object through human stories: a person turned into stone, a supernatural contest, or a monument created by beings of unusual strength. The value of these traditions lies not in proving the legends literally happened, but in showing how Aberdeenshire people continued to give meaning to the oldest features of their surroundings.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.aberdeenshire.gov.uk/leisure-sport-and-culture/archaeology/sites-to-visit/maiden-stone/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: aberdeenshire.gov.uk">[Aberdeenshire Council]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">aberdeenshire.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Aberdeenshire Council Maiden Stone</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Aberdeenshire CouncilMaiden Stone - Aberdeenshire Council...</span></span></span>

## Recumbent stone circles and local memory

Aberdeenshire is especially important for its recumbent stone circles, a distinctive monument type concentrated in north-east Scotland. Unlike a simple ring of upright stones, these circles include a large stone laid horizontally between two upright “flanker” stones. Up to around 99 examples have been recorded in the wider tradition, with Aberdeenshire containing a major concentration. They date mainly from the later Neolithic and Bronze Age periods, although the precise meanings of individual sites remain debated.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.aberdeenshire.gov.uk/leisure-sport-and-culture/archaeology/special-archaeology-in-the-area/recumbent-stone-circles/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: aberdeenshire.gov.uk">[Aberdeenshire Council]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">aberdeenshire.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Aberdeenshire CouncilRecumbent Stone CirclesThe Recumbent Stone Circle (RSC) is Aberdeenshire&#x27;s unique contribution to the tradition of e...</span></span></span>

Sites such as East Aquhorthies Stone Circle and Tomnaverie Stone Circle demonstrate why these monuments inspired later stories. Their unusual layouts, isolated settings and immense stones encouraged explanations that connected them with powerful beings or lost peoples. Tomnaverie, for example, is recognised as a recumbent stone circle whose defining feature is the great horizontal stone within the ring.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.historicenvironment.scot/visit-a-place/places/tomnaverie-stone-circle/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: historicenvironment.scot">[Historic Environment Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">historicenvironment.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Historic Environment ScotlandTomnaverie Stone CircleTomnaverie is a recumbent stone circle, a kind of monument found only in north-easter...</span></span></span>

Folklore collectors found that stories around Aberdeenshire stone circles often followed recurring themes. A 1926 study of the county’s monument legends grouped traditions around ideas such as good and evil spirits, supposed ancient worship, buried treasure, human associations and animal connections. The use of terms such as “druid” in local tradition often reflected a general idea of an ancient or pagan past rather than evidence of actual Druid activity at these sites.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://journals.socantscot.org/index.php/psas/article/view/7678" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journals.socantscot.org">[Society of Antiquaries of Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journals.socantscot.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Society of Antiquaries of ScotlandFolklore of the Aberdeenshire Stone Circles and Standing-...by J Ritchie · 1926 · Cited by 6 — The leg...</span></span></span>

These stories helped preserve monuments in cultural memory. A farmer or traveller who knew a circle through a tale about giants or supernatural power was also recognising that the stones were special places that should not simply be treated as ordinary field material. In this way, folklore sometimes acted as an informal form of protection, even though the stories themselves developed long after the monuments were built.

### Giants, witches and the mystery of the stones

Giant traditions are among the most common explanations attached to ancient stones across Britain, including Aberdeenshire. When people encountered huge stones whose builders had been forgotten, stories often imagined them as the work of beings with extraordinary strength. Similar traditions attached stones to witches, magical contests and punishments, turning fixed objects in the landscape into reminders of dramatic events.

The attraction of these stories is easy to understand. A prehistoric circle on a windswept hill already feels separated from ordinary life. Folklore adds characters and events: a giant carrying stones, a witch creating a monument, or a person transformed into stone as a warning. These narratives explain why the monument exists while also giving it a moral or emotional meaning.

## Maiden Stone legends and transformations

The Maiden Stone is one of Aberdeenshire’s clearest examples of a monument whose physical history and legendary history developed together. The stone is a tall Pictish cross-slab, probably carved after AD 700, with Christian imagery on one face and Pictish symbols on the other. Archaeologically, it belongs to the early medieval world of carved stones rather than the prehistoric stone-circle tradition.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.historicenvironment.scot/visit/all/maiden-stone/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: historicenvironment.scot">[Historic Environment Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">historicenvironment.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Historic Environment Scotland Maiden Stone &#124; Historic Scotland</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Historic Environment Scotland Maiden Stone &#124; Historic Scotland</span></span></span>

Yet local tradition gave it a very different explanation. One well-known legend says that a maiden was turned into stone after losing a bet with the Devil. Another version links the monument with a young woman caught up in a family dispute. These stories explain the stone’s human-like appearance and unusual presence in the landscape, transforming an ancient carved monument into a tale of tragedy and supernatural punishment.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.aberdeenshire.gov.uk/leisure-sport-and-culture/archaeology/sites-to-visit/maiden-stone/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: aberdeenshire.gov.uk">[Aberdeenshire Council]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">aberdeenshire.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Aberdeenshire Council Maiden Stone</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Aberdeenshire CouncilMaiden Stone - Aberdeenshire Council...</span></span></span>

The contrast between the archaeological and legendary interpretations is what makes the Maiden Stone important. The carvings point to Pictish identity, Christianity and early medieval artistic traditions, while the folklore reflects later communities trying to understand a remarkable object whose original symbolism was no longer fully known.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.historicenvironment.scot/visit/all/maiden-stone/history-and-stories/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: historicenvironment.scot">[Historic Environment Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">historicenvironment.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on historicenvironment.scot.</span></span></span>

This pattern appears throughout Aberdeenshire: monuments survive physically, but their meanings change. A carved stone may become the trace of a cursed person; a prehistoric circle may become a place associated with hidden powers. The later story does not replace the earlier history, but it becomes another layer in the monument’s biography.

## Archaeology versus later storytelling

Ancient monuments often attract legends because they create a gap between what people can see and what they can know. A stone circle may be thousands of years old, while the communities living nearby may have no written record explaining who built it or why. Folklore fills that gap with memorable narratives.

Archaeology and folklore therefore answer different questions:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--comparison" markdown="1">

* **Archaeology asks:** when was the monument built, how was it constructed, and what evidence survives?
* **Folklore asks:** how did later communities explain, remember and emotionally connect with the place?

</div>

For Aberdeenshire’s stones, the two approaches are most valuable when considered together. The archaeological evidence shows the extraordinary age and craftsmanship of the monuments, while legends reveal the continuing relationship between people and landscape.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.aberdeenshire.gov.uk/leisure-sport-and-culture/archaeology/special-archaeology-in-the-area/recumbent-stone-circles/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: aberdeenshire.gov.uk">[Aberdeenshire Council]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">aberdeenshire.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Aberdeenshire CouncilRecumbent Stone CirclesThe Recumbent Stone Circle (RSC) is Aberdeenshire&#x27;s unique contribution to the tradition of e...</span></span></span>

A common misunderstanding is that a local legend must either be “true” or be dismissed as meaningless. In practice, traditions around stones are historical evidence of another kind: evidence for memory, belief and changing interpretations. A tale about a maiden turned to stone tells us little about how the monument was originally created, but it tells us a great deal about how later generations reacted to an ancient object standing in their midst.

## Why Aberdeenshire’s stones still matter

Today, Aberdeenshire’s stone monuments remain powerful meeting points between heritage and imagination. Visitors encounter them as archaeological sites, but they also encounter landscapes shaped by centuries of storytelling. A recumbent stone circle on farmland or a solitary carved stone beside a road carries both the evidence of the distant past and the stories attached to it.

The continuing appeal of these places comes from that combination. The stones are not mysterious because folklore has hidden their history; they are fascinating because their histories have multiple layers. The builders of the monuments, the medieval communities who carved and remembered stones such as the Maiden Stone, the storytellers who preserved legends, and modern visitors who seek out these landscapes have all contributed to Aberdeenshire’s living relationship with ancient monuments.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.aberdeenshire.gov.uk/leisure-sport-and-culture/archaeology/sites-to-visit/maiden-stone/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: aberdeenshire.gov.uk">[Aberdeenshire Council]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">aberdeenshire.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Aberdeenshire Council Maiden Stone</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Aberdeenshire CouncilMaiden Stone - Aberdeenshire Council...</span></span></span>
