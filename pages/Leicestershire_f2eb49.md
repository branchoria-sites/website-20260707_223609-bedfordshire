## Introduction

For this UK historic-county project, Leicestershire is treated as the historic county rather than only the modern administrative county. The Wikishire historic-county map and the Wikimedia historic-counties SVG use historic county boundaries as their organising frame, while modern local government and tourism material may group Leicester, Leicestershire and Rutland together.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://wikishire.co.uk/map/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wikishire.co.uk">[wikishire.co.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wikishire.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Wikishire Great Britain and Ireland</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wikishire Great Britain and Ireland</span></span></span> That matters because many older folklore sources, including Charles James Billson’s 1895 county collection and Roy Palmer’s later work, habitually discuss Leicestershire with Rutland, while this page keeps Leicestershire as its centre of gravity.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/stream/countyfolklore00folkuoft/countyfolklore00folkuoft_djvu.txt" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on archive.org.</span></span></span>

## What makes Leicestershire folklore distinctive?

Leicestershire’s folklore feels unusually grounded in named places. Black Annis belongs to the Dane Hills on the west side of Leicester; [Hallaton]({{ 'hallaton/' | relative_url }})’s Easter custom is tied to Hare Pie Bank and the neighbouring rivalry with Medbourne; St Morrell’s chapel is a specific hillside pilgrimage site; the Humber Stone survives as a local landmark with fairy and curse stories; and Bradgate Park’s legends grow from the ruins and oaks associated with Lady Jane Grey. These are not floating tales that could be dropped anywhere. They attach to roads, slopes, fields, churches, city buildings and old parish memory.

The county also sits in a useful middle ground between urban and rural folklore. Leicester supplies bogey figures, witch trials, guildhall ghosts and city legends; the villages preserve rough seasonal sport, saintly landscapes and church customs. That mixture helps explain why Leicestershire is strong in what might be called “localised folklore”: stories with a firm address, even when their origins are uncertain.

The main collector for older printed folklore is Charles James Billson, whose *County Folklore: Leicestershire and Rutland* was published by the Folk-Lore Society in 1895.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/stream/countyfolklore00folkuoft/countyfolklore00folkuoft_djvu.txt" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on archive.org.</span></span></span> Roy Palmer’s *The Folklore of Leicestershire and Rutland*, first published in 1985, later widened the reader’s view with seasonal customs, rituals, taboos, places, people, churches, ghosts, songs and local tradition.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://books.google.com/books/about/The_Folklore_of_Leicestershire_and_Rutla.html?id=PemBAAAAMAAJ" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: books.google.com">[Google Books]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">books.google.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Books The Folklore of Leicestershire and Rutland</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Books The Folklore of Leicestershire and Rutland</span></span></span> These works matter because many Leicestershire traditions are otherwise scattered through parish histories, local newspapers, archaeological reports, heritage websites and oral retellings.

## Black Annis: Leicester’s great bogeywoman

Black Annis is Leicestershire’s most famous supernatural figure. She is usually described as a witch-like hag or bogeywoman connected with the Dane Hills, west of Leicester, and known by variants such as Black Anna, Black Agnes or Cat Anna. The modern Story of Leicester account says she was believed to live in a cave in the Dane Hills and that her name was used as a threat to naughty children. It also notes a 1764 legal reference to land called “Black Anny’s Bower Close”, and John Heyrick’s 1797 poem, which helped fix her terrifying image in print.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.storyofleicester.info/faith-belief/black-annis-and-dane-hills/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: storyofleicester.info">[storyofleicester.info]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">storyofleicester.info</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Black Annis</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Black Annis</span></span></span>

The classic legend gives her a blue or dark face, iron claws and a taste for children and lambs. Stories say she dug her cave into sandstone, waited by an oak tree, reached into cottages through small windows, and prowled at night while people fastened doors and kept children indoors. Some versions say she had a tunnel to Leicester Castle or lurked near Rupert’s Gateway.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.storyofleicester.info/faith-belief/black-annis-and-dane-hills/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: storyofleicester.info">[storyofleicester.info]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">storyofleicester.info</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Black Annis</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Black Annis</span></span></span> These details make her more than a generic witch. She is part ogress, part child-warning figure, part landscape spirit and part local explanation for a frightening place at the edge of the old city.

There are several competing ways to read Black Annis. One cautious view treats her as a bogey figure preserved through local warning tales and sharpened by Heyrick’s poem. A more historicising interpretation links her to Agnes Scott, an anchoress or religious woman associated in later accounts with Swithland and the Dane Hills; this idea appears in older local discussion and was later assessed by Ronald Hutton, who rejected grand goddess-origin theories in favour of a more local development.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_Annis" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Black Annis</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Black Annis</span></span></span> More speculative modern writing has tried to connect her with ancient mother goddesses or pan-European hag figures, but those claims are much harder to prove and should be treated as interpretation rather than evidence.

What makes Black Annis powerful is the way she sits between old oral warning, antiquarian record and modern reinvention. The 1764 place-name gives the tradition a firm eighteenth-century footing; Heyrick’s poem gives it a memorable literary shape; later folklore books and local-history retellings keep her alive for modern Leicester. She is not simply “a witch from Leicestershire”. She is the county’s clearest example of how a local landscape can produce a monster with national reach.

## Hallaton: hare pie, bottle kicking and a living Easter custom

If Black Annis is Leicestershire’s best-known monster, Hallaton’s Hare Pie Scramble and Bottle Kicking is its most spectacular living custom. It takes place on Easter Monday in Hallaton and centres on a procession, a hare pie, penny loaves, decorated “bottles” that are really small barrels, and a rough contest between Hallaton and Medbourne. The English Folk Dance and Song Society describes the custom as beginning with a pie blessed at the parish church, followed by distribution or scrambling, a procession, and the carrying of the bottles towards Hare Pie Bank.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.efdss.org/learning/resources/beginners-guides/48-british-folk-customs-from-plough-monday-to-hocktide/3388-hare-pie" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: efdss.org">[English Folk Dance and Song Society]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">efdss.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">English Folk Dance and Song Society Hare Pie Scramble and Bottle Kicking</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">English Folk Dance and Song Society Hare Pie Scramble and Bottle Kicking</span></span></span>

The origin story usually says that two Hallaton women were saved from a charging bull when a hare distracted it. In gratitude, land was given so that a hare pie, loaves and ale would be provided every Easter Monday. Another strand explains the event as a survival of older spring or hare ritual, though that is much harder to demonstrate historically. What can be said safely is that records reach at least into the late eighteenth century, while the custom itself is widely believed locally to be older.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bottle-kicking" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wikipedia.org.</span></span></span>

The “bottle kicking” element is not a tidy village game. It is closer to the family of rough traditional football contests, such as Ashbourne’s Shrovetide Football in Derbyshire or the Haxey Hood in Lincolnshire. Two sides try to force the bottles across goal streams, and the struggle can range across fields, hedges and ditches.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bottle-kicking" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wikipedia.org.</span></span></span> The point is not sport in the modern regulated sense, but communal performance: rivalry, endurance, local identity and seasonal release.

Hallaton is also important because archaeology has deepened the folklore rather than replacing it. Excavations by the Hallaton Field Work Group with University of Leicester Archaeological Services uncovered the lost chapel of St Morrell and a medieval cemetery at the very place where the Easter Monday bottle kicking traditionally begins.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archaeologyuk.org/resource/buildings--burials-and-bones--the-story-of-hallaton-s-lost-pilgrims-chapel.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archaeologyuk.org">[Archaeology UK]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archaeologyuk.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">buildings burials and bones the story of hallaton s lost pilgrims chapel</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">buildings burials and bones the story of hallaton s lost pilgrims chapel</span></span></span> This does not prove a direct line from medieval pilgrimage to modern bottle kicking, but it shows that the hillside was already a charged religious landscape long before the custom was written down in detail.

## Saints, wells and pilgrimage: St Morrell’s Leicestershire afterlife

St Morrell is one of the county’s most intriguing saintly traditions because the evidence is both medieval and newly visible. Hallaton Museum explains that St Morrell’s Round, a modern circular pilgrimage, is inspired by medieval pilgrimages to St Morrell’s Chapel on the hillside near Hallaton. It identifies Morrell with Maurilius, a fifth-century bishop of Angers, and notes that the Hallaton dedication is unusual in Britain.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://hallaton-museum.co.uk/st-morrells-round/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: hallaton-museum.co.uk">[Hallaton Museum]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">hallaton-museum.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on hallaton-museum.co.uk.</span></span></span>

The lost chapel was rediscovered through community archaeology. University of Leicester Archaeological Services reported that work with local volunteers revealed the chapel plan, a cemetery, and evidence that the hillside had been used since at least the Roman period. A pilgrim badge inscribed with “Morrell” strengthened the case that this was a medieval pilgrimage site.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ulasnews.com/2014/09/16/hallatons-lost-chapel/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ulasnews.com">[ULAS News]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ulasnews.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">hallatons lost chapel</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">hallatons lost chapel</span></span></span> The discovery also produced the widely reported burial of two skeletons holding hands, a vivid reminder that pilgrimage sites were not only religious destinations but also places where memory, death and community overlapped.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://phys.org/news/2014-09-lost-chapel-skeletons-years.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: phys.org">[Phys.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">phys.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Lost chapel&#x27; skeletons found holding hands after 700 years</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Lost chapel&#x27; skeletons found holding hands after 700 years</span></span></span>

Holy wells add another layer to this sacred landscape. Bob Trubshaw’s survey for the Leicestershire Archaeological and Historical Society notes that, as elsewhere in Britain, Leicestershire and Rutland contain wells called holy or dedicated to saints, often valued for clean water and for reputations of never running dry.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://lahs.org.uk/blog/leicestershire-and-rutlands-holy-wells" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: lahs.org.uk">[Leicestershire Archaeology Society]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">lahs.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Leicestershire Archaeology Society Leicestershire and Rutland&#x27;s Holy Wells</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Leicestershire Archaeology Society Leicestershire and Rutland&#x27;s Holy Wells</span></span></span> In Hallaton, the field-name St Morells Well is recorded in 1606, and Trubshaw links it with earlier local names and the saintly tradition.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://hoap.co.uk/leics_rutland_holy_wells.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: hoap.co.uk">[Hoap]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">hoap.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Hoap Leicestershire and Rutland&#x27;s Holy Wells</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Hoap Leicestershire and Rutland&#x27;s Holy Wells</span></span></span>

For folklore readers, the key point is that Leicestershire’s sacred traditions are not only about miracle tales. They are about dependable water, parish boundaries, hillside chapels, medieval roads and the modern revival of walking routes. St Morrell’s Round explicitly does not claim to follow an exact medieval route; instead, it creates a modern pilgrimage through places that medieval pilgrims might have recognised.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.stmorrellsround.com/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: stmorrellsround.com">[stmorrellsround.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">stmorrellsround.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on stmorrellsround.com.</span></span></span> That honesty is valuable: it shows the difference between old devotion, archaeological recovery and present-day heritage-making.

## Witches, cunning fears and the law

Leicestershire witchcraft tradition has two different faces. One is legendary, represented by Black Annis and other frightening figures. The other is historical, preserved in records of accusation, examination and trial. These cases are not charming folklore in the cosy sense; they show how belief could become legal danger, especially for women.

Several Leicester and Leicestershire names appear in witch-trial lists and historical studies. Wallace Notestein’s *A History of Witchcraft in England* records Anne Chettle of Leicester being searched in 1650 and acquitted, with a later trial two years afterwards whose result is uncertain.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.free-ebooks.net/academic-history/A-History-of-Witchcraft-in-England/html/14" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: free-ebooks.net">[Free-eBooks.net]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">free-ebooks.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on free-ebooks.net.</span></span></span> Other lists include Agnes Tedsall of Leicester in 1635 and a cluster of Leicester-related cases across the late sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, though such lists need careful handling because they compress complex legal histories into brief entries.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.witchtrials.co.uk/county.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: witchtrials.co.uk">[witchtrials.co.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">witchtrials.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on witchtrials.co.uk.</span></span></span>

The Vale of Belvoir cases are especially well known. The “Witches of Belvoir” tradition concerns accusations connected with the deaths of children of the Earl of Rutland, and the wider examination named women such as Anne Baker of Bottesford, Joan Willimot of Goadby and Ellen Greene of Stathern. In the recorded narratives, familiars, visions and fairy-like beings appear beside household suspicion and elite misfortune.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Witches_of_Belvoir" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Witches of Belvoir</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Witches of Belvoir</span></span></span> This is where folklore and legal history meet: beliefs about familiars and supernatural harm were not just stories by the fire, but could be repeated under examination.

The Leicestershire Archaeological and Historical Society notes a later local incident at Great Glen in 1760, where accusations between two older women led to a swimming test and attacks on several women accused of witchcraft.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://lahs.org.uk/blog/something-wicked-this-way-comes-witchcraft-in-leicestershire" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: lahs.org.uk">[Leicestershire Archaeology Society]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">lahs.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">something wicked this way comes witchcraft in leicestershire</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">something wicked this way comes witchcraft in leicestershire</span></span></span> By then formal witchcraft prosecution had changed, but popular fear had not vanished. Leicestershire’s witchcraft record therefore belongs alongside its folklore, not because the accusations were true, but because they reveal what communities feared, how suspicion travelled, and how supernatural explanation could become social violence.

## Stones, hills and old places with fairy or curse traditions

Leicestershire is not a county of famous stone circles on the scale of Wiltshire or Orkney, but it does have old stones and rocky landmarks with folklore attached. The Humber Stone in Leicester is the clearest example. Local folklore says the stone was dropped by a god, associated with fairies, and unlucky to damage or move. One account says that after a farmer broke off the top to ease ploughing, he lost his prosperity and died in the workhouse.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.megalithic.co.uk/article.php?sid=10131" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: megalithic.co.uk">[Megalithic Portal]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">megalithic.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on megalithic.co.uk.</span></span></span>

The Humber Stone is also a good example of how folklore can preserve attention around a natural or prehistoric-looking feature even when interpretation is disputed. It has been described as a large glacial erratic and as one of Leicester’s oldest landmarks; its story now survives in local-history writing, megalithic gazetteers and popular news features.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.themodernantiquarian.com/site/295/humber-stone" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: themodernantiquarian.com">[themodernantiquarian.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">themodernantiquarian.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on themodernantiquarian.com.</span></span></span> Whether or not every detail is ancient, the tale gives the stone a protective aura: fairies, curses and misfortune all warn against treating the landmark as ordinary rubble.

Other Leicestershire stone traditions are more fragmentary. St John’s Stone, or Little John’s Stone, near Leicester Abbey, is described in antiquarian and modern megalithic sources as a once-impressive standing stone that gradually diminished and disappeared; local tradition says fragments may have been moved to St Luke’s Church.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.thenorthernantiquarian.org/2025/06/02/st-johns-stone/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: thenorthernantiquarian.org">[The Northern Antiquarian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">thenorthernantiquarian.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">st johns stone</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">st johns stone</span></span></span> Such stories are typical of British stone folklore: the object vanishes or is broken, but the place-name, warning or rumour lingers.

Beacon Hill and Charnwood add a different kind of mythic landscape. The British Geological Survey notes that rocks at Beacon Hill are among the oldest in England and Wales, formed from volcanic ash around 600 million years ago.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.bgs.ac.uk/discovering-geology/maps-and-resources/office-geology/beacon-hill-leicestershire/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: bgs.ac.uk">[British Geological Survey]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">bgs.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on bgs.ac.uk.</span></span></span> The National Forest Way points walkers to the “Old Man of Beacon Hill”, a named rock formation within the country park.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nationalforest.org/explore/walking/national-forest-way-stage-1-beacon-hill-to-bradgate" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nationalforest.org">[National Forest]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nationalforest.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nationalforest.org.</span></span></span> These are not necessarily old folktales in the strict collector’s sense, but they show how Leicestershire’s ancient geology invites personification and storytelling.

## Ghosts and haunted heritage

Leicestershire ghost lore is strongest where old buildings are already full of public history. Leicester Guildhall, a major civic building in the city, is now promoted through events and visitor material that include ghost stories. Visit Leicester mentions sightings of a phantom dog in the courtyard and a black cat in the Great Hall, while Leicester Museums hosts seasonal storytelling events such as “Ghosts at the Guildhall”.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://visitleicester.info/point-of-interest/leicester-guildhall/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: visitleicester.info">[Visit Leicester]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">visitleicester.info</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on visitleicester.info.</span></span></span>

Belgrave Hall shows how modern media can transform a local haunting into a wider paranormal story. In 1999, staff reported two ghostly figures on security cameras; later investigations suggested environmental or ordinary explanations, including a falling leaf or people in reflective jackets. Yet the hall remained part of Leicester’s haunted reputation through television and ghost-hunting interest.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Belgrave_Hall" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Belgrave Hall</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Belgrave Hall</span></span></span> This is modern folklore in action: even when an explanation is offered, the story may continue because the place has already gained a haunted identity.

Bradgate Park has its own ghostly layer, centred on Lady Jane Grey. The park is historically linked with the Grey family and the ruins of Bradgate House, where Lady Jane is traditionally associated with childhood before her brief reign and execution in 1554.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bradgate_House%2C_Bradgate_Park" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Bradgate House, Bradgate Park</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Bradgate House, Bradgate Park</span></span></span> Modern ghost-walk material retells the famous story of Lady Jane riding through the park in a coach at midnight, often on New Year’s Eve or another charged night depending on the version.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://hauntedheritage.co.uk/product/ghost-walk-bradgate-park/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: hauntedheritage.co.uk">[Haunted Heritage]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">hauntedheritage.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on hauntedheritage.co.uk.</span></span></span> The historical Lady Jane and the ghostly Lady Jane are not the same thing, but the haunting gives emotional form to a local memory of ambition, youth and political catastrophe.

Belvoir Castle, on the north-eastern edge of Leicestershire, also gathers witch and ghost associations. It is historically tied to the Manners family and the Vale of Belvoir witchcraft cases, while modern haunted-castle accounts add apparitions and spectral figures.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Witches_of_Belvoir" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Witches of Belvoir</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Witches of Belvoir</span></span></span> For a folklore reader, Belvoir is best understood as a layered site: aristocratic history, witch-trial memory, gothic architecture and visitor storytelling all reinforce one another.

## Folklore collectors and how the stories reached us

Leicestershire’s folklore survives through a chain of collectors, antiquaries, historians, local institutions and modern enthusiasts. John Nichols and other antiquarian writers preserved parish and county material, while Charles James Billson gathered printed extracts for the Folk-Lore Society’s 1895 county volume.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/stream/countyfolklore00folkuoft/countyfolklore00folkuoft_djvu.txt" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on archive.org.</span></span></span> Billson is particularly important for Black Annis because later accounts often return to his collection for early references, poems and local variants.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_Annis" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Black Annis</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Black Annis</span></span></span>

Roy Palmer later gave the county a broader, more accessible folklore treatment. His book covers places, people, churches, customs, songs, seasonal rites, ghosts, witches and other traditions, and later descriptions of his work emphasise that he treated folklore as a living field, not merely an antiquarian curiosity.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://books.google.com/books/about/The_Folklore_of_Leicestershire_and_Rutla.html?id=PemBAAAAMAAJ" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: books.google.com">[Google Books]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">books.google.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Books The Folklore of Leicestershire and Rutland</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Books The Folklore of Leicestershire and Rutland</span></span></span> That approach suits Leicestershire especially well because the county’s folklore is not sealed in the past. Hallaton still performs its Easter custom; St Morrell’s pilgrimage has been revived; Leicester’s haunted buildings are used for events; and Black Annis continues to appear in books, art, local media and online retellings.

Local institutions now play a major role in preservation. Story of Leicester gives public-facing accounts of city legends such as Black Annis; the Record Office for Leicestershire, Leicester and Rutland preserves material on the area’s history and culture; museums and archaeological groups interpret Hallaton, St Morrell and the Hallaton Treasure; and heritage bodies turn old routes and places into modern visitor experiences.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.storyofleicester.info/faith-belief/black-annis-and-dane-hills/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: storyofleicester.info">[storyofleicester.info]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">storyofleicester.info</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Black Annis</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Black Annis</span></span></span>

The result is a folklore record with uneven evidence but strong local texture. Some tales are old and documented; some are nineteenth-century literary elaborations; some are modern ghost-tour traditions; some are revived customs with medieval or archaeological echoes. Good Leicestershire folklore writing should keep those layers visible rather than flattening everything into either “ancient pagan survival” or “made-up tourist tale”.

## Old tradition, literary invention and modern retelling

A useful way to read Leicestershire folklore is to ask what kind of tradition each story is.

Black Annis has an eighteenth-century place-name anchor, a strong late eighteenth-century literary shaping through Heyrick, and a much wider modern life. Hallaton’s custom is living practice with late eighteenth-century documentation and older origin legends, but claims of pre-Christian survival remain uncertain. St Morrell has medieval pilgrimage evidence and modern revival, but the current circular route openly presents itself as inspired by, not identical with, medieval routes.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.storyofleicester.info/faith-belief/black-annis-and-dane-hills/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: storyofleicester.info">[storyofleicester.info]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">storyofleicester.info</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Black Annis</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Black Annis</span></span></span>

Ghost stories around Guildhall, Belgrave Hall and Bradgate Park are different again. They are often modern heritage retellings attached to genuinely old places. That does not make them worthless; it simply means their value lies in how people now experience historic buildings, ruins and landscapes. A phantom dog at the Guildhall or a coach at Bradgate Park tells us as much about modern appetite for haunted history as about older oral belief.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://visitleicester.info/point-of-interest/leicester-guildhall/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: visitleicester.info">[visitleicester.info]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">visitleicester.info</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on visitleicester.info.</span></span></span>

The same caution applies to stones and wells. A fairy tradition at the Humber Stone or a saint’s well at Hallaton may preserve older reverence for landscape features, but the surviving evidence usually comes through later recording. The folklore is still meaningful because it shaped behaviour: avoid the stone after dark, do not damage it, respect the well, remember the saint, walk the route.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.megalithic.co.uk/article.php?sid=10131" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: megalithic.co.uk">[Megalithic Portal]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">megalithic.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on megalithic.co.uk.</span></span></span>

## Why Leicestershire matters in UK folklore

Leicestershire matters because it shows how folklore works in a county without needing a single grand mythology. Its traditions are local, practical and place-bound. They live in a city cave that may no longer be findable, a rough Easter contest that still draws crowds, a saint’s chapel recovered from a hillside, a fairy-haunted stone in modern Leicester, and ghost stories wrapped around civic buildings and ruined houses.

The county also provides useful cross-links across the wider UK folklore map. Black Annis belongs beside English bogeywomen such as Jenny Greenteeth, Peg Powler and other child-warning figures. Hallaton’s Bottle Kicking belongs beside rough village games such as Ashbourne Shrovetide Football and the Haxey Hood. The Humber Stone belongs with the widespread British pattern of stones that cannot be counted, moved or damaged without consequence. St Morrell’s landscape belongs with holy wells, saints’ cults and modern pilgrimage revivals across England.

What keeps Leicestershire distinctive is the density of local address. The stories do not merely say “there was once a witch”, “there was once a ghost” or “there was once a custom”. They say: go to Dane Hills, Hallaton, Belvoir, Bradgate, the Guildhall, the Humber Stone, St Morrell’s hillside. In Leicestershire, folklore is a way of reading the county’s map.

    var select = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-select]');
    if (select) {
      return Array.prototype.slice.call(select.options)
        .map(function (option) { return option.value; })
        .filter(function (marketId) { return !!markets[marketId]; });
    }
    return Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]'))
      .map(function (button) { return button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button'); })
      .filter(function (marketId) { return !!markets[marketId]; });
  }
  function applyMarket(section, marketId, persist) {
    var available = availableMarkets(section);
    if (available.indexOf(marketId)< 0) marketId = available[0] || defaultMarket;
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-localized-link]')).forEach(function (link) {
      var query = link.getAttribute('data-ebay-query') || '';
      var reference = link.getAttribute('data-ebay-reference') || '';
      link.setAttribute('href', buildUrl(query, reference, marketId));
    });
    var select = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-select]');
    if (select && available.indexOf(marketId) >= 0) select.value = marketId;
    var selectedText = marketId;
    if (select && select.selectedIndex >= 0) selectedText = select.options[select.selectedIndex].text;
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-selected-market-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = selectedText;
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-selected-market-flag], [data-ebay-trigger-market-flag]')).forEach(function (flag) {
      flag.className = flagClass(marketId);
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-trigger-market-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = selectedText;
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-domain-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = domainLabel(marketId);
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-option]')).forEach(function (button) {
      button.setAttribute('aria-selected', button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-option') === marketId ? 'true' : 'false');
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      var active = button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button') === marketId;
      button.setAttribute('aria-pressed', active ? 'true' : 'false');
    });
    section.setAttribute('data-ebay-selected-market', marketId);
    if (persist) {
      try { if (window.localStorage) localStorage.setItem('phoenix-ebay-market', marketId); } catch (err) {}
    }
    closeMenu(section);
  }
  sections.forEach(function (section) {
    if (section.getAttribute('data-ebay-localized-init') === '1') return;
    section.setAttribute('data-ebay-localized-init', '1');
    var available = availableMarkets(section);
    var marketId = defaultMarket;
    if (window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation && window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation.inferMarket) {
      marketId = window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation.inferMarket(available, {
        storageKey: 'phoenix-ebay-market',
        defaultMarket: defaultMarket
      });
    } else if (available.indexOf(defaultMarket)< 0) {
      marketId = available[0] || defaultMarket;
    }
    var select = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-select]');
    if (select) {
      select.addEventListener('change', function () {
        applyMarket(section, select.value, true);
      });
    }
    var trigger = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-trigger]');
    var menu = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-menu]');
    if (trigger && menu) {
      trigger.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
        event.stopPropagation();
        var expanded = trigger.getAttribute('aria-expanded') === 'true';
        menu.hidden = expanded;
        trigger.setAttribute('aria-expanded', expanded ? 'false' : 'true');
        var picker = trigger.closest('.fr-ebay-market-picker');
        if (picker) picker.classList.toggle('fr-ebay-market-picker--open', !expanded);
      });
      Array.prototype.slice.call(menu.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-option]')).forEach(function (button) {
        button.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
          event.stopPropagation();
          applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-option'), true);
        });
      });
      document.addEventListener('click', function () { closeMenu(section); });
    }
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      button.addEventListener('click', function () {
        applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button'), true);
      });
    });
    applyMarket(section, marketId, false);
  });
})();
