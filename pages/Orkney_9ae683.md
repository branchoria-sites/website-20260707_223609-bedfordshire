## Introduction

Geographically, this page treats Orkney as the historic county and island group recognised in the UK historic-counties frame. The modern local authority, Orkney Islands Council, covers the islands today, while historic-county mapping and gazetteers continue to treat Orkney as its own county-level unit; for folklore, that matters because the stories are strongly island-rooted rather than simply “north Scottish” in a general sense.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://wikishire.co.uk/wiki/Orkney" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wikishire.co.uk">[Wikishire]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wikishire.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wikishire.co.uk.</span></span></span>

## Why Orkney folklore feels different

Orkney’s tradition is unusually easy to place on the map. Many counties have legends attached to a hill, wood or ruined castle; Orkney has whole story systems tied to visible prehistoric structures, shorelines, ferry crossings, lochs and uninhabited islands. The World Heritage landscape on Mainland is a good starting point: UNESCO describes the Heart of Neolithic Orkney as four major monuments that give exceptional evidence for Neolithic material life, belief and social organisation, while Historic Environment Scotland presents the same landscape as a group of stone monuments built around 5,000 years ago and set within a wider archaeological zone rich in remains.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.historicenvironment.scot/protect-and-care/protected-historic-places/world-heritage-sites/heart-of-neolithic-orkney/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: historicenvironment.scot">[Historic Environment Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">historicenvironment.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Historic Environment ScotlandThe Heart of Neolithic OrkneyThe Heart of Neolithic Orkney is a collection of four stone monuments built aro...</span></span></span>

That does not mean modern folklore preserves direct Neolithic belief. In fact, one of the most important things to understand about Orkney is the gap between archaeology and story. The builders of Stenness, Brodgar, Maeshowe and Skara Brae left no written explanation of their religion. Later Orcadians, antiquarians and visitors therefore interpreted the monuments through the ideas available to them: Norse gods, fairies, trows, giants, saints, treasure, oaths and dangerous enchantment. The result is a folklore of re-reading, where prehistoric sites became active places in much later imagination.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.orkney.com/news/scotland-in-six" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: orkney.com">[Orkney.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">orkney.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The secrets of Orkney&#x27;s World Heritage Site</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The secrets of Orkney&#x27;s World Heritage Site</span></span></span>

Orkney also sits at a cultural crossing point. The islands were under Norse rule for centuries before passing to the Scottish Crown in the fifteenth century, and the older Norn language left traces in place-names and folk vocabulary. Wikishire’s county profile summarises the deep Norse imprint, while modern Orkney accounts and folklore scholarship repeatedly point to the mix of Norse, Scottish and maritime influences in Orcadian tradition.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://wikishire.co.uk/wiki/Orkney" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wikishire.co.uk">[wikishire.co.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wikishire.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wikishire.co.uk.</span></span></span>

## Trows, mounds and the hidden life under the hills

The trow is one of the clearest examples of Orkney’s distinctive folklore. Trows are supernatural beings of Orkney and Shetland tradition, often compared with trolls, fairies or mound-dwellers, but they are not quite the same as any one of those categories. Accounts describe them as nocturnal, mischievous or dangerous, connected with earthen mounds and attracted to music, dancing and human households. Their name is usually linked to the Norse cultural world of the Northern Isles, though the exact relationship between trows, trolls and fairies is more complicated than a simple “Viking survival” label suggests.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trow_%28folklore%29" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Trow (folklore</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Trow (folklore</span></span></span>

For readers, the key point is that trow lore helped explain the uncanny presence of ancient mounds and ruined structures in everyday island life. A mound was not just archaeology before archaeology had a vocabulary for it; it could be a trowie knowe, a place where hidden beings lived, danced, played music or interfered with human life. Modern retellings such as “The Dancers Under the Hill” preserve this logic well: mysterious stone buildings and mounds are interpreted through stories of magical beings below ground.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.northlinkferries.co.uk/orkney-blog/an-orkney-folk-tale-the-dancers-under-the-hill/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: northlinkferries.co.uk">[NorthLink Ferries]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">northlinkferries.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on northlinkferries.co.uk.</span></span></span>

This is where Orkney differs from counties where “fairy hill” traditions are scattered and localised. In Orkney, the density of prehistoric remains gives the motif a special force. The islands are full of visible ancient stonework, chambered tombs, brochs, mounds and settlement traces. Trow stories turned those places into inhabited, morally charged landscapes. They made the past feel present, not as a museum exhibit but as a neighbourly danger: something nearby, old, hidden and not entirely friendly.

## Selkies, finfolk and the dangerous edge of the sea

Orkney’s sea folklore is among the richest in Britain because the sea is not background scenery; it is food, work, travel, threat and boundary. Selkie stories, finfolk tales and mermaid traditions all use the same basic tension: the shore is where human and non-human worlds meet, and where beauty can become captivity. National Library of Scotland material on Orkney and Shetland highlights shape-shifting seals, finfolk and other sea beings as central to the northern island story-world.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://artsandculture.google.com/story/myths-amp-legends-from-orkney-and-shetland-national-library-of-scotland/xAVB8Bl-MIaRKw?hl=en" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: artsandculture.google.com">[Google Arts &amp; Culture]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">artsandculture.google.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on google.com.</span></span></span>

Selkies are the best known outside Orkney: seal people who can remove their skins and appear human. Popular retellings often make selkie stories romantic or melancholy, especially when a human steals a seal-woman’s skin and traps her in marriage. Orkney’s finfolk, however, are darker and less sentimental. They are usually described as sea-dwelling or amphibious sorcerers who move between the underwater realm and the islands, abduct humans, prize silver and use deception.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finfolk" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wikipedia.org.</span></span></span>

The finfolk stories are strongly associated with Eynhallow and the legendary Hildaland, the hidden island. In the National Library of Scotland’s retelling, Eynhallow was once understood as Hildaland, the invisible summer home of the finfolk. Orkney archaeology and folklore commentary likewise describes finfolk as part of a world of shape-shifting, magic and deception, with Finfolkaheem beneath the sea and Hildaland as the place to which captives might be taken.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://artsandculture.google.com/story/myths-amp-legends-from-orkney-and-shetland-national-library-of-scotland/xAVB8Bl-MIaRKw?hl=en" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: artsandculture.google.com">[Google Arts &amp; Culture]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">artsandculture.google.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on google.com.</span></span></span>

A useful distinction is this: selkie stories often focus on loss, longing and the divided life between land and sea; finfolk stories focus more sharply on abduction, coercion, sorcery and bargaining. Both belong to the same maritime imagination, but they answer different emotional questions. Selkie lore asks what happens when love crosses the boundary between species and worlds. Finfolk lore asks what happens when the sea itself wants something from you.

## The nuckelavee and the fear of blight, storm and salt water

The nuckelavee is Orkney’s most frightening supernatural creature: a monstrous sea-being often described in later retellings as a skinless horse-and-rider horror whose breath brings sickness, crop failure and disaster. The most important caution is that modern monster summaries can flatten the tradition into fantasy-horror. In older Orcadian context, the nuckelavee belongs to a world of maritime weather, agricultural vulnerability and island fear, not simply to a gallery of “creatures”.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuckelavee" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wikipedia.org.</span></span></span>

Accounts commonly connect the nuckelavee with Walter Traill Dennison, the nineteenth-century Orcadian folklorist who preserved important sea-myth material. The creature is often said to be unable to cross fresh water and to be restrained in summer by the Mither o’ the Sea, another Orcadian sea figure. This seasonal element matters: the tale turns the changing sea, disease, drought, failed crops and dangerous coasts into a supernatural drama with rules and rhythms.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuckelavee" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wikipedia.org.</span></span></span>

The nuckelavee also shows how Orkney folklore can blend Norse and wider Scottish motifs without becoming generic. It resembles water-horse traditions found elsewhere in Scotland and northern Europe, but its Orcadian form is unusually sea-bound, grotesque and destructive. That makes it a strong candidate for internal linking to wider water-horse traditions, kelpie lore and coastal demon stories elsewhere in the UK, while keeping Orkney as the centre of gravity.

## Standing stones: ancient monuments, later vows and walking stones

Orkney’s standing-stone folklore is not the same thing as Neolithic religion, and readers are often misled if the two are merged too easily. Archaeology can tell us that the Stones of Stenness, Ring of Brodgar, Maeshowe and Skara Brae are among Western Europe’s most important Neolithic sites; it cannot tell us that later tales of Odin, fairies or walking stones are direct memories from 3000 BC. UNESCO and Historic Environment Scotland both stress the monuments’ outstanding prehistoric importance, but the folklore attached to them often comes from much later oral, antiquarian and literary layers.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.historicenvironment.scot/protect-and-care/protected-historic-places/world-heritage-sites/heart-of-neolithic-orkney/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: historicenvironment.scot">[Historic Environment Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">historicenvironment.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Historic Environment ScotlandThe Heart of Neolithic OrkneyThe Heart of Neolithic Orkney is a collection of four stone monuments built aro...</span></span></span>

The Odin Stone is the clearest example. This holed stone once stood near Stenness and became famous for oath-making, especially handfasting or marriage promises made by clasping hands through the hole. The stone was destroyed in 1814, an act remembered in local and antiquarian accounts as a cultural loss because Orcadians believed the stone had special power. The Ness of Brodgar account notes that the Odin Stone was especially associated with sealing agreements and marriage vows through the so-called Odin Oath.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nessofbrodgar.co.uk/odinstone/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nessofbrodgar.co.uk">[The Ness of Brodgar Project]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nessofbrodgar.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nessofbrodgar.co.uk.</span></span></span>

Brodgar and Stenness were also interpreted through romantic “temple” language by some later writers, with associations to the sun, moon and Odin. Modern commentary is more careful: these names and explanations may reflect eighteenth- and nineteenth-century antiquarian imagination as much as genuine long-lived tradition. That uncertainty does not make the folklore worthless. It makes it historically interesting, because it shows how people in different periods tried to make sense of monuments whose original meanings were already lost.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ring_of_Brodgar" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Ring of Brodgar</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Ring of Brodgar</span></span></span>

Orkney also has the memorable motif of moving or walking stones. A Hogmanay tale from Birsay, for example, tells of a standing stone that moves at midnight towards the loch before returning to its place. Such stories belong to a wider British and Irish pattern in which stones walk, drink, dance or punish disrespect, but in Orkney the motif gains extra power from the sheer presence of monumental stone in the landscape.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.firesidehorror.co.uk/blog-2/new-years-folklore-the-hogmanay-horror" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: firesidehorror.co.uk">[Fireside Horror]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">firesidehorror.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Fireside Horror New Years Folklore</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Fireside Horror New Years Folklore</span></span></span>

## Witches, courts and the memory of injustice

Orkney’s witchcraft history is not merely spooky folklore; it is a documented history of accusation, coercion, trial and execution, later surrounded by oral memory and modern commemoration. Orkney Heritage Society notes that court records and oral traditions both preserve memories of witchcraft, and identifies Janet Forsyth, the Westray Storm Witch, as the best-known victim in folk memory. Orkney Museum records that accused people could be held in Marwick’s Hole, the dungeon-like cell in St Magnus Cathedral, before trial and execution.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://orkneyheritagesociety.org.uk/projects/orkney-witchcraft-trial-memorial/history-of-witchcraft-trials-in-orkney/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: orkneyheritagesociety.org.uk">[Orkney Heritage Society]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">orkneyheritagesociety.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on orkneyheritagesociety.org.uk.</span></span></span>

The first major Orkney witchcraft case is usually associated with Alison Balfour in 1594. Modern Orkney visitor material explains that she confessed under torture after being accused in the context of a plot against Patrick Stewart, Earl of Orkney. Wider summaries of Orkney witchcraft trials record that the trials continued into the early eighteenth century and that more than seventy people were taken to court, most of them women.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.orkney.com/news/witchcraft-trials-walk" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: orkney.com">[Orkney.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">orkney.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Walking in the footsteps of Orkney&#x27;s witches</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Walking in the footsteps of Orkney&#x27;s witches</span></span></span>

What makes Orkney’s witch-trial memory especially important is the meeting point between official persecution and older belief. Some cases involved accusations about charms, fairies, weather-working or harmful magic rather than the tidy modern image of the “witch”. Orkney Museum notes that most of the accused were poor women on the margins of society, and that recent research may put the number tried higher than the older figure of seventy-two.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://orkneymuseum.wordpress.com/category/folklore-customs/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: orkneymuseum.wordpress.com">[Orkney Museum]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">orkneymuseum.wordpress.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Orkney Museum Folklore &amp; Customs</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Orkney Museum Folklore &amp; Customs</span></span></span>

The modern memorial at Gallow Ha’ in Kirkwall, unveiled in 2019, changes how the tradition is retold. It moves the story away from Halloween entertainment alone and towards remembrance of people harmed by fear, power and legal violence. That does not remove the folklore; it reframes it. The “witch” becomes not only a supernatural figure in story but also a historical person whose reputation was shaped by accusation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://orkneymuseums.co.uk/the-witchs-spell-box/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: orkneymuseums.co.uk">[Orkney Council Museums]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">orkneymuseums.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on orkneymuseums.co.uk.</span></span></span>

## Saints, holy places and healing water

Christian legend is another major layer in Orkney’s story culture. St Magnus is central: Orkney Museum describes St Magnus Cathedral as dedicated to Magnus, twelfth-century Earl of Orkney, killed on the orders of his cousin and rival Hakon, after which miracles were reported. Historic Environment Scotland’s account of St Magnus Church on Egilsay places the killing within the saga tradition, where Magnus travelled to Egilsay to resolve a dispute over control of Orkney and was murdered there.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://orkneymuseums.co.uk/our-museums/st-magnus-cathedral/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: orkneymuseums.co.uk">[Orkney Council Museums]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">orkneymuseums.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on orkneymuseums.co.uk.</span></span></span>

For folklore, Magnus matters because his story ties sanctity, violence, kingship and place together. Kirkwall Cathedral is not just an architectural landmark; it is the built centre of a saint’s cult, with relics, saga memory and local identity bound into one institution. This gives Orkney a different sacred texture from counties where saints’ legends survive mainly in isolated wells or parish dedications. Here, the saint’s story stands in the main town, in stone.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://orkneymuseums.co.uk/our-museums/st-magnus-cathedral/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: orkneymuseums.co.uk">[Orkney Council Museums]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">orkneymuseums.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on orkneymuseums.co.uk.</span></span></span>

Holy wells and healing waters add a quieter but important strand. St Tredwell’s Chapel and Loch on Papa Westray are associated with healing traditions, especially eye cures in later accounts, and older descriptions mention offerings made into the loch. Broader work on Scottish holy wells notes that wells and springs could function as places of healing, penance and Christianised ritual, sometimes absorbing older water reverence into saintly devotion.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.thenorthernantiquarian.org/2023/01/20/st-tredwells/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: thenorthernantiquarian.org">[thenorthernantiquarian.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">thenorthernantiquarian.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">St. Tredwell&#x27;s Chapel, Papa Westray, Orkney Isles</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">St. Tredwell&#x27;s Chapel, Papa Westray, Orkney Isles</span></span></span>

These traditions are best read with care. A healing loch or saint’s well does not prove an unbroken pagan cult, and the temptation to make every holy well “pre-Christian” can overreach the evidence. What can be said with confidence is that Orkney, like much of Scotland, preserved traditions in which water, offering, saintly protection and bodily cure were closely linked.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://scarf.scot/regional/higharf/medieval/9-6-religion-and-ritual/9-6-6-holy-wells/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: scarf.scot">[scarf.scot]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">scarf.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on scarf.scot.</span></span></span>

## The Ba’ and living custom in Kirkwall

Not all Orkney folklore is about supernatural beings. The Kirkwall Ba’ is a living seasonal custom played in the streets at Christmas and New Year, and it is one of the islands’ strongest public traditions. Orkney Museum describes the Uppies and Doonies competing to get the handmade leather ball to historically defined goals, and notes the first women’s Ba’ in 1945. Orkney’s official events material describes the game as a custom played in Kirkwall’s streets for hundreds of years, with teams originally associated with the south and north ends of the town.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://orkneymuseums.co.uk/the-ba-games/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: orkneymuseums.co.uk">[Orkney Council Museums]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">orkneymuseums.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Orkney Council Museums Case Study: The Ba&#x27; Games</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Orkney Council Museums Case Study: The Ba&#x27; Games</span></span></span>

The Ba’ belongs to the family of traditional mass football games once found in several parts of Britain, but in Orkney it remains strongly local and identity-forming. NorthLink’s beginner guide notes early records of an old-style football game in 1650 and describes the movement of the game from open ground towards the Kirk green and then the streets.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.northlinkferries.co.uk/orkney-blog/beginners-guide-to-the-ba/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: northlinkferries.co.uk">[NorthLink Ferries]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">northlinkferries.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on northlinkferries.co.uk.</span></span></span>

For folklore readers, the Ba’ matters because it shows tradition as action, not just narrative. It is memory carried by bodies, routes, allegiance, family and town geography. It also demonstrates why Orkney folklore should not be reduced to “myths and monsters”. Custom, sport, seasonal rivalry and communal belonging are part of the same cultural field.

Other calendar customs survive more unevenly. Halloween in Orkney has been remembered as “Devilment Night”, associated with pranks and mischief, while Hogmanay stone legends and wider Scottish New Year customs show how the turning of the year attracted both communal ritual and uncanny storytelling. Some of this material is recent, local or anecdotal rather than deeply documented, but it still shows how seasonal thresholds invite misrule, joking, fear and story.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.jollysoforkney.co.uk/blogs/news/orkney-halloween-traditions" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: jollysoforkney.co.uk">[jollysoforkney.co.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">jollysoforkney.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on jollysoforkney.co.uk.</span></span></span>

## Who preserved Orkney’s stories?

Much of what modern readers know as “Orkney folklore” comes through collectors, antiquarians, local historians, storytellers and museums. Walter Traill Dennison is especially important for nineteenth-century sea myths, including material later associated with the nuckelavee and finfolk. Ernest Marwick’s work on Orkney and Shetland folklore is another major reference point, frequently cited in modern discussions of trows, witchcraft and island belief.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuckelavee" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wikipedia.org.</span></span></span>

Tom Muir is central to contemporary public retelling. TRACS describes him as an Orcadian storyteller who tells traditional stories from Orkney, including sea monsters, trows, mermaids, finfolk, selkie folk, witches, shipwrecks and ghosts. The Bottle Imp notes that he has written on Orkney folklore, archaeology and history and worked in the archaeology field and as Exhibitions Officer in the Orkney Museum.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://tracscotland.org/storytellers/tom-muir/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: tracscotland.org">[TRACS]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">tracscotland.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">TRACSTom Muir</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">TRACSTom Muir</span></span></span>

Museums and archives also shape the tradition. Orkney Museum’s articles on the witch’s spell box, the Ba’ and folklore customs show how objects, court memory and seasonal practice are interpreted for public audiences. Orkney Library and Archive material on witchcraft trial sources points readers towards the record base behind what might otherwise be treated as legend alone.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://orkneymuseums.co.uk/the-witchs-spell-box/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: orkneymuseums.co.uk">[Orkney Council Museums]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">orkneymuseums.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on orkneymuseums.co.uk.</span></span></span>

This chain of preservation matters because folklore is not raw survival from an ancient past. It is collected, edited, performed, printed, archived, illustrated, walked as a tour, marketed to visitors and retold by families. Orkney’s stories are old in theme, but their public forms are often modern.

## Old oral tradition, antiquarian romance and tourism retelling

A good Orkney folklore page has to separate layers without stripping the stories of charm. Some traditions are strongly rooted in oral culture and local belief: trows in mounds, sea-beings by the shore, dangerous weather lore, witchcraft memories, healing waters and seasonal games. Others come to us through antiquarian framing, where eighteenth- or nineteenth-century writers attached classical, Norse or romantic labels to older sites. The “Temple of the Sun” and “Temple of the Moon” language around Brodgar and Stenness is a useful example of a tradition that may tell us more about antiquarian imagination than about ancient Orcadian ritual.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ring_of_Brodgar" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Ring of Brodgar</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Ring of Brodgar</span></span></span>

Tourism adds another layer. The Heart of Neolithic Orkney is now a major visitor landscape, and modern guides naturally present the stones, tombs and village through a mixture of archaeology, mystery and atmosphere. VisitScotland frames the World Heritage Site as a way to step back into the lives of ancient people, while Orkney.com acknowledges that questions about why the monuments were built have no simple answers.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.visitscotland.com/things-to-do/unesco-trail/neolithic-orkney" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: visitscotland.com">[VisitScotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">visitscotland.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Visit Scotland Heart of Neolithic Orkney</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Visit Scotland Heart of Neolithic Orkney</span></span></span>

Recent archaeology can also change how folklore feels, even when it does not “prove” a legend. The Ness of Brodgar excavations, begun after discoveries in the early 2000s and reburied for preservation in 2024, have transformed understanding of the Neolithic ceremonial landscape between the Loch of Stenness and the Loch of Harray. This makes the old story-rich landscape feel even more densely meaningful, but it should not be used to claim that trow or Odin stories are Neolithic memories.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.theguardian.com/science/article/2024/may/11/orkney-ness-of-brodgar-neolithic-site-reburied-after-excavation" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theguardian.com">[The Guardian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theguardian.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on theguardian.com.</span></span></span>

The honest approach is also the most interesting one: Orkney’s folklore is powerful precisely because it is layered. A single place may be prehistoric monument, Norse-influenced legend site, antiquarian puzzle, tourist destination, conservation challenge and living local landmark all at once.

## What Orkney contributes to UK folklore

Orkney’s contribution to UK folklore is not just a list of unusual beings. Its real importance lies in the way it shows how island communities turn landscape pressure into story. Dangerous seas become finfolk, selkies and the nuckelavee. Ancient mounds become trow dwellings. Standing stones become oath-sites and moving stones. A cathedral becomes both shrine and witch-trial memory. A town game becomes a living seasonal identity.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://artsandculture.google.com/story/myths-amp-legends-from-orkney-and-shetland-national-library-of-scotland/xAVB8Bl-MIaRKw?hl=en" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: artsandculture.google.com">[google.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">artsandculture.google.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on google.com.</span></span></span>

For cross-branch comparison, Orkney naturally links to Shetland through trows, finfolk, Norn vocabulary and northern sea lore. It links to wider Scottish folklore through water-horses, witchcraft trials, holy wells, Halloween and Hogmanay customs. It links to other UK prehistoric landscapes, such as Wiltshire’s stone-circle traditions, through the question of how later communities explain monuments whose builders left no text.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trow_%28folklore%29" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[wikipedia.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Trow (folklore</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Trow (folklore</span></span></span>

But Orkney should not be treated as a remote appendix to mainland folklore. It is one of the places where the UK’s legendary landscape is most concentrated and most legible. The stories remain compelling because they are attached to real routes, shores, ruins, lochs and public customs. In Orkney, folklore is not only something once told by the fire; it is something a reader can still meet in the shape of the land.
