## Introduction

Radnor Forest’s dragon legend says that the last dragon in Wales is not dead, but asleep beneath the upland mass of historic [Radnorshire]({{ 'where-radnorshires-old-stories-still/' | relative_url }}). It is kept there by a ring of churches dedicated to St Michael, the archangel traditionally shown defeating the dragon. In the best-known version there are four churches in the ring: Llanfihangel Rhydithon, Llanfihangel Nant Melan, Llanfihangel Cefnllys and Llanfihangel Cascob. A wider modern version adds a fifth, Llanfihangel Discoed. The legend matters because it turns Radnor Forest itself into a sacred mechanism: hills become the dragon’s body, churches become locks, and the county’s remote lanes and churchyards become part of one protective pattern.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.midwalesmyway.com/thedragonofradnorforest" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: midwalesmyway.com">[Mid Wales My Way]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">midwalesmyway.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on midwalesmyway.com.</span></span></span>



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/Radnorshire_2d75e1_radnor_forest_dragon_8fc92c-overview.webp" | relative_url }}" alt="Overview image for Dragon Ring" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

The story is not strongly attested as an early medieval tale in the way some national Welsh legends are. Its public evidence is mostly modern local retelling, tourism writing, church-trail material and county folklore preservation. That does not make it unimportant. It makes it a revealing piece of Radnorshire folklore: a local myth that binds Christian dedication, borderland topography, medieval church sites, ancient-looking churchyards and modern landscape identity into one memorable tale.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://books.google.com/books/about/The_Folklore_of_Radnorshire.html?id=wqb_AAAACAAJ" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: books.google.com">[Google Books]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">books.google.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Books The Folklore of Radnorshire</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Books The Folklore of Radnorshire</span></span></span>

## The sleeping dragon story

The core tale is simple. Somewhere under Radnor Forest sleeps the last dragon in Wales. The people of the district, or sometimes unnamed earlier powers, built churches in a ring around the forest and dedicated them to St Michael. As long as the ring remains intact, the dragon sleeps. If one of the churches is destroyed, the protection fails and the dragon wakes. Mid Wales My Way gives the common four-church form and names Llanfihangel Rhydithon, Llanfihangel Nant Melan, Llanfihangel Cefnllys and Llanfihangel Cascob as the guarding churches.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.midwalesmyway.com/thedragonofradnorforest" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: midwalesmyway.com">[Mid Wales My Way]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">midwalesmyway.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on midwalesmyway.com.</span></span></span>

It is a legend of containment rather than combat. Unlike a heroic dragon-slaying story, it does not centre on a named knight or saint arriving at the last moment with a sword. Its drama lies in maintenance. The dragon is already below the hills, the churches are already in place, and the danger is not that the beast will be killed or conquered but that the old protective order might be neglected. That is why the legend works so well as a local landscape story: the reader or walker can look at the map, visit the churches, and imagine the ring still doing its work.

St Michael is the key to the mechanism. In Christian iconography he is the heavenly warrior who defeats the dragon, commonly understood as the Devil. The Friends of Radnor Forest page summarises this local logic directly: St Michael is associated with fighting the dragon, and the St Michael churches around Radnor Forest are said to contain the beast and protect the area.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.radnorfforest.co.uk/explore" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: radnorfforest.co.uk">[Friends of Radnor Forest]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">radnorfforest.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Friends of Radnor Forest Explore — Friends of Radnor Forest</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Friends of Radnor Forest Explore — Friends of Radnor Forest</span></span></span>

The name of several places also helps the story feel rooted rather than imported. “Llanfihangel” means a church or enclosure of St Michael, so the dedication is built into the place-name itself. Around Radnor Forest, the repeated Llanfihangel names make the map look as if it is already whispering the legend: Rhydithon, Nant Melan, Cefnllys, Cascob and, in the five-church version, Discoed.

## Four or five St Michael churches?

The first thing readers usually notice is that the number changes. Some retellings say four churches guard the dragon; others say five. That variation is not a mistake to be smoothed away. It is part of the way the legend now survives.

The four-church version is the tidiest. It appears in public-facing tourism material and presents the ring as Llanfihangel Rhydithon, Llanfihangel Nant Melan, Llanfihangel Cefnllys and Llanfihangel Cascob. This version is easy to remember and gives the story a strong protective-circle shape.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.midwalesmyway.com/thedragonofradnorforest" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: midwalesmyway.com">[Mid Wales My Way]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">midwalesmyway.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on midwalesmyway.com.</span></span></span>

The five-church version adds Llanfihangel Discoed. Friends of Radnor Forest says there are ten St Michael churches in Radnorshire and identifies five around Radnor Forest: Llanfihangel Nant Melan, Llanfihangel Cefnllys, Llanfihangel Rhydithon, Llanfihangel Discoed and Llanfihangel Cascob. It then links these churches to the sleeping dragon tradition.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.radnorfforest.co.uk/explore" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: radnorfforest.co.uk">[Friends of Radnor Forest]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">radnorfforest.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Friends of Radnor Forest Explore — Friends of Radnor Forest</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Friends of Radnor Forest Explore — Friends of Radnor Forest</span></span></span>

The difference probably reflects two overlapping ways of telling the story:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--caution" markdown="1">

* **A symbolic ring:** Four churches make a clean encircling pattern and a clear warning: break one part of the ring and the dragon wakes.
* **A fuller St Michael landscape:** Five churches better reflects the wider cluster of St Michael dedications around the forest and the Marches border.
* **A living local tradition:** Folklore often survives in variants, especially when it is transmitted through walks, parish interpretation, tourism copy, local groups and oral retelling rather than through a single fixed medieval text.

</div>

For a folklore page, the safest reading is therefore not “which number is correct?” but “what does each number do?” Four gives the story its memorable protective geometry. Five makes the Radnor Forest landscape feel even more saturated with St Michael dedications.

## The churches in the ring

The legend depends on real churches, not invented shrines. Their settings are as important as their names: remote valleys, old churchyards, yews, mounds, ruined lordship landscapes and border-country lanes all help the dragon story feel plausible as a local belief-world.

### Llanfihangel Rhydithon, also known as Dolau

St Michael, Dolau represents Llanfihangel Rhydithon in the legend. Church Heritage Cymru identifies it as St Michael, Dolau, gives the grid reference SO 151 667, and notes that the church is open for worship. Its setting is described as a small settlement on the Central Wales Railway, looking south over the Maes Brook, a tributary system that eventually runs towards the Ithon.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://churchheritagecymru.org.uk/CHR/ChurchDetails.aspx?id=4340" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: churchheritagecymru.org.uk">[churchheritagecymru.org.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">churchheritagecymru.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Church Details.aspx</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">St Michael, Dolau - CHC Church...</span></span></span>

This matters because Rhydithon gives the ring a northern or north-western anchor in the older Radnorshire landscape. The present building is not a pristine medieval survival: Church Heritage Cymru records that a previous church was rebuilt in 1838 and later restored, while retaining older features such as a fourteenth-century font and eighteenth-century memorials.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://churchheritagecymru.org.uk/CHR/ChurchDetails.aspx?id=4340" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: churchheritagecymru.org.uk">[churchheritagecymru.org.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">churchheritagecymru.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Church Details.aspx</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">St Michael, Dolau - CHC Church...</span></span></span>

That mixture is typical of the legend’s evidence. The folklore imagines an ancient protective ring, while the buildings themselves have been repaired, rebuilt and altered across centuries. The power of the story does not rest on every stone being original. It rests on continuity of dedication, site and local memory.

### Llanfihangel Nant Melan

Llanfihangel Nant Melan sits near the A44 on the southern side of the Radnor Forest story-map. The public church-survey record identifies it as the Church of St Michael, Llanfihangel Nant Melan, in the community of New Radnor, with the grid reference SO1801358186.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://heneb.org.uk/archive/cpat/Archive/churches/radnor/16855.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: heneb.org.uk">[Heneb]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">heneb.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Heneb Radnorshire Churches Survey</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Heneb Radnorshire Churches Survey</span></span></span>

The church is often described as medieval in origin but heavily rebuilt in the nineteenth century. A widely repeated architectural note says it was rebuilt in 1846 in a Norman revival style, based on the celebrated Herefordshire church at Kilpeck.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Llanfihangel_Nant_Melan" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Llanfihangel Nant Melan</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Llanfihangel Nant Melan</span></span></span>

For the dragon legend, Nant Melan is valuable because it ties the supernatural ring to one of Radnorshire’s most important approaches: the A44 corridor near New Radnor and the edge of the forest. The road, the valley and the dedication combine to make this church feel like a threshold marker between everyday travel and the dragon-bearing upland.

### Llanfihangel Cefnllys

Cefnllys is the most atmospheric of the four-church version. St Michael, Cefnllys stands in a remote setting near Llandrindod Wells, below the site of Cefnllys Castle. Britain Express describes it as a restored medieval church at the foot of the thirteenth-century fortress associated with Roger Mortimer and the Marcher lordship world. It also notes that the church probably predates the castle, with the circular churchyard and dedication to St Michael suggesting an early foundation, although the earliest written evidence cited there is from 1291.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.britainexpress.com/wales/mid/llanfihangel-cefnllys-church.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: britainexpress.com">[Britain Express]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">britainexpress.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on britainexpress.com.</span></span></span>

This is where the legend gathers historical weight. Cefnllys is not only a church; it is a lost settlement landscape. The same account says the church once served a medieval borough that declined until almost nothing remained of the village. The result is a place that feels suspended between ruin and survival: castle earthworks above, a remote church below, and a tradition in which one damaged or abandoned holy site could endanger the whole countryside.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.britainexpress.com/wales/mid/llanfihangel-cefnllys-church.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: britainexpress.com">[Britain Express]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">britainexpress.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on britainexpress.com.</span></span></span>

The Church in Wales still lists St Michael, Cefnllys as a church in the West Radnor Ministry Area, giving its address at Cefnllys, Llandrindod Wells. That continuing ecclesiastical identity matters for a legend whose central anxiety is what happens if the guarding churches fail.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.churchinwales.org.uk/en/structure/church/5544/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: churchinwales.org.uk">[Church in Wales]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">churchinwales.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">St Michael, Cefnllys - The Church in Wales...</span></span></span>

### Llanfihangel Cascob

Cascob is one of the strongest physical anchors for the legend because its churchyard and building invite older, mound-like interpretations. British Listed Buildings, drawing on Cadw data, lists the Church of St Michael at Cascob as Grade II*, gives its traditional county as Radnorshire, and describes it as standing in a secluded setting at the head of the narrow Cascob valley below the slopes of Radnor Forest.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://britishlistedbuildings.co.uk/300009079-church-of-st-michael-whitton" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: britishlistedbuildings.co.uk">[British Listed Buildings]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">britishlistedbuildings.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">British Listed Buildings Church of St Michael, Whitton, Powys</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">British Listed Buildings Church of St Michael, Whitton, Powys</span></span></span>

The same listing records the church as medieval in origin, possibly on a pre-Christian sacred site, and says the tower is built over what may be a large prehistoric burial mound. It also notes a circular enclosed churchyard, thick walls, fifteenth-century tracery and a fourteenth-century font.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://britishlistedbuildings.co.uk/300009079-church-of-st-michael-whitton" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: britishlistedbuildings.co.uk">[British Listed Buildings]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">britishlistedbuildings.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">British Listed Buildings Church of St Michael, Whitton, Powys</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">British Listed Buildings Church of St Michael, Whitton, Powys</span></span></span>

Cascob therefore gives the dragon legend one of its most persuasive textures: not proof of a buried monster, but a real place where Christian dedication, possible prehistoric memory, unusual churchyard form and the slopes of Radnor Forest meet. It is easy to see why a storyteller would treat such a site as one of the nails holding down the beast.

### Llanfihangel Discoed

Discoed appears in the five-church version. Visit Mid Wales describes St Michael’s, Discoed as a modest medieval “Shepherd’s” church near Presteigne, on the border between Powys and Herefordshire, and notes its circular site, ancient yew and spring by the churchyard gate as signs that the place has been respected for a very long time.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.visitmidwales.co.uk/things-to-do/st-michaels-church-p1726351" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: visitmidwales.co.uk">[Visit Mid Wales]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">visitmidwales.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Visit Mid Wales St Michaels Church</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Visit Mid Wales St Michaels Church</span></span></span>

Discoed also sharpens the borderland character of the legend. It lies in the Welsh Marches world near Offa’s Dyke, where Welsh and English histories press closely together. Visit Mid Wales describes this section of Offa’s Dyke Path at Discoed as one of the best places to appreciate the form and scale of the early medieval earthwork.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.visitmidwales.co.uk/things-to-do/st-michaels-church-p1726351" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: visitmidwales.co.uk">[Visit Mid Wales]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">visitmidwales.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Visit Mid Wales St Michaels Church</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Visit Mid Wales St Michaels Church</span></span></span>

Including Discoed changes the legend from a compact Radnor Forest ring into a broader Marches pattern. The dragon is still Radnorshire’s, but the protective geography begins to lean towards the old frontier with England. That fits the county’s wider folklore character: local, upland, and repeatedly shaped by crossings.

## Why the landscape matters

Radnor Forest is not a forest in the modern sense alone. Natural Resources Wales explains that it was once a royal hunting ground: an unenclosed area legally set aside for Norman kings to hunt deer. Today the area is hill farming, moorland, steep narrow valleys and wooded sections, rising to Black Mixen, the highest point in Radnorshire at about 2,150 feet or 650 metres.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://naturalresources.wales/days-out/places-to-visit/mid-wales/warren-wood/?lang=en" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: naturalresources.wales">[Natural Resources Wales]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">naturalresources.wales</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on naturalresources.wales.</span></span></span>

That history gives the legend a deeper charge. A medieval “forest” was a jurisdiction as well as a landscape: a place with special rules, boundaries and authority. The dragon legend overlays that legal-hunting geography with spiritual boundaries. The old forest was controlled by law; the legendary forest is controlled by churches.

The terrain also helps. Radnor Forest is a raised, rounded upland block with valleys cut into it. Friends of Radnor Forest describes open access land, public rights of way, old drovers’ roads and a landscape of big skies, ravines and moorland. It also notes Bronze Age round barrows, ring cairns and evidence of prehistoric settlement platforms across the area.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.radnorfforest.co.uk/explore" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: radnorfforest.co.uk">[Friends of Radnor Forest]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">radnorfforest.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Friends of Radnor Forest Explore — Friends of Radnor Forest</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Friends of Radnor Forest Explore — Friends of Radnor Forest</span></span></span>

A sleeping dragon is a natural fit for such a place. Rounded hills can become a buried back. Ravines can become claw marks or old wounds. Church towers on the surrounding lanes can become watch-posts. The story asks the reader to see geography as a body and parish churches as protective pins.

Water-Break-its-Neck adds a further layer. Natural Resources Wales says Warren Wood, near New Radnor, has been popular with tourists for more than 200 years because of the waterfall, which can be spectacular after rain and reduced to a trickle after dry weather.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://naturalresources.wales/days-out/places-to-visit/mid-wales/warren-wood/?lang=en" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: naturalresources.wales">[Natural Resources Wales]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">naturalresources.wales</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on naturalresources.wales.</span></span></span> A landscape that already drew picturesque visitors becomes a natural home for a retold legend: walkers come for the waterfall and woods, then leave with the dragon in mind.

## How old is the legend?

The honest answer is that the Radnor Forest dragon legend is easier to document as a modern public tradition than as an ancient text. Modern websites, local walking material and heritage pages repeat it confidently, but the readily available evidence does not show a single early manuscript source for the full “last dragon under Radnor Forest guarded by St Michael churches” story.

Roy Palmer’s *The Folklore of Radnorshire* is the key modern county collection to know about. Bibliographic records identify it as a Logaston Press volume by Roy Palmer, published in 2001 and later available in a 2007 edition.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://books.google.com/books/about/The_Folklore_of_Radnorshire.html?id=wqb_AAAACAAJ" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: books.google.com">[Google Books]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">books.google.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Books The Folklore of Radnorshire</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Books The Folklore of Radnorshire</span></span></span> Palmer’s work matters because it gathered Radnorshire traditions at county scale, helping preserve the kind of local material that otherwise survives only in parish memory, local history talks, small press trails and scattered oral retellings.

That said, caution is needed. A legend can be old in its ingredients without being provably old in its current form. The Radnor Forest dragon story combines several older elements:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **St Michael as dragon-defeater**, a long-established Christian image.
* **Llanfihangel place-names**, showing repeated local dedications to St Michael.
* **Ancient or medieval church sites**, including circular churchyards, yews, fonts, towers and rebuilt medieval fabric.
* **A dramatic upland landscape**, with prehistoric monuments and a medieval hunting-forest identity.
* **Welsh dragon symbolism**, which gives the sleeping beast national resonance.

</div>

The current “ring of churches holding down the last dragon” may therefore be best understood as a local synthesis. It feels ancient because its pieces are old, but the neat plotted version has become especially visible through modern local heritage, walking and tourism retelling.

## What the legend says about Radnorshire

The Radnor Forest dragon is one of the clearest examples of Radnorshire’s borderland folklore. It is Welsh in its dragon imagery and St Michael place-names, Marcher in its castle-and-church settings, and deeply local in the way it depends on small churches rather than famous national monuments.

It also reflects a county identity built from dispersed places. Radnorshire has no single dominant folklore city or grand mythic centre. Its stories often cling to hills, farms, wells, stones, churches, lanes and lost settlements. The dragon ring makes that scattered geography feel connected. Llanfihangel Rhydithon, Nant Melan, Cefnllys, Cascob and sometimes Discoed become parts of one hidden machine.

The legend is also unusually good at making church preservation feel mythic. A closed, ruined or neglected church is not just a heritage loss in this story. It is a crack in the ring. That does not mean local people literally expect a monster to rise if masonry fails, but it gives a vivid folklore shape to a real rural concern: what happens when small churches, remote communities and old paths fall out of use?

Cefnllys shows this especially well. Its church is tied to a vanished borough and a ruined castle landscape, yet it remains a named Church in Wales site. The dragon story turns that survival into something larger than antiquarian interest. The church is not merely left over from the past; in the legend, it is still doing protective work.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.britainexpress.com/wales/mid/llanfihangel-cefnllys-church.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: britainexpress.com">[Britain Express]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">britainexpress.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on britainexpress.com.</span></span></span>

## How the dragon is retold today

Today the Radnor Forest dragon lives through walks, local websites, church interest, landscape groups and heritage tourism. Mid Wales My Way presents the four-church legend as part of the area’s visitor appeal, linking it with forest trails, wildlife and Water-Breaks-its-Neck.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.midwalesmyway.com/thedragonofradnorforest" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: midwalesmyway.com">[Mid Wales My Way]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">midwalesmyway.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on midwalesmyway.com.</span></span></span> Friends of Radnor Forest folds the legend into a broader account of rights of way, geology, archaeology, vegetation and wildlife, making the dragon one strand in a larger effort to promote and protect the landscape.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.radnorfforest.co.uk/explore" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: radnorfforest.co.uk">[Friends of Radnor Forest]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">radnorfforest.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Friends of Radnor Forest Explore — Friends of Radnor Forest</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Friends of Radnor Forest Explore — Friends of Radnor Forest</span></span></span>

This modern retelling changes the legend’s function. In older-style folklore, the warning might be heard as a supernatural safeguard: do not destroy the churches or the dragon wakes. In present-day public culture, the same story works as a map, a walking theme and a way of noticing the relationship between church sites and hills.

It also invites slow exploration. The legend is not best understood by standing at one viewpoint and asking where the dragon “really” is. It makes more sense as a circuit: Cefnllys for the lost medieval borough, Cascob for the circular churchyard and possible mound, Nant Melan for the A44 approach, Rhydithon for the northern Radnorshire settlement, and Discoed for the five-church Marches version.

The story remains most powerful when kept grounded. There is no need to inflate it into paranormal certainty or claim more ancient proof than the sources provide. Its real strength is that it gives Radnorshire a memorable mythic landscape: a high forest that was never just woodland, a dragon that is dangerous because it is sleeping, and a ring of St Michael churches that turns local geography into a living charm.

    var available = availableMarkets(section);
    if (available.indexOf(marketId)< 0) marketId = available[0] || defaultMarket;
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-localized-link]')).forEach(function (link) {
      var query = link.getAttribute('data-ebay-query') || '';
      var reference = link.getAttribute('data-ebay-reference') || '';
      link.setAttribute('href', buildUrl(query, reference, marketId));
    });
    var select = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-select]');
    if (select && available.indexOf(marketId) >= 0) select.value = marketId;
    var selectedText = marketId;
    if (select && select.selectedIndex >= 0) selectedText = select.options[select.selectedIndex].text;
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-selected-market-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = selectedText;
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-selected-market-flag], [data-ebay-trigger-market-flag]')).forEach(function (flag) {
      flag.className = flagClass(marketId);
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-trigger-market-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = selectedText;
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-domain-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = domainLabel(marketId);
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-option]')).forEach(function (button) {
      button.setAttribute('aria-selected', button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-option') === marketId ? 'true' : 'false');
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      var active = button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button') === marketId;
      button.setAttribute('aria-pressed', active ? 'true' : 'false');
    });
    section.setAttribute('data-ebay-selected-market', marketId);
    if (persist) {
      try { if (window.localStorage) localStorage.setItem('phoenix-ebay-market', marketId); } catch (err) {}
    }
    closeMenu(section);
  }
  sections.forEach(function (section) {
    if (section.getAttribute('data-ebay-localized-init') === '1') return;
    section.setAttribute('data-ebay-localized-init', '1');
    var available = availableMarkets(section);
    var marketId = defaultMarket;
    if (window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation && window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation.inferMarket) {
      marketId = window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation.inferMarket(available, {
        storageKey: 'phoenix-ebay-market',
        defaultMarket: defaultMarket
      });
    } else if (available.indexOf(defaultMarket)< 0) {
      marketId = available[0] || defaultMarket;
    }
    var select = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-select]');
    if (select) {
      select.addEventListener('change', function () {
        applyMarket(section, select.value, true);
      });
    }
    var trigger = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-trigger]');
    var menu = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-menu]');
    if (trigger && menu) {
      trigger.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
        event.stopPropagation();
        var expanded = trigger.getAttribute('aria-expanded') === 'true';
        menu.hidden = expanded;
        trigger.setAttribute('aria-expanded', expanded ? 'false' : 'true');
        var picker = trigger.closest('.fr-ebay-market-picker');
        if (picker) picker.classList.toggle('fr-ebay-market-picker--open', !expanded);
      });
      Array.prototype.slice.call(menu.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-option]')).forEach(function (button) {
        button.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
          event.stopPropagation();
          applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-option'), true);
        });
      });
      document.addEventListener('click', function () { closeMenu(section); });
    }
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      button.addEventListener('click', function () {
        applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button'), true);
      });
    });
    applyMarket(section, marketId, false);
  });
})();
