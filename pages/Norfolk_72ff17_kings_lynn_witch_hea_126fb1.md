## Introduction

King’s Lynn’s Witch’s Heart is a small carved heart inside a diamond-shaped mark on the brickwork above a window on the north side of Tuesday Market Place. Local tradition links it to Margaret Read, also remembered as Shady Meg, who is said to have been burned there for witchcraft in 1590. The famous story says that, as the fire took hold, her heart burst from her body, struck the wall, left the mark still shown to visitors, and then bounded away towards the River Great Ouse. The tale matters because it turns a public square into a visible memory-place: a single brick mark holds together execution history, women accused of witchcraft, civic punishment, and the way [Norfolk]({{ 'why-norfolks-legends-still-haunt-the/' | relative_url }}) folklore keeps attaching itself to exact streets, walls and routes. It is also a good example of a legend that is locally powerful but historically awkward: Margaret Read appears in later records, yet the burning itself sits uneasily beside what is known about English witchcraft law.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://norfolkrecordofficeblog.org/2018/10/31/the-witches-of-lynn/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: norfolkrecordofficeblog.org">[Norfolk Record Office]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">norfolkrecordofficeblog.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Witches of Lynn &#124; Norfolk Record Office...</span></span></span>

## Where Is the Witch’s Heart?

The Witch’s Heart is associated with Tuesday Market Place in King’s Lynn, one of the town’s best-known historic spaces. Modern local heritage trails usually direct readers to look up at numbers 15 and 16 on the north side of the square, where a heart shape is carved within a diamond on the brickwork above a window. Some accounts give number 17 instead, which is a useful warning that the tradition has circulated through guidebooks, blogs, walks and retellings rather than through one perfectly stable antiquarian description.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.norfolk.gov.uk/article/42981/Weird-walk-in-Kings-Lynn" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: norfolk.gov.uk">[norfolk.gov.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">norfolk.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on norfolk.gov.uk.</span></span></span>

That visible setting is central to the legend’s appeal. Norfolk has many supernatural traditions tied to marshes, lanes, churchyards and coast roads, but King’s Lynn’s witch story is unusually urban. It does not ask the reader to imagine a hidden fairy hill or a distant fen track. It says: stand in a public market square, look up at a real wall, and see the place where the old story claims the impossible happened.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.visitwestnorfolk.com/news/strange-stroll-in-kings-lynn/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: visitwestnorfolk.com">[Visit West Norfolk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">visitwestnorfolk.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on visitwestnorfolk.com.</span></span></span>

Tuesday Market Place also gives the legend a plausible civic frame. Historic England describes the market place as a scene of public executions of alleged witches during the 16th century and singles out the Margaret Read story as the best-known King’s Lynn example. Norfolk Record Office likewise notes that the market place had long been used for public execution before giving the local version of the heart bursting from Margaret Read’s body, striking the wall and then travelling towards the river.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://historicengland.org.uk/whats-new/features/halloween/witchcraft-and-witch-trials-in-england/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: historicengland.org.uk">[Historic England]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">historicengland.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Historic England Witchcraft and Witch Trials in England &#124; Historic England</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Historic England Witchcraft and Witch Trials in England &#124; Historic England</span></span></span>

## Margaret Read and Shady Meg

The person at the centre of the tradition is usually named Margaret Read, sometimes Margaret Reed, and in modern retellings she is often given the nickname Shady Meg. The most familiar version says she was convicted of witchcraft and burned in Tuesday Market Place in 1590. At the moment of death, her heart supposedly flew from her body and hit the brickwork opposite, leaving the heart-shaped sign. In one vivid form of the legend, the heart then bounced through the town towards the River Great Ouse and disappeared into the water, making it froth or boil.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://norfolkrecordofficeblog.org/2018/10/31/the-witches-of-lynn/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: norfolkrecordofficeblog.org">[Norfolk Record Office]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">norfolkrecordofficeblog.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Witches of Lynn &#124; Norfolk Record Office...</span></span></span>

There is some record-based footing beneath the name, though not enough to make the whole story straightforward. Norfolk Record Office reports that Margaret Read was an actual person and points to St Margaret’s parish registers, while also stressing that there are two possible candidates. One was baptised on 25 March 1568, which would make her only about 22 in 1590. The other was Margaret Hammond, who married Thomas Read at St Margaret’s on 8 April 1562 and would probably have been nearer 48 or 50 by 1590.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://norfolkrecordofficeblog.org/2018/10/31/the-witches-of-lynn/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: norfolkrecordofficeblog.org">[Norfolk Record Office]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">norfolkrecordofficeblog.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Witches of Lynn &#124; Norfolk Record Office...</span></span></span>

That uncertainty matters. Witchcraft accusations in early modern England often fell on women who were socially vulnerable, disliked, feared, poor, old, quarrelsome in local memory, or simply unlucky enough to be blamed when illness, death or misfortune followed a dispute. But the surviving snippets do not let us confidently reconstruct Margaret Read’s life, household, accusation or trial. The legend remembers the spectacular heart far more clearly than it remembers the legal case.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.parliament.uk/about/living-heritage/transformingsociety/private-lives/religion/overview/witchcraft/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: parliament.uk">[parliament.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">parliament.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on parliament.uk.</span></span></span>

The name Shady Meg seems to belong more to the storytelling tradition than to a securely documented court record. Modern folklore retellings give her a fuller legendary backstory, sometimes placing her in a small house near alleys leading towards the Ouse, giving her inherited powers, or linking her reputation to earlier local suspicion. These details make the tale richer, but they should be read as local legend and modern folklore development rather than as firmly evidenced biography.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.icysedgwick.com/shady-meg-kings-lynn/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: icysedgwick.com">[Icy Sedgwick]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">icysedgwick.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">shady meg kings lynn</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">shady meg kings lynn</span></span></span>

## Tuesday Market Place and the Brick Mark

The most distinctive feature of the legend is not just that a woman was accused of witchcraft. It is that the story leaves a mark. Many witch legends fade into general claims about curses, familiars or trials, but King’s Lynn’s Witch’s Heart has a physical focus that visitors can still seek out. The mark is described as a crude heart within a diamond, carved or impressed into the brickwork above a window.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.norfolk.gov.uk/article/42981/Weird-walk-in-Kings-Lynn" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: norfolk.gov.uk">[Norfolk County Council]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">norfolk.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on norfolk.gov.uk.</span></span></span>

That concreteness helps explain why the story has stayed so visible in local memory. A mark on a wall invites explanation. Once people are told it is a witch’s heart, the object becomes a kind of public riddle: is it a memorial, a scorch mark, a mason’s mark, a later carving, a household emblem, a joke, or a deliberate tourist-era sign? The available sources do not settle the origin of the carving. What they do show is that the mark has become the anchor for the story, regardless of whether it began as evidence, commemoration or coincidence.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.visitwestnorfolk.com/news/two-days-in-kings-lynn-heritage-hanse-and-hauntings/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: visitwestnorfolk.com">[Visit West Norfolk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">visitwestnorfolk.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">two days in kings lynn heritage hanse and hauntings</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">two days in kings lynn heritage hanse and hauntings</span></span></span>

The route in the story also matters. Norfolk Record Office’s version has the heart bouncing towards the river, “presumably via Page Stair Street”, before plunging into the water. That detail makes the tale recognisably Lynn: the market square, the lanes, the river and the old port landscape are all folded into the supernatural action. It is not just a witch legend placed in King’s Lynn; it is a King’s Lynn legend shaped by the town’s own geography.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://norfolkrecordofficeblog.org/2018/10/31/the-witches-of-lynn/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: norfolkrecordofficeblog.org">[Norfolk Record Office]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">norfolkrecordofficeblog.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Witches of Lynn &#124; Norfolk Record Office...</span></span></span>

Modern visitor material keeps that geography alive. West Norfolk tourism material includes the Witch’s Heart among “heritage, Hanse, and hauntings” routes and repeats the version in which Margaret Read’s heart hits the building and rolls or bounces towards the river. Norfolk County Council’s “Weird walk in King’s Lynn” likewise places the Witch’s Heart within a short route around haunted and strange sites in the town.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.visitwestnorfolk.com/news/two-days-in-kings-lynn-heritage-hanse-and-hauntings/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: visitwestnorfolk.com">[Visit West Norfolk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">visitwestnorfolk.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">two days in kings lynn heritage hanse and hauntings</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">two days in kings lynn heritage hanse and hauntings</span></span></span>

## Witchcraft Record, Legend and Local Memory

The historical difficulty is that English witches were normally hanged, not burned. Parliament’s own historical account notes that witchcraft was made a capital crime under Henry VIII in 1542, restored under Elizabeth I in 1562, and later reshaped under James I in 1604; English Heritage’s summary of witchcraft myths states plainly that in England witchcraft was a felony and convicted witches were hanged rather than burned. Burning in England was usually tied to other offences, especially heresy or treason, including petty treason for some women convicted of murdering a husband, master or mistress.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.parliament.uk/about/living-heritage/transformingsociety/private-lives/religion/overview/witchcraft/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: parliament.uk">[parliament.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">parliament.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on parliament.uk.</span></span></span>

That does not automatically prove the Margaret Read story is false, but it does make it historically complicated. A later source noted by folklore writer Willow Winsham gives the entry: “1590 One Margaret Read was this Year Burnt at Lynn for Witchcraft.” Winsham also points out that English burnings for witchcraft are rare and often hard to verify, and that even where witchcraft was involved, burning usually came through another legal category.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://winsham.blogspot.com/2015/05/the-burning-matter-of-english-witches.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: winsham.blogspot.com">[winsham.blogspot.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">winsham.blogspot.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Witch, The Weird, and The Wonderful: The Burning Matter of English Witches</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Witch, The Weird, and The Wonderful: The Burning Matter of English Witches</span></span></span>

This is why the Witch’s Heart should be read as a boundary story: part record, part legal confusion, part place-memory, part moral drama. It may preserve a real execution, a real accused woman, or a real public punishment later reshaped into a witch legend. It may also have absorbed details from other punishments in King’s Lynn, where public execution was a visible civic fact. The most cautious reading is that Margaret Read’s name and the 1590 date have some historical presence, while the flying heart and the precise meaning of the brick mark belong to legend.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://norfolkrecordofficeblog.org/2018/10/31/the-witches-of-lynn/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: norfolkrecordofficeblog.org">[Norfolk Record Office]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">norfolkrecordofficeblog.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Witches of Lynn &#124; Norfolk Record Office...</span></span></span>

There is also a nearby documented witchcraft case that shows King’s Lynn was not merely inventing witch panic after the fact. Mary Smith, wife of Henry Smith, was the subject of Alexander Roberts’s 1616 pamphlet *A Treatise of Witchcraft*. The title page says Roberts was a preacher of God’s word at King’s Lynn and that the account concerned Mary Smith’s alleged witchcrafts, confession, public examinations and execution on 12 January. Wellcome Collection catalogues the 1616 work as a book concerning Mary Smith of King’s Lynn, and Norfolk witchcraft research identifies her as the most sensational local case written up in early 17th-century Norfolk.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/files/17209/17209-h/17209-h.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[gutenberg.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on gutenberg.org.</span></span></span>

Mary Smith’s case is useful because it shows what a better-documented King’s Lynn witchcraft accusation looks like. Roberts’s pamphlet gives allegations about neighbourly quarrels, curses, illness and a demonic pact; later historical discussion treats it as a valuable source for early modern belief, while also recognising that witchcraft pamphlets were shaped by religious argument and popular readership. Margaret Read, by contrast, survives more strongly as a place legend than as a detailed trial narrative.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://keithparry.org/my-writing-2/witchcraft-in-seventeenth-century-norfolk/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: keithparry.org">[The Diary of One Who Disappeared]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">keithparry.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Witchcraft in seventeenth-century Norfolk &#124; The Diary of One Who Disappeared...</span></span></span>

## Why the Witch’s Heart Still Matters in Norfolk Folklore

The Witch’s Heart endures because it does three things at once. First, it gives King’s Lynn a memorable witch legend that is attached to a precise public object. Secondly, it preserves an uneasy memory of early modern punishment and suspicion against women accused of maleficium, or harmful magic. Thirdly, it shows how local folklore can keep a story alive even when the formal record is incomplete, contradictory or legally puzzling.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://norfolkrecordofficeblog.org/2018/10/31/the-witches-of-lynn/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: norfolkrecordofficeblog.org">[Norfolk Record Office]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">norfolkrecordofficeblog.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Witches of Lynn &#124; Norfolk Record Office...</span></span></span>

It also helps distinguish King’s Lynn within Norfolk’s wider supernatural map. Much of the county’s folklore is landscape-led: black dogs on roads and coasts, strange lights over marshes, holy wells, ruined religious sites and fen-edge giants. The Witch’s Heart is different because it is civic folklore. Its landscape is not the lonely heath or the reed bed but the market square, the execution site, the wall and the river route.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.visitwestnorfolk.com/news/strange-stroll-in-kings-lynn/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: visitwestnorfolk.com">[Visit West Norfolk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">visitwestnorfolk.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on visitwestnorfolk.com.</span></span></span>

The story’s modern afterlife is part of its folklore, not a distraction from it. Visitor trails, local-history blogs, county heritage pages and ghost walks have helped keep Margaret Read visible to people who might otherwise pass the mark without noticing it. These retellings sometimes smooth over the legal problems, sometimes highlight them, and sometimes fold the story into a broader haunted-King’s-Lynn experience. That is exactly how modern local legend often works: it turns a hard-to-verify fragment into a shared public memory that can be pointed at, walked to and retold.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.visitwestnorfolk.com/news/two-days-in-kings-lynn-heritage-hanse-and-hauntings/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: visitwestnorfolk.com">[visitwestnorfolk.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">visitwestnorfolk.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">two days in kings lynn heritage hanse and hauntings</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">two days in kings lynn heritage hanse and hauntings</span></span></span>

The responsible way to tell the Witch’s Heart story is therefore neither to debunk it flatly nor to present it as proven supernatural history. The mark is real. The Margaret Read tradition is well established locally. King’s Lynn genuinely belongs within Norfolk’s witchcraft history, especially when read alongside Mary Smith and the later Hopkins-era panic in East Anglia. But the flying heart is legend, and the burning of a witch in England in 1590 is a claim that needs caution. Its power lies in that tension: a small heart on a wall remembers not only one alleged witch, but also the way a town turns fear, punishment and uncertainty into a story that can still be seen from the street.
