## Introduction

The Four Stones near Old Radnor are among [Radnorshire]({{ 'where-radnorshires-old-stories-still/' | relative_url }})’s most memorable folklore sites because the story is simple, local and landscape-shaped: four prehistoric stones are said to leave their field at night and go down to Hindwell Pool to drink. The tale links a Bronze Age stone setting, the bells of Old Radnor church, an ancient font said in tradition to have come from a missing stone, and the wet spring landscape of the Walton Basin. Archaeologically, the stones are a scheduled monument in the historic county of Radnorshire, now within Powys, and are recorded as a four-poster stone setting about 4m by 3m, with stones up to roughly 1.9m high.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archwilio.org.uk/her/chi3/report/page.php?watprn=CPAT1072" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archwilio.org.uk">[Archwilio]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archwilio.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on archwilio.org.uk.</span></span></span>



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/Radnorshire_2d75e1_four_stones_hindwell_0539a0-overview.webp" | relative_url }}" alt="Overview image for Four Stones" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

The folklore matters because it shows how Radnorshire legend often works: not through grand heroic epics, but through small, stubborn places where farming memory, church history and prehistoric remains overlap. Hindwell Pool is not just a convenient pond in the tale. It sits within a basin now recognised as a major prehistoric ceremonial landscape, where springs, watercourses, standing stones, barrows and huge Neolithic timber enclosures give the old “drinking stones” tradition an unusually strong sense of place.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://heneb.org.uk/archive/cpat/wp-content/uploads/2018/12/How-the-West-was-Won-Panel-5.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: heneb.org.uk">[Heneb]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">heneb.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Henebsacred springs? making rings</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Henebsacred springs? making rings</span></span></span>

## The Old Radnor stone setting

The Four Stones stand in the Old Radnor community, near the Kinnerton-Walton road in the Walton Basin. Heneb’s Historic Environment Record identifies the site as a Bronze Age stone setting and a scheduled monument, with a National Grid Reference of SO2458060800. The record describes a “four poster” setting: four large stones arranged in a rough rectangle or small ring, with the stones’ flat faces turned inward.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archwilio.org.uk/her/chi3/report/page.php?watprn=CPAT1072" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archwilio.org.uk">[Archwilio]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archwilio.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on archwilio.org.uk.</span></span></span>

That archaeological description is important for folklore readers because it keeps the story anchored. This is not a vague “ancient stones somewhere in Wales” legend, but a named monument in a particular Radnorshire field. The stones are large enough to have a presence in the landscape, but compact enough for the mind to turn them into a group of characters. The Historic Environment Record gives the setting as about 4m by 3m, and notes that earlier measurements placed the stones roughly between 4ft and 6ft high.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archwilio.org.uk/her/chi3/report/page.php?watprn=CPAT1072" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archwilio.org.uk">[Archwilio]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archwilio.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on archwilio.org.uk.</span></span></span>

The monument has also attracted antiquarian uncertainty. Older writers sometimes treated it as a possible cromlech or burial structure, while later records stressed its regular spacing and four-stone form. Heneb notes that the name “Four Stones” appears in a will of 1652, so the name is not a modern tourist label. The same record also says that Gibson’s later assessment regarded the monument as relatively complete, even though nineteenth-century discussion mentioned the removal of “3 or 4” stones.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archwilio.org.uk/her/chi3/report/page.php?watprn=CPAT1072" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archwilio.org.uk">[Archwilio]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archwilio.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on archwilio.org.uk.</span></span></span>

For Radnorshire folklore, the key point is that the site has two kinds of age. The stones themselves belong to the prehistoric ritual landscape of the Walton Basin. The stories attached to them, as written down, are much later: antiquarian, local-history and twentieth-century folklore records. That does not make the traditions unimportant. It means they are part of the county’s long afterlife of prehistoric places: later communities repeatedly explained old stones by turning them into graves, people, church material or beings with habits of their own.

## Four kings, church fonts and unmown hay

One of the best-attested story clusters around the Four Stones explains them as memorial stones. In a version printed by Ella M. Leather in *Folklore* in 1913, the tradition says that a great battle was fought there, four kings were killed, and the Four Stones were raised over their graves. The note gives “Kington Workhouse, 1908” as the source context, which is useful because it places the tale in the border world around Radnorshire and Herefordshire rather than in a remote literary mythology.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.themodernantiquarian.com/site/106/four-stones" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: themodernantiquarian.com">[The Modern Antiquarian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">themodernantiquarian.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on themodernantiquarian.com.</span></span></span>

This “four kings” explanation is typical of standing-stone folklore across Britain. When later people meet large stones whose original purpose is no longer remembered, they often become graves, petrified dancers, punished sinners, giants’ playthings or the remains of battles. At Old Radnor the number four makes the legend especially neat: four stones, four kings, four graves. The story does not need archaeological proof to be meaningful. Its function is to give a human reason for an otherwise strange prehistoric arrangement.

A second tradition links the stones to Old Radnor church. Heneb’s record says folklore suggests that the font in nearby Old Radnor Church may once have filled a gap in the stone setting, and also records an older view that a similar stone, possibly not originally part of the group, was used for the church font.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archwilio.org.uk/her/chi3/report/page.php?watprn=CPAT1072" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archwilio.org.uk">[Archwilio]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archwilio.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on archwilio.org.uk.</span></span></span> The National Churches Trust describes the font at St Stephen’s as a primitive-looking circular block of stone with a shallow bowl, standing on four squat feet, and says it may date back to the eighth century.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nationalchurchestrust.org/church/st-stephen-old-radnor" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nationalchurchestrust.org">[National Churches Trust]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nationalchurchestrust.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nationalchurchestrust.org.</span></span></span>

The font tradition matters because it gives the Four Stones a Christian afterlife. In local imagination, one stone may have crossed from a prehistoric field into a church, from unknown ritual to baptism. Antiquarian writers did not all accept the claim straightforwardly. A passage quoted from Richard W. Banks’s study of the Four Stones argued that the local tradition was “valuable” as evidence of remote belief, but questioned whether the stone could really have been dressed into a regular font if it were made of the same difficult erratic material as the surviving stones.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.themodernantiquarian.com/site/106/four-stones" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: themodernantiquarian.com">[The Modern Antiquarian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">themodernantiquarian.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on themodernantiquarian.com.</span></span></span>

There is also a farming-memory strand. A note drawing on W. H. Howse’s *Radnorshire* of 1949 says that, at the time of writing, many farmers still felt awe towards the stones: hay was left unmown around them, and some people avoided the place after dark.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.themodernantiquarian.com/site/106/four-stones" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: themodernantiquarian.com">[The Modern Antiquarian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">themodernantiquarian.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on themodernantiquarian.com.</span></span></span> This is one of the most revealing details in the whole tradition. It suggests that the Four Stones were not only an antiquarian curiosity. They still shaped behaviour, even if only in small gestures of caution, respect or habit.

## Hindwell Pool and the walking-stone motif

The most vivid Radnorshire tale says that the Four Stones go to Hindwell Pool to drink. The common version has them moving at night, often at midnight, and sometimes specifically when the bells of Old Radnor church are heard. Britain Express summarises one version in which the stones go down to Hindwell Pool when the church bells can be heard at night.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.britainexpress.com/wales/mid/four-stones.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: britainexpress.com">[Britain Express]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">britainexpress.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on britainexpress.com.</span></span></span> The Modern Antiquarian’s Hindwell Pool page gives the fuller popular form: the stones are reputed to make the half-mile journey to the pool at midnight, sometimes on the full moon, and return before dawn.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.themodernantiquarian.com/site/350/hindwell-pool" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: themodernantiquarian.com">[The Modern Antiquarian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">themodernantiquarian.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on themodernantiquarian.com.</span></span></span>

This is a classic “walking stones” legend. Across Britain and Brittany, standing stones are often said to move briefly at night, especially to drink, dance or answer a sound such as a cockcrow, bell or clock strike. A digitised passage from Walter Johnson’s discussion of old mark-stone traditions gives a useful comparison: it says the Four Stones at New Radnor were reputed to go down to Hindwell Pool to drink when they heard the Old Radnor bells, and compares this with similar tales of stones in Herefordshire, Norfolk, Lowestoft and Brittany.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cantab.net/users/michael.behrend/repubs/dutt_markstones/pages/section_3.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cantab.net">[Alumni]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cantab.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cantab.net.</span></span></span>

The Old Radnor version is especially satisfying because its triggers are local. The stones do not wander randomly. They respond to the church bells and head for a named pool in the same landscape. The story joins three points: field, church and water. It also creates a rule for seeing and not seeing. The stones move at the time when ordinary people should not be abroad, and they are back before daylight can catch them. That makes the absence of visible movement part of the tale rather than a problem for it.

Hindwell Pool adds a further layer. A Clwyd-Powys Archaeological Trust interpretation panel on the Walton Basin argues that the siting of the Neolithic enclosures appears to relate to streams and springs, possibly with religious significance. It explains that water draining from Radnor Forest can disappear into the gravel of the basin and re-emerge near Hindwell Pool, which forms the source of Hindwell Brook. The same panel directly suggests that this natural water behaviour is “no doubt” the origin of the folk tale about the Four Stones going down to the pool at night to drink.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://heneb.org.uk/archive/cpat/wp-content/uploads/2018/12/How-the-West-was-Won-Panel-5.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: heneb.org.uk">[Heneb]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">heneb.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Henebsacred springs? making rings</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Henebsacred springs? making rings</span></span></span>

That explanation should not be treated as disproving the legend. It makes the tradition more interesting. The tale may preserve, in playful form, a local awareness that water behaves strangely in the basin: vanishing, reappearing, gathering at springs, and giving Hindwell Pool a special place in the valley. In folklore terms, the stones “drink” because the land itself seems to drink and release water.

## Why Hindwell Pool is more than scenery

Hindwell Pool sits within one of the most remarkable prehistoric landscapes in Wales. Archaeologist Alex Gibson’s work on the Hindwell palisaded enclosure describes a Later Neolithic monument of exceptional scale, an enclosure made of timber posts rather than a simple earthwork. The article’s abstract calls Hindwell a “megadendric” structure of impressive dimensions and notes that purely palisaded enclosures of such monumentality are exceptional.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.jna.uni-kiel.de/index.php/jna/article/view/72/73" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: jna.uni-kiel.de">[jna.uni-kiel.de]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">jna.uni-kiel.de</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on uni-kiel.de.</span></span></span>

Local interpretation of the Walton Basin has emphasised the same point in more accessible language. Radnor Forest’s community history account describes postholes indicating a vast palisaded area of about 35 hectares, with roughly 1,400 oak posts standing around 18ft high and weighing over 4 tons each, alongside at least one cursus and many standing stones and burial mounds.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.radnorfforest.co.uk/news-views/searching-for-stones" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: radnorfforest.co.uk">[Friends of Radnor Forest]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">radnorfforest.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Friends of Radnor Forest Searching for Stones</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Friends of Radnor Forest Searching for Stones</span></span></span> The Modern Antiquarian’s Hindwell Enclosure page similarly describes Hindwell as the largest palisaded site then found in Britain, giving an oval form of about 800m by 400m and an enclosed area of 34 hectares.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.themodernantiquarian.com/site/3447/hindwell-enclosure" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: themodernantiquarian.com">[The Modern Antiquarian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">themodernantiquarian.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">hindwell enclosure</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">hindwell enclosure</span></span></span>

For the Four Stones page, this background matters only because it changes how the folklore reads. The tale is not just about four stones popping down to a pond. It is attached to a basin where prehistoric people built on a huge scale, where timber, water and stone marked the land long before Old Radnor church existed. The legend is late in its recorded form, but it clings to a genuinely ancient ritual landscape.

There is also a smaller Hindwell Pool tradition involving a witch. The Modern Antiquarian records a version in which the Four Stones are four princes turned to stone by a witch, while the witch herself is said to be entombed in stone at Hindwell Pool.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.themodernantiquarian.com/site/350/hindwell-pool" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: themodernantiquarian.com">[The Modern Antiquarian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">themodernantiquarian.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on themodernantiquarian.com.</span></span></span> This version is less widely evidenced in the sources found than the drinking-stones motif, so it should be handled cautiously. Still, it fits the same pattern: unexplained stones become transformed people, and the pool becomes a place where dangerous power is contained.

## What the evidence can and cannot prove

The Four Stones traditions are well worth retelling, but their evidence is uneven. The archaeological record is strong for the monument’s existence, location, protected status and physical form. Heneb’s record supplies the clearest baseline: a scheduled Bronze Age stone setting, probably a Welsh example of a four-poster, with long antiquarian discussion and a name recorded from the seventeenth century.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archwilio.org.uk/her/chi3/report/page.php?watprn=CPAT1072" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archwilio.org.uk">[Archwilio]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archwilio.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on archwilio.org.uk.</span></span></span>

The folklore evidence is more mixed. The “four kings” tale has a printed early twentieth-century source through Ella M. Leather’s 1913 folklore note.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.themodernantiquarian.com/site/106/four-stones" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: themodernantiquarian.com">[The Modern Antiquarian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">themodernantiquarian.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on themodernantiquarian.com.</span></span></span> The “stones go drinking at Hindwell Pool” story appears in later local-history, antiquarian and modern heritage retellings, and is explicitly linked by at least one comparative folklore passage to Old Radnor bells.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cantab.net/users/michael.behrend/repubs/dutt_markstones/pages/section_3.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cantab.net">[Alumni]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cantab.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cantab.net.</span></span></span> The awe of farmers, unmown hay and avoidance after dark is reported through W. H. Howse’s 1949 account as relayed in later folklore notes.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.themodernantiquarian.com/site/106/four-stones" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: themodernantiquarian.com">[The Modern Antiquarian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">themodernantiquarian.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on themodernantiquarian.com.</span></span></span>

That means the page should not claim an unbroken prehistoric survival of belief. There is no need to imagine Bronze Age people telling the same story about midnight drinking stones. The safer and more interesting reading is that later Radnorshire communities made sense of an ancient monument through familiar traditional motifs: battle graves, petrifaction, church bells, holy or uncanny water, and the power of old stones to move when humans are not watching.

The Old Radnor font tradition is a good example of this careful balance. It may preserve a real memory of stone reuse, or it may be an attractive explanation for a strange early font in a church near a prehistoric landscape. The National Churches Trust supports the font’s great age and unusual form, while Heneb records the local tradition connecting it to the Four Stones but does not present that link as settled fact.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nationalchurchestrust.org/church/st-stephen-old-radnor" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nationalchurchestrust.org">[National Churches Trust]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nationalchurchestrust.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nationalchurchestrust.org.</span></span></span>

## Why this small legend matters in Radnorshire

The Four Stones and Hindwell Pool traditions are valuable because they condense Radnorshire’s borderland folklore into one compact case. The setting is prehistoric, the sound cue is Christian, the route is agricultural, the water is local, and the explanations are human: kings, princes, witches, farmers and churchgoers all enter the story at different moments.

This is also a landscape tale rather than a single fixed plot. A visitor can stand at the Four Stones and understand why the tradition endured: Old Radnor church is part of the wider view, Hindwell Pool lies in the basin, and the surrounding fields are full of archaeological depth. Modern retellings often emphasise the charm of the stones going for a drink, but the older details give the charm weight. The hay left unmown and the avoidance after dark show that the stones once commanded a kind of local respect, not just curiosity.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.themodernantiquarian.com/site/106/four-stones" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: themodernantiquarian.com">[The Modern Antiquarian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">themodernantiquarian.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on themodernantiquarian.com.</span></span></span>

Within a wider Radnorshire folklore map, the Four Stones belong beside the dragon of Radnor Forest, the county’s church-and-landscape traditions, and other standing-stone stories in the Welsh Marches. Their distinctiveness lies in the way they connect stone, water and bell-sound. The stones are not merely ancient objects. In the county’s story-world, they listen, thirst, travel and return before dawn.

    var select = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-select]');
    if (select) {
      select.addEventListener('change', function () {
        applyMarket(section, select.value, true);
      });
    }
    var trigger = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-trigger]');
    var menu = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-menu]');
    if (trigger && menu) {
      trigger.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
        event.stopPropagation();
        var expanded = trigger.getAttribute('aria-expanded') === 'true';
        menu.hidden = expanded;
        trigger.setAttribute('aria-expanded', expanded ? 'false' : 'true');
        var picker = trigger.closest('.fr-ebay-market-picker');
        if (picker) picker.classList.toggle('fr-ebay-market-picker--open', !expanded);
      });
      Array.prototype.slice.call(menu.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-option]')).forEach(function (button) {
        button.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
          event.stopPropagation();
          applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-option'), true);
        });
      });
      document.addEventListener('click', function () { closeMenu(section); });
    }
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      button.addEventListener('click', function () {
        applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button'), true);
      });
    });
    applyMarket(section, marketId, false);
  });
})();
</script>
