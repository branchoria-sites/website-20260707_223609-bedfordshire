## Introduction

[Shetland]({{ 'shetland/' | relative_url }})’s trows are the islands’ hidden hill people: small, uncanny neighbours said to live in mounds, knolls and heathery inland places, coming out after dark to make mischief, play music, borrow or steal from households, and sometimes punish careless humans. They are not simply “fairies” moved north, nor just Scandinavian trolls renamed. The word, the landscape and many of the story settings are distinctly Northern Isles, while the actual tale patterns often overlap with wider Scottish fairy tradition. That mixture is what makes the trow so important to Shetland folklore: it turns familiar places — a mound beside a track, a moor road at night, a fiddle tune heard from underground — into signs of another community living just out of sight. Shetland Museum and Archives still presents the trowie knowe as part of the islands’ customs and folklore, while modern Shetland culture keeps trows alive in storytelling, craft, music and children’s literature.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.shetlandmuseumandarchives.org.uk/visit/galleries/customs-and-folklore" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: shetlandmuseumandarchives.org.uk">[shetlandmuseumandarchives.org.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">shetlandmuseumandarchives.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Shetland Museum &amp; ArchivesCustoms and FolkloreThis zone includes a trowie knowe; an example of where Shetland&#x27;s little people live - ente...</span></span></span>

## What makes a trow different from an ordinary fairy?

A trow is usually described as a supernatural being of Shetland and Orkney: human-like, often smaller or uglier than humans, nocturnal, fond of music and connected with underground dwellings. The Dictionaries of the Scots Language define the word in the Shetland and Orkney setting as a mischievous sprite or fairy, while also recording “trowie” meanings linked with being troll-like, supernatural, superstitious or even made sickly by trow influence. That matters because the trow belongs to living dialect as well as to story: it is a local word for a local kind of fear, explanation and imagination.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.dsl.ac.uk/entry/snd/trow_n1" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: dsl.ac.uk">[dsl.ac.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">dsl.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Dictionaries of the Scots Language:: SND:: trow n1adj.1; (3) trowi(e), -y, adj., (i) pertaining to the trolls, having the appearance of...</span></span></span>

The usual explanation links “trow” with Scandinavian troll tradition, which fits Shetland’s long Norse inheritance. Yet the relationship is not simple. The Institute for Northern Studies at the University of the Highlands and Islands summarises folklorist Alan Bruford’s important argument: although Shetland fairies are usually called trows and the word comes from Norse troll, many of the story types told about them are closer to Scottish fairy legends, including Gaelic parallels, than to purely Scandinavian myth.

So a trow is best understood as a Shetland form of the “hidden people” idea: a being shaped by Norse vocabulary, island dialect, mound lore and Scottish fairy-story patterns. That is why it can feel familiar to readers who know fairy folklore from elsewhere in Britain, yet still unmistakably Shetlandic. Trows live in the same mental world as fairy thefts, changelings, enchanted music and uncanny night travel, but they do so in the language and terrain of Shetland’s peatlands, knolls and old routes.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.shimajournal.org/issues/v4n2/c.-Jennings-Shima-v4n2-1-14.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: shimajournal.org">[shimajournal.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">shimajournal.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Jennings: The Giantess as metaphor for Shetland&#x27;s</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Jennings: The Giantess as metaphor for Shetland&#x27;s</span></span></span>

## Trowie knowes and moorland places

A “trowie knowe” is a trow mound or knoll: the sort of raised, enclosed or suggestive place where stories imagine the hidden folk living. Shetland Museum and Archives includes a trowie knowe in its Customs and Folklore gallery, explaining it as an example of where Shetland’s little people live. The museum also notes a problem common to supernatural folklore: many beliefs left few physical objects, so they are interpreted through reconstructions, artists’ impressions, language and story rather than through relics alone.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.shetlandmuseumandarchives.org.uk/visit/galleries/customs-and-folklore" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: shetlandmuseumandarchives.org.uk">[Shetland Museum &amp; Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">shetlandmuseumandarchives.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Shetland Museum &amp; ArchivesCustoms and FolkloreThis zone includes a trowie knowe; an example of where Shetland&#x27;s little people live - ente...</span></span></span>

Shetland.org places the trows especially in the hills and “heathery peatlands inland from the sea”, a detail that helps distinguish them from Shetland’s sea beings. [Selkies]({{ 'selkies/' | relative_url }}), sea-people and dangerous water-horses belong to shore, skerry and loch traditions; trows belong more naturally to the inland night, the mound, the old path and the uncanny sound from the hill.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.shetland.org/blog/shetland-folklore" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: shetland.org">[Shetland.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">shetland.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">9 Feb 2021 — One of the most common and best-loved tales from Shetland&#x27;s folklore are those about the trows, or little people, who live i...</span></span></span>

The word “knowe” is important because it does not simply mean “a fairy house” in a storybook sense. In Shetland and Orkney tradition, a knowe can be a visible feature in the landscape: a mound that invites explanation. Some trow stories sit close to the archaeological imagination, because burial mounds, ancient sites and standing stones are exactly the kinds of places that later communities often wrapped in legend. Shetland.org notes that many standing stones in the islands were said to be petrified trows, turned to stone after being caught by the rising sun.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.shetland.org/blog/shetland-folklore" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: shetland.org">[Shetland.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">shetland.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">9 Feb 2021 — One of the most common and best-loved tales from Shetland&#x27;s folklore are those about the trows, or little people, who live i...</span></span></span>

A particularly revealing example appears in Jakob Jakobsen’s *Etymological Dictionary of the Norn Language in Shetland*. He records a hill name connected with trolls, where tradition said the sound of a violin was often heard; the same hill was also called “the trowie knowe”, showing how older Norse-derived naming, local landscape and trow music could overlap in one place.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Page%3AAn_Etymological_Dictionary_of_the_Norn_Language_in_Shetland_Part_I.pdf/408" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: en.wikisource.org">[Wikisource]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">en.wikisource.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Page:An Etymological Dictionary of the Norn Language in Shetland Part I</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Page:An Etymological Dictionary of the Norn Language in Shetland Part I</span></span></span>

## Why trows come out at night

Trows are creatures of timing as much as place. They come out after dark, enter human spaces when people are vulnerable, and must return underground before daylight. In popular Shetland summaries, the rule is stark: if a trow is caught by the rising sun, it turns to stone before it can get back to its underground lair.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.shetland.org/blog/shetland-folklore" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: shetland.org">[Shetland.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">shetland.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">9 Feb 2021 — One of the most common and best-loved tales from Shetland&#x27;s folklore are those about the trows, or little people, who live i...</span></span></span>

This daylight rule does several kinds of folklore work at once. It explains stones and strange shapes in the landscape. It makes night travel feel risky without turning every story into a ghost story. It also gives trows a strangely practical weakness: they may be dangerous, but they are bound by time. Humans who understand the rules of the encounter sometimes survive by endurance, trickery or luck.

Many trow stories are built around that pressure. A person meets them on the road, hears them under a mound, finds them in a house, or sees them washing, dancing or carrying something that belongs to humans. The danger is not always open attack. More often it is entanglement: being drawn into their world, losing time, being made ill, having a child or musician taken, or discovering too late that the night’s odd event had a supernatural explanation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://faeryfolklorist.blogspot.com/2015/10/the-trows-of-orkney-and-shetland.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: faeryfolklorist.blogspot.com">[Faery Folklorist]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">faeryfolklorist.blogspot.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">the trows of orkney and shetland</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">the trows of orkney and shetland</span></span></span>

## Music, mischief and night encounters

Trows are among the most musical beings in British folklore. Shetland.org says they particularly loved fiddle music and that much traditional music was thought to have been learned from trows, either by people taken by them or by those who heard music coming from underground knowes.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.shetland.org/blog/shetland-folklore" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: shetland.org">[Shetland.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">shetland.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">9 Feb 2021 — One of the most common and best-loved tales from Shetland&#x27;s folklore are those about the trows, or little people, who live i...</span></span></span>

That link between trows and music is more than decorative. In Shetland, fiddle music is one of the most recognisable forms of local identity, so stories about trow tunes give music a deep, uncanny source. A reel might be imagined not simply as a human composition but as something overheard from another world. The trowie knowe becomes a hidden music room under the hill.

The wider folklore pattern is well known: supernatural beings lure, teach, capture or enchant musicians. But in Shetland the story feels especially place-bound because the sound comes from the knowe, the hill or the moor. A fiddler may not need to see the trows clearly. Hearing the tune is enough to suggest the presence of the hidden people.

Trow mischief is broad rather than random. Stories and summaries associate them with entering houses at night, stealing or borrowing goods, interfering with people and animals, and causing uncanny illness or bad luck. The Scots dictionary record preserves “trow bund” for being bewitched by trolls, and “trowie” can describe someone sickly or ailing under supposed supernatural influence.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.dsl.ac.uk/entry/snd/trow_n1" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: dsl.ac.uk">[dsl.ac.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">dsl.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Dictionaries of the Scots Language:: SND:: trow n1adj.1; (3) trowi(e), -y, adj., (i) pertaining to the trolls, having the appearance of...</span></span></span>

This gives the trow a practical role in older belief culture. It offered a way to explain why a cow failed, why a child sickened, why a tool vanished, why music was heard where no musician stood, or why a person should avoid a certain road after dark. Trow stories are not just entertainment; they are warnings about boundaries — between night and day, home and moor, human dwelling and mound, ordinary music and enchanted sound.

## Who collected and preserved trow lore?

Trow tradition was preserved through oral storytelling long before it was fixed in books. The most useful printed record for Shetland folklore is John Spence’s *Shetland Folk-Lore*, published in 1899. Its Wikisource text describes Spence as having gathered sayings and superstitions from older people for nearly forty years, presenting the book as the first volume specially devoted to the subject and the fullest Shetland folklore collection then available.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Shetland_Folk-Lore" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: en.wikisource.org">[Wikisource]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">en.wikisource.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Shetland Folk-Lore</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Shetland Folk-Lore</span></span></span>

The Folklore Society’s 1903 county folklore volume for Orkney and Shetland also treated trows as a major part of the islands’ traditional material, alongside selkies, witches, Finns, fishing superstitions, medical magic, charms and proverbs. Its survey refers to trow anecdotes and to items such as a bowl stolen from the trows, showing how trow stories sat among everyday belief, household objects and practical magic rather than in a separate world of fantasy.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/stream/countyfolklore03folkuoft/countyfolklore03folkuoft_djvu.txt" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on archive.org.</span></span></span>

Jessie Margaret Edmondston Saxby is another key figure in the preservation and shaping of Shetland tradition. Born in Unst, she became one of Shetland’s best-known writers and later published *Shetland Traditional Lore* in 1932. Modern literary resources identify her as a prolific Shetland writer with deep local roots, while library records preserve her folklore volume as part of the islands’ written heritage.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://discovered.ed.ac.uk/discovery/fulldisplay?context=L&amp;docid=alma994921193502466&amp;lang=en&amp;query=sub%2Cexact%2C+Fairies&amp;tab=Everything&amp;vid=44UOE_INST%3A44UOE_VU2" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: discovered.ed.ac.uk">[ed.ac.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">discovered.ed.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on ed.ac.uk.</span></span></span>

These collectors matter because trow lore is not a single authorised mythology. It is a mass of local tales, dialect words, family memories, place associations and printed retellings. The nineteenth- and early twentieth-century books did not invent the trow, but they did shape how later readers encounter it: as a distinctive Shetland being with enough consistency to be recognisable, yet enough local variation to remain slippery.

## How trows link Shetland and Orkney

Trows are shared by Shetland and Orkney, which is why they are often described as Northern Isles beings rather than exclusively Shetlandic ones. Both island groups have Norse history, mound traditions, strong local dialects and stories in which hidden people live underground, come out at night and interact uneasily with humans.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trow_%28folklore%29" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Trow (folklore</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Trow (folklore</span></span></span>

The link is not just a matter of one island borrowing from another. Orkney and Shetland sit in a wider North Atlantic story zone where Norse vocabulary, Scottish fairy legends, maritime movement and local place traditions overlap. That is why trows can be compared with Scandinavian trolls, Scottish fairies and hidden folk traditions without being reduced to any one of them.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.shimajournal.org/issues/v4n2/c.-Jennings-Shima-v4n2-1-14.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: shimajournal.org">[shimajournal.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">shimajournal.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Jennings: The Giantess as metaphor for Shetland&#x27;s</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Jennings: The Giantess as metaphor for Shetland&#x27;s</span></span></span>

There are differences in emphasis. Orkney writing often stresses howes and ancient mounds; Shetland accounts commonly bring out heathery peatland, fiddle music and the trowie knowe. The same creature type therefore adapts to local geography. In a Shetland page, the centre of gravity should remain on how trows make sense of Shetland places: inland moor, night road, mound, fiddle tune and dialect expression.

The Shetland-Orkney link also helps explain why “trow” is a useful bridge term for this folklore project. It connects Shetland to neighbouring Northern Isles tradition without making Shetland folklore seem like a branch of mainland Scottish fairy lore. It also gives a natural route to related pages on Orkney trows, selkies, sea-trows, mound legends, fiddle lore and Norse-influenced place-names.

## Old belief, literary retelling and modern trow culture

Trows still appear in Shetland culture, but not always in the same way they did in older belief. In oral tradition, trows could explain fear, illness, luck, theft, music and dangerous encounters. In museum interpretation, they help visitors understand a world of custom and supernatural belief that left few physical objects. In modern writing, tourism and craft, they become symbols of Shetland imagination and identity.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.shetlandmuseumandarchives.org.uk/visit/galleries/customs-and-folklore" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: shetlandmuseumandarchives.org.uk">[Shetland Museum &amp; Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">shetlandmuseumandarchives.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Shetland Museum &amp; ArchivesCustoms and FolkloreThis zone includes a trowie knowe; an example of where Shetland&#x27;s little people live - ente...</span></span></span>

Modern retellings often make the trow more charming than frightening. A visitor may meet trows through a blog, a children’s book, a craft name, a museum display or a seasonal folklore video rather than through a warning from an elder about a particular knowe. Shetland Islands Council’s library pages, for example, note that Valerie Watt writes children’s books in dialect and takes inspiration from Shetland’s trowie tales.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.shetland.gov.uk/libraries/valerie-watt" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: shetland.gov.uk">[Shetland Islands Council]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">shetland.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on shetland.gov.uk.</span></span></span>

That shift does not make the tradition fake. It shows how folklore changes use. A being that once helped explain the dangers of night travel or the anxiety of hidden illness can later become a way to celebrate dialect, landscape and local distinctiveness. The trow’s modern friendliness often sits on top of an older unease.

There is also a useful caution here. Because trows are attractive to modern fantasy culture, they can easily be flattened into generic “dark elves” or “troll-fairies”. The older Shetland material is more interesting than that. Trows are not just a species in a monster catalogue. They are a story mechanism: they make the mound inhabited, the night road risky, the fiddle tune uncanny, the missing object suspicious and the Shetland landscape socially alive.

## Why trows matter to Shetland identity

Trows matter because they carry several layers of Shetland identity at once. They preserve a Norse-derived word in a Scottish island setting. They attach supernatural belief to peatland, hill and knowe rather than to castles or courtly romance. They link the islands’ famous musical culture to the idea of hidden, underground musicians. They also show how Shetland folklore can be both local and connected: part of wider fairy tradition, yet unmistakably shaped by Northern Isles history and dialect.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.dsl.ac.uk/entry/snd/trow_n1" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: dsl.ac.uk">[dsl.ac.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">dsl.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Dictionaries of the Scots Language:: SND:: trow n1adj.1; (3) trowi(e), -y, adj., (i) pertaining to the trolls, having the appearance of...</span></span></span>

For a reader walking through Shetland folklore for the first time, the trow is the key inland being. The selkie draws the eye to the shore; the njuggle to dangerous water; witches to human fear and accusation; winter guizers to custom and performance. The trow draws attention to the hill, the mound, the track, the fiddle and the unseen neighbour.

That is why the trowie knowe remains such a powerful image. It is small enough to be local — a particular mound, a particular sound, a particular night story — but large enough to hold Shetland’s mixed inheritance: Norse memory, Scottish legend, dialect speech, moorland geography, music, mischief and the old feeling that the islands are never inhabited by humans alone.
