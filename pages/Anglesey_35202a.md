## Introduction

For this UK historic-county project, Anglesey is treated as the historic county of Anglesey, centred on the island also known as Ynys Môn. The modern Isle of Anglesey council area broadly keeps the island identity intact, so the historic and modern geographies are less confusing here than in many counties, though the wider folklore setting naturally crosses the Menai Strait into Gwynedd, the Llŷn Peninsula, Ireland and the Irish Sea. The Wikishire and Gazetteer entries identify Anglesey as both island and historic county, while Welsh historic-county datasets record Anglesey as one of Wales’s thirteen historic counties before the 1974 local-government changes.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://gazetteer.org.uk/place/Anglesey%2C_Anglesey_65792" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gazetteer.org.uk">[gazetteer.org.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gazetteer.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Anglesey, Anglesey 65792</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Anglesey, Anglesey 65792</span></span></span>

## Why Anglesey feels so folkloric

Anglesey’s folklore has unusual force because the island is both physically distinct and historically central. It is separated from mainland Wales by the Menai Strait, faces Ireland across the western sea, and has long been imagined as a threshold place: a land apart, but not isolated. That helps explain why its legends so often involve crossings: Roman soldiers crossing to attack Mona, saints walking from coast to coast, Branwen’s marriage linking Wales and Ireland, pilgrims reaching Llanddwyn Island, and strangers arriving by sea in the Llanddona witch story.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_conquest_of_Anglesey" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[wikipedia.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Roman conquest of Anglesey</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Roman conquest of Anglesey</span></span></span>

The island is also unusually rich in visible ancient remains. Cadw lists major Anglesey sites such as Bryn Celli Ddu, Barclodiad y Gawres, Bodowyr, Trefignath and Penrhosfeilw among its prehistoric places to visit, while Gwynedd Archaeological Trust material describes the island’s standing stones as striking figures in the landscape that still shape routes for walkers and cyclists. Such monuments are not “proof” of later legends, but they gave storytellers memorable anchors: an ancient tomb could become a giantess’s dropped apronful, a stone chamber could become a place of solar mystery, and an isolated well could gather healing, wishing or saintly traditions around it.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cadw.gov.wales/visit/places-to-visit/find-a-place-to-visit" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cadw.gov.wales">[gov.wales]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cadw.gov.wales</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on gov.wales.</span></span></span>

This is the key to Anglesey folklore: old stones, sacred water, sea roads and remembered power. Some traditions are medieval literary inheritance; some are nineteenth- and twentieth-century retellings of oral lore; some are modern heritage interpretation. They are not all the same kind of evidence, but together they explain why Anglesey still feels like one of Wales’s most story-charged counties.

## Druids, Mona and the Roman memory

No Anglesey folklore page can avoid the Roman image of Mona, the island associated with Druids. The main ancient source is Tacitus, who describes the Roman governor Suetonius Paulinus attacking Mona in AD 60 or 61, meeting armed defenders, women in black with torches and Druids calling down curses, before Roman troops destroyed sacred groves. Tacitus is a hostile Roman writer, not a neutral local witness, but his account became enormously influential in later ideas of Anglesey as a last stronghold of British Druidic religion.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_conquest_of_Anglesey" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[wikipedia.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Roman conquest of Anglesey</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Roman conquest of Anglesey</span></span></span>

Modern readers should handle this tradition carefully. Tacitus gives us a dramatic written account, but archaeology has not identified a specific “Druid grove” from his story. What archaeology does show is that Anglesey had important late prehistoric ritual activity. The most famous example is Llyn Cerrig Bach, where objects including weapons, horse gear, tools and decorated metalwork were deposited in a lake or wetland over the last centuries of the Iron Age. Amgueddfa Cymru notes that Llyn Cerrig Bach has encouraged some people to connect Anglesey’s archaeology with Tacitus’s [Druidic Mona]({{ 'druidic-mona/' | relative_url }}), but that connection is an inference rather than a direct match.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://museum.wales/blog/1343/Ancient-druids-of-Wales/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: museum.wales">[Museum Wales]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">museum.wales</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Ancient druids of Wales</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Ancient druids of Wales</span></span></span>

That distinction matters because it keeps the story powerful without making it misleading. Anglesey was almost certainly important in late Iron Age religious life; watery places such as Llyn Cerrig Bach were treated as meaningful locations for offerings; and Roman authors framed Mona as a dangerous religious and political centre. What cannot be proved is the exact relationship between the objects in the lake, any named Druids, and Tacitus’s battle scene. The folklore value lies partly in that tension: Anglesey’s “Druid island” identity is built from a rare meeting of classical text, impressive archaeology and later Welsh cultural imagination.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://museum.wales/news/1353/Religious-Spring-Find-from-Anglesey-Declared-Treasure/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: museum.wales">[Museum Wales]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">museum.wales</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Religious Spring Find from Anglesey Declared Treasure</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Religious Spring Find from Anglesey Declared Treasure</span></span></span>

## Giant stones and prehistoric tombs

### Barclodiad y Gawres: the giantess’s apronful

Barclodiad y Gawres, on the south-west coast near Porth Trecastell, has one of Anglesey’s clearest links between archaeology and legend. Cadw translates the name as “The Giantess’s Apronful” and describes the site as a reconstructed Neolithic chambered tomb with a passage leading to a cross-shaped chamber and rare prehistoric art. Local retellings explain the name through a giantess carrying stones in her apron; when she drops them, the mound is formed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cadw.gov.wales/visit/places-to-visit/barclodiad-y-gawres-chambered-tomb" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cadw.gov.wales">[gov.wales]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cadw.gov.wales</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Cadw Barclodiad y Gawres Chambered Tomb &#124; Cadw</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cadw Barclodiad y Gawres Chambered Tomb &#124; Cadw</span></span></span>

The legend is not an archaeological explanation of how the tomb was built. It is a folk explanation for a striking monument whose true builders were Neolithic communities thousands of years older than the story. That makes it especially useful for readers: the tale shows how later people made ancient architecture legible. “A giantess dropped it” is not a scientific account, but it is a memorable way of saying that the monument looked too large, too old and too strange to belong to ordinary human time.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cadw.gov.wales/visit/places-to-visit/barclodiad-y-gawres-chambered-tomb" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cadw.gov.wales">[Cadw]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cadw.gov.wales</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Cadw Barclodiad y Gawres Chambered Tomb &#124; Cadw</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cadw Barclodiad y Gawres Chambered Tomb &#124; Cadw</span></span></span>

Barclodiad y Gawres also links Anglesey to the Irish Sea world. Cadw’s education material notes that Anglesey has passage tombs such as Bryn Celli Ddu and Barclodiad y Gawres, a style corresponding with Irish examples. Visit Wales similarly describes Barclodiad y Gawres as an unusual decorated cruciform passage grave on Anglesey, with parallels more commonly found in Ireland. That cross-sea context is important: Anglesey’s folklore often looks both inward to Welsh identity and outward to Ireland.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cadw.gov.wales/sites/default/files/2019-05/151214-neolithicresource-eng.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cadw.gov.wales">[Cadw]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cadw.gov.wales</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Cadw Neolithic Burial Chambers Education Resource Pack</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cadw Neolithic Burial Chambers Education Resource Pack</span></span></span>

### Bryn Celli Ddu: the tomb where sunlight still matters

Bryn Celli Ddu is one of Anglesey’s most famous prehistoric places because it is both physically visitable and cosmically suggestive. Cadw calls it an “iconic Neolithic tomb with a celestial secret”, while the Royal Commission on the Ancient and Historical Monuments of Wales describes it as a late Neolithic passage grave built around 3000 BC, excavated and partly restored in the 1920s. The name is commonly rendered in English as “mound in the dark grove”.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cadw.gov.wales/visit/places-to-visit/bryn-celli-ddu-chambered-tomb" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cadw.gov.wales">[Cadw]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cadw.gov.wales</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Cadw Bryn Celli Ddu Chambered Tomb</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cadw Bryn Celli Ddu Chambered Tomb</span></span></span>

Its best-known feature is the midsummer sunrise alignment. Cadw’s Wales history map says Bryn Celli Ddu is the only tomb on Anglesey accurately aligned so that the rising sun on the longest day sends light down the passage into the chamber. This is not folklore in the same sense as a witch story or fairy tale, but it has become part of the island’s living mythic landscape. Modern visitors, photographers, archaeologists, pagans and local storytellers all approach the tomb as a place where ancient architecture and seasonal meaning meet.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cadw.gov.wales/visit/best-history/wales-history-map" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cadw.gov.wales">[gov.wales]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cadw.gov.wales</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on gov.wales.</span></span></span>

The folklore lesson is subtle. Bryn Celli Ddu does not need an invented ghost to be mysterious. Its interest comes from the way a real Neolithic monument still stages a moment of wonder: darkness, passage, chamber and sunlight. That makes it a natural internal-link point for pages on standing stones, solstice customs, prehistoric tomb legends and modern ritual at ancient sites.

## Saints, wells and pilgrimage

Anglesey’s holy wells belong to a different layer of folklore: Christian, local, healing and often practical. These stories are not simply about doctrine. They are about walking, water, cure, courtship, friendship and place.

St Dwynwen’s tradition is the best known outside the island. Llanddwyn Island, off the south-west coast, is associated with Dwynwen, widely treated as the Welsh patron saint of lovers. The National Churches Trust records that pilgrimages were made to her holy well and that the faithfulness of a lover was said to be divined through the movement of eels in the water: breadcrumbs and a handkerchief were used, and the eel’s disturbance of the cloth signalled fidelity.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nationalchurchestrust.org/church/st-dwynwen-llanddwyn-island" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nationalchurchestrust.org">[National Churches Trust]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nationalchurchestrust.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nationalchurchestrust.org.</span></span></span>

That story explains why Dwynwen’s tradition still travels so easily into modern culture. It has a clear emotional question — “Is my lover faithful?” — and a vivid ritual answer involving water, animals and a saintly place. It also shows the blend typical of Welsh well lore: Christian saint, folk divination and local landscape all operating together. The point is not whether the eel “really” foretold love, but that generations of visitors understood the well as a place where private longing could be acted out through a recognised custom.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nationalchurchestrust.org/church/st-dwynwen-llanddwyn-island" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nationalchurchestrust.org">[National Churches Trust]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nationalchurchestrust.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nationalchurchestrust.org.</span></span></span>

At the eastern end of the island, Penmon is tied to St Seiriol. Cadw says Penmon’s history reaches back to the sixth century, when a monastery was established by Seiriol, and that the holy well bearing his name was reputed to have healing properties, although the present well-house is later. This is a good example of how folklore and heritage overlap: visitors see medieval remains, a holy well, a saint’s memory and a coastal view towards Puffin Island, all in one place.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cadw.gov.wales/visit/places-to-visit/penmon-priory-cross-and-st-seiriols-well" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cadw.gov.wales">[Cadw]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cadw.gov.wales</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Cadw Penmon Priory, Cross and St Seiriol&#x27;s Well &#124; Cadw</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cadw Penmon Priory, Cross and St Seiriol&#x27;s Well &#124; Cadw</span></span></span>

The friendship of Seiriol and Cybi gives Anglesey one of its most appealing saintly legends. Modern pilgrimage material for the Anglesey Saints Way tells how Cybi, associated with Holyhead in the west, and Seiriol, associated with Penmon in the east, walked daily to meet near the middle of the island. Because of the directions they faced while walking, Cybi became “the Tanned” and Seiriol “the Fair”. The contemporary route turns that legend into a 57-mile coast-to-coast pilgrimage across churches, wells and sacred places.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.britishpilgrimage.org/portfolio/anglesey-saints-way-seiriol-cybi" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: britishpilgrimage.org">[British Pilgrimage Trust]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">britishpilgrimage.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on britishpilgrimage.org.</span></span></span>

## Branwen, Aberffraw and the Mabinogion

Anglesey’s most important medieval literary connection is Branwen, the Second Branch of the Mabinogi. Cadw’s educational material places the royal wedding of Branwen and Matholwch at Aberffraw on Anglesey and says Branwen was later buried at Bedd Branwen in the heart of Anglesey after dying of grief. This is a legendary narrative, not a historical chronicle, but it gives Anglesey a major role in one of Wales’s foundational story cycles.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cadw.gov.wales/sites/default/files/2019-05/Branwen_EN.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cadw.gov.wales">[Cadw]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cadw.gov.wales</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on gov.wales.</span></span></span>

The Mabinogion itself is not a single “folklore book” in the simple modern sense. Wales.com describes it as an anthology of early Welsh prose tales whose stories preserve medieval literary tradition, mythic themes and named Welsh landscapes. The tales survive through medieval manuscript culture but draw on older narrative material, courtly storytelling and oral tradition. For Anglesey readers, the point is that Aberffraw is not merely a pretty coastal village: in legendary geography it becomes a royal stage where a marriage between Britain and Ireland begins a tragic chain of insult, war and loss.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.wales.com/about/history-and-heritage/welsh-traditions-myths-and-legends/timeless-magic-mabinogion" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wales.com">[Wales]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wales.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The timeless magic of The Mabinogion</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The timeless magic of The Mabinogion</span></span></span>

Bedd Branwen is especially important because it shows how story attaches to a monument or place-name. “Branwen’s grave” matters whether approached as legend, literature, local identity or heritage tourism. It gives the island a grief-marked female figure whose story crosses the Irish Sea, connecting Anglesey’s folklore with wider Welsh and Irish narrative worlds.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cadw.gov.wales/sites/default/files/2019-05/Branwen_EN.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cadw.gov.wales">[Cadw]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cadw.gov.wales</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on gov.wales.</span></span></span>

## The witches of Llanddona

The Llanddona witches are among Anglesey’s most memorable local legends because they combine fear of outsiders, coastal arrival, village conflict and named magical women. In the version preserved in The Welsh Fairy Book, a rudderless, oarless boat comes ashore at Red Wharf Bay carrying hungry strangers. Local people suspect criminals and try to push the boat back to sea, but the strangers eventually settle near Llanddona and become feared as witches. The story includes named figures such as Bella Fawr and Goronwy Tudor, giving it the texture of local anecdote rather than remote myth.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://sacred-texts.com/neu/celt/wfb/wfb54.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sacred-texts.com">[Internet Sacred Text Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sacred-texts.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on sacred-texts.com.</span></span></span>

This is a tradition to read socially as well as supernaturally. The tale imagines witchcraft as something brought by sea, associated with people who sound different, arrive outside ordinary community structures and then gain power through fear. That does not mean there was a real coven terrorising the island in the way the legend describes. It means the story preserves anxieties about poverty, migration, reputation, curses, illness and social control. The People’s Collection Wales also presents the Llanddona witches as a local legend linked to shipwrecked arrivals in the seventeenth century, showing its continued importance in public-facing Welsh heritage.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.peoplescollection.wales/items/2027371" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: peoplescollection.wales">[People&#x27;s Collection Wales]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">peoplescollection.wales</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on peoplescollection.wales.</span></span></span>

The modern life of the legend is also active. In 2026, Nation.Cymru reported an aerial performance bringing the Llanddona witches story to life at a Welsh castle, describing it as a seventeenth-century legend about exiled, Irish-accented strangers arriving in a rudderless boat on Anglesey. This kind of adaptation is not “old folklore” in itself, but it shows how old local material keeps being reworked for performance, tourism and Welsh cultural storytelling.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://nation.cymru/culture/aerial-performance-brings-llanddona-witches-legend-to-life-at-welsh-castle/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nation.cymru">[Nation.Cymru]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nation.cymru</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nation.cymru.</span></span></span>

## Fairies, black dogs and what is less Anglesey-specific

Anglesey belongs firmly within Welsh fairy tradition, but the evidence is stronger for general Welsh fairy belief than for a single famous Anglesey fairy site on the level of Llanddona or Llanddwyn. Welsh fairy lore commonly refers to the fair folk, underground or watery dwellings, dancing, changelings, fairy music, gifts that vanish when spoken of, and taboos around iron. Wirt Sikes’s nineteenth-century British Goblins and John Rhys’s later Celtic Folklore helped preserve and interpret such Welsh material, though both must be read as collectors and interpreters of their time rather than as transparent windows onto a timeless past.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://publicdomainreview.org/collection/british-goblins-welsh-folk-lore-fairy-mythology-legends-and-traditions-1880/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: publicdomainreview.org">[publicdomainreview.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">publicdomainreview.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">british goblins welsh folk lore fairy mythology legends and traditions 1880</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">british goblins welsh folk lore fairy mythology legends and traditions 1880</span></span></span>

For an Anglesey page, the useful approach is to treat fairy traditions as part of the island’s wider Welsh context, especially where they attach to mounds, water, music, hidden dwellings or liminal routes. Anglesey’s prehistoric tombs and wells make it easy for modern retellings to sound fairy-haunted, but responsible writing should not invent a specific “fairy hill” tradition where the evidence is only general. This is where a county folklore project should distinguish between local attestation and national motif.

The same caution applies to black dogs. Spectral black dogs are a major British folklore motif, and Welsh tradition has its own dark hound figure often called the “Dog of Darkness”. But Anglesey’s strongest attested material in the sources found here is not a famous county black-dog legend comparable to East Anglia’s Black Shuck or Yorkshire’s Barghest. A good Anglesey page can cross-link to Welsh black-dog traditions, but should not make them central unless a specific local source is identified.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_dog_%28folklore%29" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Black dog (folklore</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Black dog (folklore</span></span></span>

## How old are these traditions?

Anglesey’s folklore is not one age. It is a stack of traditions preserved through different kinds of evidence.

The oldest layer is prehistoric landscape, visible in tombs, standing stones and ritual deposits. Bryn Celli Ddu and Barclodiad y Gawres are Neolithic monuments, while Llyn Cerrig Bach belongs to late Iron Age religious deposition. These are archaeological facts, but the stories attached to them — giants, Druids, solstice meanings, ritual imaginings — have been interpreted and reinterpreted long after the original builders and worshippers were gone.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://rcahmw.gov.uk/angleseys-neolithic-tomb-with-a-solar-secret-here-comes-the-sun/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: rcahmw.gov.uk">[rcahmw.gov.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">rcahmw.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">angleseys neolithic tomb with a solar secret here comes the sun</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">angleseys neolithic tomb with a solar secret here comes the sun</span></span></span>

The classical layer comes from Roman writing, especially Tacitus. His account of Mona’s Druids is early and influential, but it is external, hostile and literary. It tells us how a Roman elite author framed the island and its people; it does not give us a local Anglesey oral tradition in its own voice.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_conquest_of_Anglesey" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Roman conquest of Anglesey</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Roman conquest of Anglesey</span></span></span>

The medieval layer includes the Mabinogi, where Anglesey appears in legendary geography through Branwen, Aberffraw and Bedd Branwen. These tales are preserved in medieval manuscript culture and are central to Welsh literary identity, but they are not straightforward historical records.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cadw.gov.wales/sites/default/files/2019-05/Branwen_EN.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cadw.gov.wales">[gov.wales]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cadw.gov.wales</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on gov.wales.</span></span></span>

The early Christian and medieval ecclesiastical layer includes saints, wells and pilgrimage sites: Dwynwen at Llanddwyn, Seiriol at Penmon and Cybi at Holyhead. These stories blend religious memory, hagiography, local devotion and later popular custom.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nationalchurchestrust.org/church/st-dwynwen-llanddwyn-island" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nationalchurchestrust.org">[nationalchurchestrust.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nationalchurchestrust.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nationalchurchestrust.org.</span></span></span>

The antiquarian and modern heritage layer includes collected witch tales, guidebook legends, museum interpretation, walking routes and performances. These are often the versions most readers meet first, and they matter because they keep the stories visible. But they should be labelled honestly as retellings, not treated as unchanged survivals from remote antiquity.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://sacred-texts.com/neu/celt/wfb/wfb54.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sacred-texts.com">[sacred-texts.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sacred-texts.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on sacred-texts.com.</span></span></span>

## Why Anglesey’s folklore still matters

Anglesey folklore matters because it gives the island a cultural map as well as a physical one. A visitor can cross the Menai Strait and see beaches, fields, villages and ruins; the stories explain why particular places feel charged. Llanddwyn becomes a place of love and divination, Penmon a place of healing and saintly retreat, Aberffraw a royal stage of Welsh legend, Llanddona a village of witch memory, Bryn Celli Ddu a meeting point of ancient architecture and sunlight, and Barclodiad y Gawres a tomb whose name still carries a giantess.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nationalchurchestrust.org/church/st-dwynwen-llanddwyn-island" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nationalchurchestrust.org">[nationalchurchestrust.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nationalchurchestrust.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nationalchurchestrust.org.</span></span></span>

It also matters because Anglesey’s traditions reveal how Welsh identity is made from crossings rather than isolation. The island’s stories point to Ireland, Rome, Gwynedd, the Christian saints, the prehistoric Atlantic world and modern Wales. Even the most local legends often contain wider patterns: giant builders, sacred wells, women blamed as witches, lovers seeking omens, saints walking boundaries, and ancient monuments reimagined by later communities.

The most grounded way to enjoy Anglesey folklore is therefore neither sceptical dismissal nor breathless belief. It is to ask what each story is doing in its place. Some preserve memory; some explain a strange monument; some warn against outsiders or arrogance; some turn grief into landscape; some turn water into hope. Together they make Anglesey one of the clearest examples in Wales of a county where folklore is not separate from history and geography, but woven through the very routes by which people move across the island.
