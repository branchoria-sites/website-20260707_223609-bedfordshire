## Introduction

[Montgomeryshire]({{ 'where-montgomeryshires-old-stories-still/' | relative_url }})’s strange stories did not survive by accident. They were kept alive because local antiquaries, clergy, editors and society members decided that parish memory, old words, ghost stories, charms, fairy lanes and place-name lore deserved the same care as charters, pedigrees and ruined abbeys. The Powysland Club, founded in 1867 as the Montgomeryshire historical society, became one of the county’s main engines of preservation through its journal, *Montgomeryshire Collections*, its library in Welshpool, and its habit of treating local evidence as worth recording before it disappeared.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.powyslandclub.co.uk/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: powyslandclub.co.uk">[powyslandclub.co.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">powyslandclub.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on powyslandclub.co.uk.</span></span></span>



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/Montgomeryshire_1814c4_powysland_folklore_c_857c1e-overview.webp" | relative_url }}" alt="Overview image for Collectors" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

For folklore readers, the Club matters because it turned scattered oral memory into searchable county lore. Through figures such as Morris Charles Jones and the Revd Elias Owen, Montgomeryshire’s supernatural and customary traditions were printed alongside parish histories, archaeology, maps, records and family papers. That setting shaped how later readers encountered the county’s legends: not as isolated campfire tales, but as part of a wider historic landscape of Trefeglwys, Llanidloes, Llanymynech Hill, Dylife, Welshpool and the border country of old Powys.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://biography.wales/article/s-JONE-CHA-1819" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: biography.wales">[biography.wales]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">biography.wales</span><span class="citation-popover-title">s JONE CHA 1819</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">s JONE CHA 1819</span></span></span>

## Why the Powysland Club mattered for county lore

The Powysland Club describes itself as the historical society for Montgomeryshire and the senior county historical society in Wales. Its present work still includes lectures, excursions, an annual journal and a library in Welshpool, but its importance for folklore began with a Victorian belief that a county’s identity could be preserved through collecting, printing and local scholarship.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.powyslandclub.co.uk/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: powyslandclub.co.uk">[powyslandclub.co.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">powyslandclub.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on powyslandclub.co.uk.</span></span></span>

That may sound dry, yet it is exactly the sort of machinery folklore needs. Oral tales are fragile. A ghost story told by a shepherd, a fairy story attached to a lane, or a cure remembered by an elderly parishioner can vanish when the speaker dies or when a place-name drops out of use. A county society gives such material a second life: it fixes names, locations and informants in print, and places them within a local archive where later writers, museums, family historians and curious readers can find them again.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/stream/collectionshist08unkngoog/collectionshist08unkngoog_djvu.txt" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on archive.org.</span></span></span>

The Club’s early ambitions were not limited to folklore. Its journal covered ecclesiastical history, genealogy, archaeology, parish histories, roads, bridges, old manuscripts, natural history and records. That breadth is part of the point. In Montgomeryshire, folklore survived not because it was separated from history, but because it was gathered into the same county habit of attention. The same pages that recorded Offa’s Dyke, parish registers, manorial evidence and ruined religious houses also made room for “Montgomeryshire Folk-lore”.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.powyslandclub.co.uk/montgomeryshire-collections/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: powyslandclub.co.uk">[powyslandclub.co.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">powyslandclub.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Montgomeryshire Collections – Powysland Club:Clwb Powysland</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Montgomeryshire Collections – Powysland Club:Clwb Powysland</span></span></span>

The founder figure behind this culture was Morris Charles Jones, a Welshpool-born antiquary and solicitor. The Dictionary of Welsh Biography records that he circulated proposals for the establishment of the Powysland Club in 1867, became its founder and secretary, contributed for many years to *Montgomeryshire Collections*, and helped found the Powysland Museum at Welshpool.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://biography.wales/article/s-JONE-CHA-1819" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: biography.wales">[Dictionary of Welsh Biography]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">biography.wales</span><span class="citation-popover-title">s JONE CHA 1819</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">s JONE CHA 1819</span></span></span>

## The Powysland Club and *Montgomeryshire Collections*

The Club’s central folklore legacy is the printed journal *Montgomeryshire Collections*. The Powysland Club’s own contents list shows how often Elias Owen’s “Montgomeryshire Folk-lore” appeared in the 1890s: in volumes for 1893, 1894, 1896, 1898 and 1900, alongside work on holy wells, archaic words, almanacs, parish histories, Offa’s Dyke and local records.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.powyslandclub.co.uk/montgomeryshire-collections/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: powyslandclub.co.uk">[powyslandclub.co.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">powyslandclub.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Montgomeryshire Collections – Powysland Club:Clwb Powysland</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Montgomeryshire Collections – Powysland Club:Clwb Powysland</span></span></span>

This matters because the journal preserved folklore at county scale rather than only as a national Welsh theme. Elias Owen’s better-known *Welsh Folk-Lore* presented North Wales traditions to a wider readership, but the journal pieces kept many stories rooted in Montgomeryshire places. A spectral funeral is not just “a Welsh ghost motif”; in Owen’s account it is connected to Llwyn-y-gog, Dylife, Craig-y-dduallt and Trefeglwys. A fairy tale is not simply a generic fairy reward story; it belongs to the route from Darowen towards Trefeglwys, to Fairy Green Lane and to local memory around Llanidloes.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/stream/collectionshist08unkngoog/collectionshist08unkngoog_djvu.txt" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on archive.org.</span></span></span>

The journal also shows the practical difficulty of keeping county memory alive. In an 1890s annual-meeting discussion, Owen warned that much work had been done, but that the work was “not yet finished”; he also noted that the journal needed fresh contributors if it was to continue. He named parishes still lacking proper treatment, including Berriew and Montgomery.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/stream/collectionshisto29powyuoft/collectionshisto29powyuoft_djvu.txt" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on archive.org.</span></span></span>

That anxiety is revealing. Folklore preservation is often imagined as a collector simply writing down old tales, but *Montgomeryshire Collections* shows a more precarious reality: editors needed contributors, subscribers, meetings, printers, money and local energy. When any of those failed, stories and records could remain uncollected. The survival of Montgomeryshire lore therefore depended not only on belief, but on institutions.

## Elias Owen and oral folklore gathering

Elias Owen is the key bridge between Montgomeryshire’s local-history world and wider Welsh folklore. The Dictionary of Welsh Biography identifies him as a cleric and antiquary whose *Welsh Folk-Lore* was a prize essay at the 1887 National Eisteddfod in London, later published in 1896.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://biography.wales/article/s-OWEN-ELI-1833" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: biography.wales">[Dictionary of Welsh Biography]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">biography.wales</span><span class="citation-popover-title">s OWEN ELI 1833</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">s OWEN ELI 1833</span></span></span>

Owen’s own preface explains why his work is so valuable and why it must be read carefully. He says he gathered information chiefly from elderly inhabitants, drawing on his travels as Diocesan Inspector of Schools in the Diocese of St Asaph, where he visited parishes annually for seventeen years. He also describes writing down “singular and valuable information” on scraps of paper before transferring it to notebooks.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/files/20096/20096-h/20096-h.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Project Gutenberg Welsh Folk-Lore</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Project Gutenberg Welsh Folk-Lore</span></span></span>

That method gives his material a strong oral flavour. He was not merely copying literary legends from books. He was listening to people, often in rural and clerical networks, and trying to rescue what he believed was disappearing. At the same time, his position as a clergyman, school inspector and antiquary shaped what he heard, whom he could approach, and how stories appeared in print. The result is not raw oral tradition untouched by editing; it is oral tradition filtered through a literate Victorian collector who cared about names, places and respectability.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/files/20096/20096-h/20096-h.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Project Gutenberg Welsh Folk-Lore</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Project Gutenberg Welsh Folk-Lore</span></span></span>

Owen was unusually explicit about evidence. In *Welsh Folk-Lore* he says he tried to give informants’ names, but that some speakers asked not to be named when pencil and paper appeared. He also states that much of his information was spoken in Welsh and that he often acted as a translator, aiming to give a literal rendering rather than embellishing the narratives.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/files/20096/20096-h/20096-h.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Project Gutenberg Welsh Folk-Lore</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Project Gutenberg Welsh Folk-Lore</span></span></span>

For a modern reader, that statement is important. It does not prove every tale happened, and it does not remove all bias from the record. But it does show that Owen understood the difference between a vague legend and a sourced local account. When Montgomeryshire folklore pages today lean on Owen, they are often leaning on a collector who was already thinking about informants, language, translation and the danger of losing oral memory.

## What kinds of Montgomeryshire lore did the Club preserve?

The Powysland Club’s folklore value lies in its mixture of the spectacular and the everyday. Some items feel recognisably supernatural: fairies, phantom funerals, ghost stories, ominous birds and strange apparitions. Others are closer to custom and belief: divination, charms, animal omens, old rhymes, seasonal habits, healing practices and local explanations of landscape. In the 1896 volume, for example, Owen’s “Montgomeryshire Folk-lore” is listed with sections on Llanymynech Hill, animal folklore, a ghost story and three crows; later in the same volume another instalment includes divination, animal folklore, social folklore and folk rhymes.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/stream/collectionshisto29powyuoft/collectionshisto29powyuoft_djvu.txt" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on archive.org.</span></span></span>

One vivid example is the spectral funeral from Trefeglwys parish. Owen begins by stressing the importance of naming the people and the source of the information. He then says the account came from the Revd David Davies, Vicar of Llansilin, who knew the man in the story personally. The tale tells of a shepherd named Lloyd, living at Llwyn-y-gog near Dylife, who encountered what appeared to be a funeral procession at Craig-y-dduallt late at night. Local people interpreted it as a death omen: a phantom burial whose route would later be followed by a real funeral.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/stream/collectionshist08unkngoog/collectionshist08unkngoog_djvu.txt" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on archive.org.</span></span></span>

That story matters because it shows how county folklore binds belief to terrain. The strange power of the tale depends on the road, the mountain setting, the named farms and the unlikelihood of a funeral in that place. Without Owen’s recording, it might survive only as “someone once saw a funeral ghost”. With the Powysland setting, it becomes part of Montgomeryshire’s haunted geography.

Another useful anchor is the fairy story of Dick the Fiddler. Owen’s *Welsh Folk-Lore* says he took the story from a parochial account of Llanidloes published in *Montgomeryshire Collections*, where it had been related by Nicholas Bennett of Glanrafon, Trefeglwys. The story places Dick on Fairy Green Lane above Cefn Cloddiau after a week’s drinking at Darowen, where the fair folk reward or trick him through a coin rattling in his fiddle.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/files/20096/20096-h/20096-h.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Project Gutenberg Welsh Folk-Lore</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Project Gutenberg Welsh Folk-Lore</span></span></span>

The tale has all the ingredients readers expect from Welsh fairy lore: music, drink, a liminal lane, a fairy meeting place, a deceptive gift and a moral sting. Yet its survival also shows the Club’s specific role. A local parochial account preserved the story; Owen reused it in a broader folklore collection; later readers now encounter it as both Montgomeryshire lore and North Welsh fairy tradition.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/files/20096/20096-h/20096-h.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Project Gutenberg Welsh Folk-Lore</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Project Gutenberg Welsh Folk-Lore</span></span></span>

## How antiquarian sources shape modern retellings

The Powysland Club did not simply preserve Montgomeryshire folklore; it shaped the way later readers understand it. Antiquarian collectors tended to favour named places, old families, parish structures, churches, ruins, roads and records. That gives modern retellings a strong sense of locality, but it can also make surviving folklore uneven. Places with energetic contributors are better represented than places where no one wrote things down. Owen himself warned that several important parishes had not yet found a place in the Club’s publications.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/stream/collectionshisto29powyuoft/collectionshisto29powyuoft_djvu.txt" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on archive.org.</span></span></span>

This means the printed record should not be mistaken for the full living tradition of the county. It is a survival map, not a complete map. Some beliefs were probably too ordinary to record, too private to name, too Welsh-language in texture to translate neatly, or too associated with poorer speakers to be treated at length by gentlemen antiquaries. Owen’s admission that some informants did not want to be named is a reminder that the printed page often hides social awkwardness behind a polished account.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/files/20096/20096-h/20096-h.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Project Gutenberg Welsh Folk-Lore</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Project Gutenberg Welsh Folk-Lore</span></span></span>

The antiquarian frame also gives folklore a historical dignity. A fairy lane appears beside parish history; a ghost account sits near records, roads and church material; a holy well can be considered with archaeology and ecclesiastical history. For modern readers, that is helpful because it prevents folklore from floating away into vague “myth”. It shows that stories were attached to real farms, hills, lanes, churches and routes across the county.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.powyslandclub.co.uk/montgomeryshire-collections/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: powyslandclub.co.uk">[powyslandclub.co.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">powyslandclub.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Montgomeryshire Collections – Powysland Club:Clwb Powysland</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Montgomeryshire Collections – Powysland Club:Clwb Powysland</span></span></span>

The risk is that later retellings may flatten the evidence. A story recorded with an informant, a place and a printed source may be retold online as if it were timeless and anonymous. A collector’s translation may become the “original” version. A tale preserved in a Victorian journal may be treated as ancient simply because it sounds old. The best modern use of the Powysland material keeps those layers visible: oral telling, local collector, printed journal, later anthology and present-day retelling.

## From Welshpool library to public memory

The Powysland Club’s role is not only historical. Its library in Welshpool still holds rare and valuable books relating to the history and archaeology of Wales and Montgomeryshire, along with reference works and map material, including early Ordnance Survey six-inch maps and copies of nineteenth-century tithe maps and apportionments.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.powyslandclub.co.uk/library/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: powyslandclub.co.uk">[powyslandclub.co.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">powyslandclub.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Library – Powysland Club:Clwb Powysland</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Library – Powysland Club:Clwb Powysland</span></span></span>

For folklore, maps matter almost as much as stories. Many traditions depend on being able to locate a lane, farm, parish, well, hill or boundary. A tale such as Dick the Fiddler’s loses force if Fairy Green Lane, Cefn Cloddiau, Darowen and Trefeglwys become vague names. The Club’s wider local-history collections help keep that landscape legible, especially for readers trying to connect oral tradition with old roads, chapels, townships and parish boundaries.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.powyslandclub.co.uk/library/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: powyslandclub.co.uk">[powyslandclub.co.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">powyslandclub.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Library – Powysland Club:Clwb Powysland</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Library – Powysland Club:Clwb Powysland</span></span></span>

The wider Powysland heritage ecosystem also carries this county memory forward. Y Lanfa: Powysland Museum and Welshpool Library is now presented as a museum of the archaeology and social history of Montgomeryshire, housed beside the Montgomery Canal, with galleries covering changing life in mid Wales and archaeological finds from prehistoric, Roman and early medieval contexts.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://storipowys.org.uk/visit-us/museums/y-lanfa/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: storipowys.org.uk">[Powys]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">storipowys.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Powys Y Lanfa</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Powys Y Lanfa</span></span></span>

That museum setting does not turn every legend into an exhibit, but it helps explain why folklore belongs within local history rather than outside it. The same county that preserves Roman coin hoards, medieval tiles, trades, pubs, canals, railways and war memories also preserves tales of fairies, spectral funerals, omens and charms. Montgomeryshire’s strange stories are part of how people made sense of the same landscape whose material history fills archives and museums.

## How to read Powysland folklore today

The most useful way to read Powysland folklore is with two thoughts in mind at once. First, these stories are precious survivals of local imagination: they show how Montgomeryshire people spoke about danger, luck, death, fairies, animals, weather, illness, roads and lonely places. Secondly, they are mediated survivals: they reached us through collectors, clergy, editors, translators, subscribers and county institutions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/files/20096/20096-h/20096-h.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Project Gutenberg Welsh Folk-Lore</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Project Gutenberg Welsh Folk-Lore</span></span></span>

That dual reading makes the stories richer, not weaker. The spectral funeral near Dylife is not less interesting because Owen names his clerical source; it is more interesting because we can see how a frightening local account moved from neighbourhood talk into county print. Dick the Fiddler is not less magical because it passed through a parochial account and then into Owen’s collection; that chain of transmission is the reason the story can still be read today.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/stream/collectionshist08unkngoog/collectionshist08unkngoog_djvu.txt" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on archive.org.</span></span></span>

For Montgomeryshire, the Powysland Club’s greatest folklore achievement was therefore not the invention of a grand county mythology. It was quieter and more durable: it gave parish memory somewhere to go. By printing local lore in *Montgomeryshire Collections*, maintaining a county library, encouraging contributors and linking stories to real places, the Club helped ensure that Montgomeryshire’s fairies, ghosts, omens, charms and odd fragments of belief were not left entirely to chance.

    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-selected-market-flag], [data-ebay-trigger-market-flag]')).forEach(function (flag) {
      flag.className = flagClass(marketId);
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-trigger-market-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = selectedText;
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-domain-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = domainLabel(marketId);
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-option]')).forEach(function (button) {
      button.setAttribute('aria-selected', button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-option') === marketId ? 'true' : 'false');
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      var active = button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button') === marketId;
      button.setAttribute('aria-pressed', active ? 'true' : 'false');
    });
    section.setAttribute('data-ebay-selected-market', marketId);
    if (persist) {
      try { if (window.localStorage) localStorage.setItem('phoenix-ebay-market', marketId); } catch (err) {}
    }
    closeMenu(section);
  }
  sections.forEach(function (section) {
    if (section.getAttribute('data-ebay-localized-init') === '1') return;
    section.setAttribute('data-ebay-localized-init', '1');
    var available = availableMarkets(section);
    var marketId = defaultMarket;
    if (window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation && window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation.inferMarket) {
      marketId = window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation.inferMarket(available, {
        storageKey: 'phoenix-ebay-market',
        defaultMarket: defaultMarket
      });
    } else if (available.indexOf(defaultMarket)< 0) {
      marketId = available[0] || defaultMarket;
    }
    var select = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-select]');
    if (select) {
      select.addEventListener('change', function () {
        applyMarket(section, select.value, true);
      });
    }
    var trigger = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-trigger]');
    var menu = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-menu]');
    if (trigger && menu) {
      trigger.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
        event.stopPropagation();
        var expanded = trigger.getAttribute('aria-expanded') === 'true';
        menu.hidden = expanded;
        trigger.setAttribute('aria-expanded', expanded ? 'false' : 'true');
        var picker = trigger.closest('.fr-ebay-market-picker');
        if (picker) picker.classList.toggle('fr-ebay-market-picker--open', !expanded);
      });
      Array.prototype.slice.call(menu.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-option]')).forEach(function (button) {
        button.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
          event.stopPropagation();
          applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-option'), true);
        });
      });
      document.addEventListener('click', function () { closeMenu(section); });
    }
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      button.addEventListener('click', function () {
        applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button'), true);
      });
    });
    applyMarket(section, marketId, false);
  });
})();
