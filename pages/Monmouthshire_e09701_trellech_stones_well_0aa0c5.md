## Introduction

Trellech attracts so many legends because its stories are unusually well anchored in things a visitor can still see: three prehistoric standing stones beside the Chepstow road, a bubbling holy well in a meadow, and a village memory that has kept treating both as more than archaeological remains. Harold’s Stones are officially recorded as a prehistoric stone alignment, probably Bronze Age, while the Virtuous Well is remembered as a healing spring, wishing well, fairy place and former pilgrim stop. The result is not one tidy legend but a layered [Monmouthshire]({{ 'where-monmouthshires-border-legends/' | relative_url }}) landscape: prehistoric monument, medieval settlement, saintly water, battle memory, fairy warning tale and modern heritage trail all sharing the same small village.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cadwpublic-api.azurewebsites.net/reports/sam/FullReport?id=372&amp;lang=en" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cadwpublic-api.azurewebsites.net">[azurewebsites.net]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cadwpublic-api.azurewebsites.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Scheduled Monument</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Full Report - HeritageBill Cadw Assets - Reports...</span></span></span>

That layering is what makes Trellech so valuable for county folklore. The stones and the well show how local people explain old, puzzling places: by connecting them to famous names such as Harold Godwinson, to border tricksters such as Jack O’Kent, to healing customs, to marriage divination, and to the idea that certain springs belong partly to the fairies. Trellech is therefore best read not as a single “mythic site”, but as a living legend cluster where archaeology, place-name lore, church memory, tourist interpretation and continuing small offerings all keep the stories in circulation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.visitmonmouthshire.com/things-to-do/harolds-stones-p2321381" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: visitmonmouthshire.com">[Visit Monmouthshire]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">visitmonmouthshire.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Visit Monmouthshire Harold&#x27;s Stones</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Visit Monmouthshire Harold&#x27;s Stones</span></span></span>

## Why Trellech Became a Legend-Magnet

Trellech lies in historic Monmouthshire, south of Monmouth and above the Wye Valley, in a part of the county where old roads, wooded slopes, springs and border history sit close together. The modern village is small, but its legendary weight is much larger than its size because its three famous features — Harold’s Stones, the Virtuous Well and the mound known as Tump Terret — were already being grouped together as defining local antiquities by the late seventeenth century. A 1689 sundial associated with Lady Probert of the Argoed depicts the stones, the well and the mound, turning them into a kind of carved emblem of the village’s identity.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://coflein.gov.uk/en/sites/221159" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: coflein.gov.uk">[coflein.gov.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">coflein.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Coflein English – Coflein</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Coflein English – Coflein</span></span></span>

This matters because folklore often strengthens when a community has visible “proof-points” in the landscape. Trellech’s stories are not free-floating tales that could happen anywhere. They attach to specific objects: three leaning stones in a field, a stone-built spring with ledges and niches, and a church where the village’s monuments were commemorated in carved form. Even the tourism presentation preserves this pattern, with Visit Monmouthshire treating Harold’s Stones and the Virtuous Well as nearby historic attractions and explicitly repeating the local legends attached to each.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.visitmonmouthshire.com/things-to-do/harolds-stones-p2321381" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: visitmonmouthshire.com">[Visit Monmouthshire]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">visitmonmouthshire.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Visit Monmouthshire Harold&#x27;s Stones</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Visit Monmouthshire Harold&#x27;s Stones</span></span></span>

There is also a deeper historical reason why Trellech accumulated story. It was once a far more substantial medieval settlement than its present scale suggests. Modern summaries of the “lost city” archaeology describe medieval Trellech as a major settlement, with 378 burgage plots recorded by 1288, and connect its growth to lordship, ironworking and the wider Marcher border economy. That does not explain the prehistoric stones or holy well by itself, but it helps explain why the village had enough historic memory, parish identity and antiquarian attention for its monuments to be repeatedly named, carved, visited and retold.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.heritagedaily.com/2020/08/the-lost-town-of-trellech/134660" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: heritagedaily.com">[heritagedaily.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">heritagedaily.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Heritage Daily</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Heritage Daily</span></span></span>

## Harold’s Stones and Battle Memory

Harold’s Stones are three large standing stones south of Trellech village. Cadw records them as a scheduled monument, reference MM017, in the community of Trellech United, and describes them as a prehistoric stone alignment probably dating to the Bronze Age, roughly 2300–800 BC. The three stones form a north-east to south-west alignment, made of local conglomerate “pudding stone”; Cadw gives the total distance between them as 14 metres and treats the site as nationally important for what it may reveal about prehistoric ritual and funerary landscapes.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cadwpublic-api.azurewebsites.net/reports/sam/FullReport?id=372&amp;lang=en" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cadwpublic-api.azurewebsites.net">[cadwpublic-api.azurewebsites.net]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cadwpublic-api.azurewebsites.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Scheduled Monument</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Full Report - HeritageBill Cadw Assets - Reports...</span></span></span>

Coflein, the online record of the Royal Commission on the Ancient and Historical Monuments of Wales, adds two details that are important for folklore readers. First, it notes that the original function of the stones is “hardly understood”: ritual use is possible, and an astronomical use cannot be ruled out. Secondly, it records that the central stone bears two large cup-mark carvings, and that geophysical survey has suggested prehistoric and later features nearby, including a possible circular monument or small henge-like feature to the west of the stones. The stones therefore sit in that classic folklore zone where archaeology can say “old, important and not fully explained”, while story steps in to supply names and motives.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://coflein.gov.uk/en/sites/221159" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: coflein.gov.uk">[Coflein]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">coflein.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Coflein English – Coflein</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Coflein English – Coflein</span></span></span>

The name “Harold’s Stones” belongs to this second layer. Local legend has connected the stones with Harold Godwinson, the last Anglo-Saxon king of England, and with a supposed victory over the Britons or Welsh. Visit Monmouthshire repeats the tradition that the stones were erected to commemorate Harold’s victory, but immediately points out the problem: the stones are far older than Harold by at least two thousand years. That does not make the legend worthless. It shows how a prehistoric monument was pulled into later border memory, giving an ancient and mysterious object a name from remembered conflict.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.visitmonmouthshire.com/things-to-do/harolds-stones-p2321381" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: visitmonmouthshire.com">[Visit Monmouthshire]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">visitmonmouthshire.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Visit Monmouthshire Harold&#x27;s Stones</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Visit Monmouthshire Harold&#x27;s Stones</span></span></span>

A related version says the stones mark the place where three British chieftains fell in battle with Harold. This is a familiar kind of explanatory folklore: three stones become three dead leaders, and an unexplained alignment becomes a memorial. The value of the tale is not that it reliably records an eleventh-century event. Its value is that it reveals how Trellech’s stones were made meaningful in a county shaped by Welsh-English border history, where Saxon, Welsh, Norman and later local identities could all be folded into stories about visible remains.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.visitmonmouthshire.com/things-to-do/harolds-stones-p2321381" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: visitmonmouthshire.com">[Visit Monmouthshire]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">visitmonmouthshire.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Visit Monmouthshire Harold&#x27;s Stones</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Visit Monmouthshire Harold&#x27;s Stones</span></span></span>

## Jack O’Kent and the Stones Thrown from the Hills

The more playful legend says the stones were thrown to Trellech by Jack O’Kent in a contest with the Devil. Visit Monmouthshire gives the version in which Jack threw the stone “from a dozen miles away” and the village became known as the City of the Stones. Other retellings connect the throw with the Skirrid, one of Monmouthshire’s most storied hills, or with nearby Beacon Hill. The exact hill varies, but the structure of the tale is stable: an uncanny strongman or wizard competes with the Devil, and the landscape is explained as the physical result of their game.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.visitmonmouthshire.com/things-to-do/harolds-stones-p2321381" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: visitmonmouthshire.com">[visitmonmouthshire.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">visitmonmouthshire.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Visit Monmouthshire Harold&#x27;s Stones</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Visit Monmouthshire Harold&#x27;s Stones</span></span></span>

Jack O’Kent is a border figure rather than a purely Trellech figure. His legends are strongly associated with Herefordshire and Monmouthshire, and he often appears as a clever magician, cleric or trickster who defeats the Devil through bargains, wordplay or cunning. That makes him a natural fit for Trellech. The stones need an origin story; Jack needs impossible feats to prove his power; and the Marches provide a cultural setting where a Welsh-English trickster can move across county lines as easily as stories themselves do.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jack_o%27_Kent" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Jack o&#x27; Kent</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Jack o&#x27; Kent</span></span></span>

This legend also changes the emotional tone of the stones. The Harold tale makes them solemn: battlefield, kingship, dead chieftains. The Jack O’Kent tale makes them mischievous: game, contest, enormous strength, Devil outwitted or challenged. Both are useful. Together they show how one monument can hold more than one kind of memory. Trellech does not ask the visitor to choose between “battle memorial” and “giant’s throw” as if they were rival historical accounts. Instead, the stories show different ways of domesticating a prehistoric mystery: one through history, one through wonder.

The Jack O’Kent layer also connects Trellech to other Monmouthshire legends, especially stories around the Skirrid and Sugar Loaf, where Jack’s dealings with the Devil explain broken hills, strange landforms and local place-memory. In a county folklore map, Harold’s Stones are therefore not isolated. They are part of a wider Marcher habit of explaining dramatic or puzzling landscape features through contests between human cunning and diabolical power.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://thejournalofantiquities.com/category/virtuous-well/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: thejournalofantiquities.com">[The Journal Of Antiquities]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">thejournalofantiquities.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">virtuous well</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">virtuous well</span></span></span>

## The Virtuous Well and Wishing Customs

The Virtuous Well, also known as St Anne’s Well, lies a short distance from Harold’s Stones and is one of Monmouthshire’s clearest examples of a holy well that has remained legible as a folk site. Visit Monmouthshire describes it as a chalybeate spring, meaning iron-rich water, bubbling into a stone basin set into an arched recess within a partly sunken, horseshoe-shaped structure. The site has steps, stone seating and niches that may have held cups or offerings, making it easy to understand why it was experienced not merely as a water source but as a place of ritual use.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.visitmonmouthshire.com/things-to-do/the-virtuous-well-p2321371" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: visitmonmouthshire.com">[Visit Monmouthshire]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">visitmonmouthshire.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Visit Monmouthshire The Virtuous Well</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Visit Monmouthshire The Virtuous Well</span></span></span>

The well’s name can mislead modern readers. “Virtuous” does not mean that the water was thought to make people morally good. Visit Monmouthshire explains that the name refers to medicinal virtue: the supposed healing power of the iron-impregnated water. The well was once famous for cures and was reportedly visited by pilgrims as late as the seventeenth century. Later traditions held that its waters were useful for eye ailments and for “complaints peculiar to women”, a phrase that reflects older medical language rather than modern diagnosis.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.visitmonmouthshire.com/things-to-do/the-virtuous-well-p2321371" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: visitmonmouthshire.com">[Visit Monmouthshire]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">visitmonmouthshire.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Visit Monmouthshire The Virtuous Well</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Visit Monmouthshire The Virtuous Well</span></span></span>

Its wishing custom is especially vivid because it made the water itself answer back. A person made a wish by dropping a small metal object into the spring. Many bubbles meant the wish would be granted quickly; fewer bubbles meant delay; no bubbles meant the wish would fail. A related marriage-divination custom said that young women could drop a pebble into the water and count the bubbles, each bubble representing one month until marriage.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.visitmonmouthshire.com/things-to-do/the-virtuous-well-p2321371" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: visitmonmouthshire.com">[Visit Monmouthshire]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">visitmonmouthshire.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Visit Monmouthshire The Virtuous Well</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Visit Monmouthshire The Virtuous Well</span></span></span>

These customs are important because they turn natural behaviour — bubbles rising through spring water — into a readable sign. The well becomes an oracle, but a modest domestic one: not a grand prophecy about kings or battles, but practical answers about health, hope and marriage. Similar bubble divinations occur at other British wells, but Trellech’s version is unusually memorable because it sits beside a named saintly well, a visible prehistoric monument and a local fairy story, giving the custom several layers of meaning at once.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://britishfairies.wordpress.com/tag/trellech/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: britishfairies.wordpress.com">[British Fairies]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">britishfairies.wordpress.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">British Fairiestrellech &#124; British Fairies</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">British Fairiestrellech &#124; British Fairies</span></span></span>

## Fairies at the Well

The fairy traditions at the Virtuous Well add a different kind of warning. Visit Monmouthshire and Literature Wales’s Land of Legends both preserve the story of fairies dancing around the well, especially at Midsummer. Land of Legends says the fairies drank the water on Midsummer eve from flowers found scattered the following morning, while Visit Monmouthshire gives the fuller tale of a farmer who dug up the fairy ring near the well because he disliked “silly tales”. The next day, the well was dry for him alone: other villagers could still draw water, but he could not.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.visitmonmouthshire.com/things-to-do/the-virtuous-well-p2321371" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: visitmonmouthshire.com">[Visit Monmouthshire]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">visitmonmouthshire.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Visit Monmouthshire The Virtuous Well</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Visit Monmouthshire The Virtuous Well</span></span></span>

The punishment is precise and revealing. The farmer is not struck dead, dragged underground or turned into a monster. He is excluded from the well’s benefit. A “little old man” sitting by the well tells him that the destruction of the fairy ring has caused offence and orders him to restore it. Once the missing turf is replaced, the water flows for him again. This is folklore as environmental etiquette: do not damage the ring, do not mock local belief, and do not treat a shared sacred place as if it has no unseen guardians.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.visitmonmouthshire.com/things-to-do/the-virtuous-well-p2321371" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: visitmonmouthshire.com">[Visit Monmouthshire]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">visitmonmouthshire.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Visit Monmouthshire The Virtuous Well</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Visit Monmouthshire The Virtuous Well</span></span></span>

The tale also makes sense beside the well’s healing reputation. If people believed the water could cure, then losing access to it mattered. The fairy story therefore protects the place not by abstract moralising but by a memorable consequence: disrespect the well’s fairy ground and the spring will withhold itself from you. In that way, the story belongs with a wider British and Welsh tradition in which wells, trees, stones and rings are not inert scenery but socially charged places that demand care.

Modern visitors may notice that offerings still appear at the well, including cloth and ribbons. Visit Monmouthshire describes strips of white cloth and ribbons on hedgerow trees behind the well, connecting them with continuing belief in medicinal properties; it also explains the older idea that a dipped garment, rotting away, would carry symptoms away with it. This makes the Virtuous Well more than a preserved legend. It is still being interacted with, even if modern motives range from belief and remembrance to curiosity, tourism and participation in a recognised folk custom.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.visitmonmouthshire.com/things-to-do/the-virtuous-well-p2321371" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: visitmonmouthshire.com">[Visit Monmouthshire]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">visitmonmouthshire.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Visit Monmouthshire The Virtuous Well</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Visit Monmouthshire The Virtuous Well</span></span></span>

## Saints, Springs and Older Water Belief

The well’s saintly name, St Anne’s Well, places it within the Christian holy-well tradition, but Trellech’s stories do not sit neatly in one religious category. Visit Monmouthshire describes pilgrimages, cures, cups, offerings and the well’s appearance on the village sundial. Land of Legends, meanwhile, presents the well as part of a watery mythic landscape and suggests that prehistoric people may have regarded watery places as portals to other worlds. The strongest evidence is for the later holy-well and folk-healing customs; claims about prehistoric sacred use are more speculative, though they are understandable given the nearby stones and the long history of ritual landscapes.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.visitmonmouthshire.com/things-to-do/the-virtuous-well-p2321371" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: visitmonmouthshire.com">[visitmonmouthshire.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">visitmonmouthshire.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Visit Monmouthshire The Virtuous Well</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Visit Monmouthshire The Virtuous Well</span></span></span>

The most useful way to read Trellech is as layering rather than replacement. An iron-rich spring becomes a healing well. A healing well becomes St Anne’s Well. A saintly well continues to work as a wishing well. A wishing well gains fairy guardians. A nearby prehistoric stone alignment is connected by story to water, battle and supernatural throwing contests. None of these layers has to erase the others. They show how local tradition keeps adapting a place to new explanations while preserving the feeling that the site is special.

This layering is visible in the built form of the well. The stone seats, ledges and niches make sense for practical visitors and pilgrims; the bubbling basin makes sense for divination; the surrounding meadow and hedgerow make sense for fairy-ring and offering traditions. The site’s physical features help the stories survive because they give each tale somewhere to attach. A purely written legend can fade; a legend with a well, stones, seats and a path can be reactivated every time someone visits.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.visitmonmouthshire.com/things-to-do/the-virtuous-well-p2321371" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: visitmonmouthshire.com">[Visit Monmouthshire]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">visitmonmouthshire.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Visit Monmouthshire The Virtuous Well</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Visit Monmouthshire The Virtuous Well</span></span></span>

## What Is Well Attested, and What Is Later Retelling?

The firmest evidence at Trellech is archaeological and topographical: Harold’s Stones are real, scheduled, prehistoric standing stones; Coflein and Cadw record their location, alignment, material and likely Bronze Age date; the Virtuous Well is a real spring site with built stonework and a long reputation for healing and wishing customs. The seventeenth-century sundial tradition is also important because it shows that Trellech’s monuments were already being treated as defining village antiquities more than three centuries ago.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cadwpublic-api.azurewebsites.net/reports/sam/FullReport?id=372&amp;lang=en" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cadwpublic-api.azurewebsites.net">[azurewebsites.net]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cadwpublic-api.azurewebsites.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Scheduled Monument</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Full Report - HeritageBill Cadw Assets - Reports...</span></span></span>

The Harold battle explanation is weaker as history but strong as folklore. The stones predate Harold Godwinson by millennia, so they cannot have been erected by him after an eleventh-century victory. However, the persistence of the name and story is significant because it shows how later communities tied prehistoric remains to remembered conflict and heroic history. In folklore terms, the question is not “Did Harold build them?” but “Why did this community choose Harold as the person who could explain them?”<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.visitmonmouthshire.com/things-to-do/harolds-stones-p2321381" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: visitmonmouthshire.com">[Visit Monmouthshire]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">visitmonmouthshire.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Visit Monmouthshire Harold&#x27;s Stones</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Visit Monmouthshire Harold&#x27;s Stones</span></span></span>

The Jack O’Kent legend is also not historical evidence for the origin of the stones. It is a classic explanatory wonder-tale, connecting an impossible landscape feature with a cunning border hero and the Devil. Its value lies in the way it links Trellech to a wider Monmouthshire and Marches story-world, where hills, stones and broken landforms become the aftermath of supernatural games.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.visitmonmouthshire.com/things-to-do/harolds-stones-p2321381" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: visitmonmouthshire.com">[Visit Monmouthshire]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">visitmonmouthshire.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Visit Monmouthshire Harold&#x27;s Stones</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Visit Monmouthshire Harold&#x27;s Stones</span></span></span>

## Why Trellech Still Feels Like Living Legend

Trellech’s legends remain alive because the sites are small enough to be encountered directly. Harold’s Stones are not locked away as abstract museum objects; they stand in the landscape, leaning and weathered, with their prehistoric purpose still partly unknown. The Virtuous Well still presents itself as a place of action: water bubbles, cloth is tied, objects are left, and visitors can recognise the pattern of old wishing and healing customs even if they do not share the beliefs behind them.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://coflein.gov.uk/en/sites/221159" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: coflein.gov.uk">[Coflein]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">coflein.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Coflein English – Coflein</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Coflein English – Coflein</span></span></span>

The village also benefits from a rare density of story. In many places, a standing stone has one legend, or a holy well has one healing claim. Trellech has multiple overlapping explanations within walking distance: Bronze Age ritual landscape, Harold’s victory, fallen chieftains, Jack O’Kent’s contest, saintly healing, bubble divination, marriage wishes, fairy dancing and a warning about damaging a fairy ring. That density makes it one of Monmouthshire’s strongest folklore locations because it shows several mechanisms of legend-making at once.

For readers exploring Monmouthshire folklore, Trellech is a useful anchor because it demonstrates the county’s broader pattern in miniature. The old county’s legends often sit at borders: between Wales and England, archaeology and story, Christianity and older water reverence, tourism and local memory, visible ruins and invisible beings. Trellech turns that pattern into a walkable landscape. The stones ask why people explain ancient monuments through kings, giants and devils; the well asks why water becomes healing, wishing and fairy-haunted; together they show how a Monmouthshire village can keep renewing its legends without needing to settle them into one final version.

    return Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]'))
      .map(function (button) { return button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button'); })
      .filter(function (marketId) { return !!markets[marketId]; });
  }
  function applyMarket(section, marketId, persist) {
    var available = availableMarkets(section);
    if (available.indexOf(marketId)< 0) marketId = available[0] || defaultMarket;
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-localized-link]')).forEach(function (link) {
      var query = link.getAttribute('data-ebay-query') || '';
      var reference = link.getAttribute('data-ebay-reference') || '';
      link.setAttribute('href', buildUrl(query, reference, marketId));
    });
    var select = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-select]');
    if (select && available.indexOf(marketId) >= 0) select.value = marketId;
    var selectedText = marketId;
    if (select && select.selectedIndex >= 0) selectedText = select.options[select.selectedIndex].text;
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-selected-market-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = selectedText;
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-selected-market-flag], [data-ebay-trigger-market-flag]')).forEach(function (flag) {
      flag.className = flagClass(marketId);
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-trigger-market-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = selectedText;
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-domain-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = domainLabel(marketId);
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-option]')).forEach(function (button) {
      button.setAttribute('aria-selected', button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-option') === marketId ? 'true' : 'false');
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      var active = button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button') === marketId;
      button.setAttribute('aria-pressed', active ? 'true' : 'false');
    });
    section.setAttribute('data-ebay-selected-market', marketId);
    if (persist) {
      try { if (window.localStorage) localStorage.setItem('phoenix-ebay-market', marketId); } catch (err) {}
    }
    closeMenu(section);
  }
  sections.forEach(function (section) {
    if (section.getAttribute('data-ebay-localized-init') === '1') return;
    section.setAttribute('data-ebay-localized-init', '1');
    var available = availableMarkets(section);
    var marketId = defaultMarket;
    if (window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation && window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation.inferMarket) {
      marketId = window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation.inferMarket(available, {
        storageKey: 'phoenix-ebay-market',
        defaultMarket: defaultMarket
      });
    } else if (available.indexOf(defaultMarket)< 0) {
      marketId = available[0] || defaultMarket;
    }
    var select = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-select]');
    if (select) {
      select.addEventListener('change', function () {
        applyMarket(section, select.value, true);
      });
    }
    var trigger = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-trigger]');
    var menu = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-menu]');
    if (trigger && menu) {
      trigger.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
        event.stopPropagation();
        var expanded = trigger.getAttribute('aria-expanded') === 'true';
        menu.hidden = expanded;
        trigger.setAttribute('aria-expanded', expanded ? 'false' : 'true');
        var picker = trigger.closest('.fr-ebay-market-picker');
        if (picker) picker.classList.toggle('fr-ebay-market-picker--open', !expanded);
      });
      Array.prototype.slice.call(menu.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-option]')).forEach(function (button) {
        button.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
          event.stopPropagation();
          applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-option'), true);
        });
      });
      document.addEventListener('click', function () { closeMenu(section); });
    }
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      button.addEventListener('click', function () {
        applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button'), true);
      });
    });
    applyMarket(section, marketId, false);
  });
})();
