## Introduction

The county’s strongest traditions cluster around a few memorable places: [Dinas Emrys]({{ 'dinas-emrys/' | relative_url }}) near Beddgelert, where red and white dragons explain the failing walls of Vortigern’s fortress; Beddgelert itself, where the faithful hound [Gelert]({{ 'gelert/' | relative_url }}) became a tourist-era legend with deep emotional force; Yr Wyddfa, where giant and Arthurian stories attach themselves to the summit; Clynnog Fawr, where St Beuno’s well and tomb shaped pilgrimage and healing belief; and the lakes, passes and old settlements of Eryri and Llŷn, where fairies, witches, omens and local customs were collected in antiquarian sources and retold for modern visitors.

## Where Caernarfonshire folklore lives

Caernarfonshire is a historic county, not just a modern travel label. Its folklore belongs to coastal villages, chapels, mountain passes, slate districts, pilgrim routes and ancient sites that now often appear under the wider names Gwynedd, Eryri or North Wales. That matters because a story labelled “Snowdonia”, “Beddgelert” or “Llŷn” may still sit squarely inside the old county.

The county’s shape helps explain the character of its legends. The Menai Strait and Caernarfon Bay gave it sea roads and pilgrim movement. Llŷn pointed west towards Bardsey Island and its saintly associations. The mountains of Eryri gave it dramatic settings for giants, dragons, hidden treasure, fairy abductions and dangerous night travel. Clynnog Fawr, on the north coast of Llŷn, was a stopping place for pilgrims going to Bardsey, while Beddgelert and Dinas Emrys became magnets for story because they sit where river valleys, mountain roads and national myth meet.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nationalchurchestrust.org/church/st-beuno-clynnog-fawr" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nationalchurchestrust.org">[National Churches Trust]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nationalchurchestrust.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Churches Trust Clynnog Fawr St Beuno &#124; National Churches Trust</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Churches Trust Clynnog Fawr St Beuno &#124; National Churches Trust</span></span></span><span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.landoflegends.wales/location/dinas-emrys" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: landoflegends.wales">[Land of Legends]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">landoflegends.wales</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Land of Legends Dinas Emrys</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Land of Legends Dinas Emrys</span></span></span>

A useful rule is to separate three layers. First are older oral and local traditions: tales of fairies, wells, omens, witches, giants and supernatural places. Second are antiquarian collections, especially nineteenth- and early twentieth-century works that tried to preserve traditions remembered by local people. Third are modern retellings by tourism bodies, museums, walking guides and community projects. None of those layers is “fake” by default, but they do different jobs: oral tradition preserves belief and memory; antiquarian writing fixes fluid tales into print; tourism retellings make selected stories easy to visit.

## Dinas Emrys and the dragons beneath the hill

The most nationally famous Caernarfonshire legend is the red and white dragon story attached to Dinas Emrys, above Llyn Dinas near Beddgelert. In the familiar version, Vortigern tries to build a fortress, but the walls keep collapsing. A child seer, Myrddin Emrys, explains that two dragons lie hidden beneath a lake inside the mountain. When the lake is drained, the red and white dragons fight; the red dragon’s survival becomes a symbol of Wales. National Trust retellings place the story firmly at Dinas Emrys and describe the hill as the place where Vortigern’s castle was finally built and named in honour of Myrddin Emrys.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nationaltrust.org.uk/visit/wales/craflwyn-and-beddgelert/discover-the-legend-of-two-dragons" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nationaltrust.org.uk">[National Trust]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nationaltrust.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Trust Discover the legend of two dragons&#124;Craflwyn &#124; National Trust</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Trust Discover the legend of two dragons&#124;Craflwyn &#124; National Trust</span></span></span>

The story matters because it joins a local hill to the symbolic heart of Wales. It is not merely “a dragon tale”; it explains why the red dragon became emotionally available as a national emblem. Land of Legends links the Dinas Emrys version to the early medieval writer traditionally called Nennius and to the story of Vortigern’s failing castle, while modern visitor pages continue to frame the hill as the original home of the Welsh red dragon.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.landoflegends.wales/location/dinas-emrys" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: landoflegends.wales">[Land of Legends]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">landoflegends.wales</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Land of Legends Dinas Emrys</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Land of Legends Dinas Emrys</span></span></span>

The interesting point is that Dinas Emrys is not only literary. National Trust notes archaeological discovery at the site, including a lake and fortress ruins, while other summaries of the excavation tradition stress that the physical site has repeatedly encouraged people to connect archaeology with the legend.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nationaltrust.org.uk/visit/wales/craflwyn-and-beddgelert/discover-the-legend-of-two-dragons" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nationaltrust.org.uk">[National Trust]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nationaltrust.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Trust Discover the legend of two dragons&#124;Craflwyn &#124; National Trust</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Trust Discover the legend of two dragons&#124;Craflwyn &#124; National Trust</span></span></span><span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dinas_Emrys" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Dinas Emrys</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Dinas Emrys</span></span></span> This does not prove that dragons slept beneath the hill. It shows something more useful for folklore: the landscape had the right ingredients for belief to stick. A hillfort, a pool, broken walls, a commanding valley position and a famous medieval narrative all reinforced one another.

Dinas Emrys also carries a treasure motif. Local retellings say Myrddin hid treasure in a cave, and that only a golden-haired, blue-eyed discoverer would be invited inside by a ringing bell. A young man who tried to dig for it was frightened away by thunder, darkness and unearthly noise.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.beddgelerttourism.com/Dinas-Emrys-Princes/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: beddgelerttourism.com">[Beddgelert Tourism]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">beddgelerttourism.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on beddgelerttourism.com.</span></span></span> This is a classic guardian-place pattern: the treasure is not simply lost property; it belongs to the hill, and the hill resists greed.

## Beddgelert: beloved dog, village identity and tourist legend

Beddgelert’s most famous story is the tragedy of Gelert, the faithful hound. In the National Trust version, Llywelyn the Great leaves his baby in Gelert’s care, returns to find the dog bloodstained and the child missing, kills the dog in a panic, then discovers the baby safe beside the body of a wolf Gelert had killed. Grief-stricken, Llywelyn buries the hound by the river, and the village becomes associated with “Gelert’s grave”.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nationaltrust.org.uk/visit/wales/craflwyn-and-beddgelert/discover-the-legend-of-gelert" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nationaltrust.org.uk">[National Trust]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nationaltrust.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Trust Discover the legend of Gelert&#124;Beddgelert &#124; National Trust</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Trust Discover the legend of Gelert&#124;Beddgelert &#124; National Trust</span></span></span>

This is one of the best examples in Caernarfonshire of a story that feels ancient but is complicated by print, tourism and place-making. The tale is now inseparable from the village: the memorial walk, the riverside grave and the emotional simplicity of the story all make it easy to retell. Local tourism repeats the tombstone version and presents Gelert’s Grave as a short walk from the village.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.beddgelerttourism.com/gelert/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: beddgelerttourism.com">[Beddgelert Tourism]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">beddgelerttourism.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on beddgelerttourism.com.</span></span></span> Yet modern folklore discussions often point out that “faithful hound wrongly killed” is an international tale type, and that Beddgelert’s version was strongly promoted in the age of picturesque tourism.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://markreesonline.com/the-legend-of-gelert-the-heroic-hound-of-beddgelert-ghosts-and-folklore-of-wales-with-mark-rees-ep05/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: markreesonline.com">[MARK REES]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">markreesonline.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">MARK REESThe Legend of Gelert: Ghosts &amp; Folklore of Wales podcast</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">MARK REESThe Legend of Gelert: Ghosts &amp; Folklore of Wales podcast</span></span></span>

That tension is not a reason to dismiss the legend. It is the reason the legend is so revealing. Gelert shows how Caernarfonshire folklore can turn a place into a moral scene: a prince, a loyal animal, a rash judgement, a grave, a village name and a walkable memorial. The story’s power does not depend on proving that the event happened exactly as told. It survives because it gives Beddgelert a human-scale tragedy in a landscape otherwise dominated by mountains and national myth.

## Fairies, lakes and dangerous crossings

Welsh fairy tradition is central to Caernarfonshire, though it is often less tied to one single monument than the dragon and Gelert stories. Welsh fairies are commonly known in English as the Fair Family or fair folk, and older collectors described them as beings associated with underground places, lakes, hills, music, dancing, changelings, fairy wives and dangerous bargains. Wirt Sikes’s 1880 collection, *British Goblins*, is an influential English-language compilation of Welsh fairy and supernatural traditions, while John Rhŷs’s *Celtic Folklore: Welsh and Manx* remains one of the major early scholarly collections.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/34704" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on gutenberg.org.</span></span></span><span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/55025" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on gutenberg.org.</span></span></span>

In Caernarfonshire, fairy tradition attaches especially well to Eryri’s lakes and mountain margins. Visit Snowdonia points modern readers to the “Llyn Cwellyn Fairies” at Rhyd Ddu and describes the boardwalk beside Llyn Cwellyn as a place where visitors are invited to keep watch for Welsh fairies.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.visitsnowdonia.info/fresh-look-some-our-oldest-tales" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: visitsnowdonia.info">[Visit Snowdonia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">visitsnowdonia.info</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on visitsnowdonia.info.</span></span></span> The Carneddau Landscape Partnership also retells local fairy traditions, including changeling stories and wise-woman tests involving strange household behaviour such as filling an eggshell with beer.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://partneriaethcarneddau.cymru/myths-and-legends/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: partneriaethcarneddau.cymru">[Carneddau Landscape Partnership]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">partneriaethcarneddau.cymru</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Carneddau Landscape Partnership Myths and legends</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Carneddau Landscape Partnership Myths and legends</span></span></span>

These traditions answer a very old rural question: what explains sudden misfortune, child illness, disorientation or uncanny luck? Fairy stories often make sense of thresholds: a path beside water, a lonely pass, a night journey, a newborn child, a person who returns changed. They also create rules for conduct. Do not boast of fairy gifts. Do not break a taboo. Do not ignore odd behaviour in a child. Do not wander lightly into places local people treat as charged.

Caernarfonshire’s fairy landscape also crosses into neighbouring counties and wider Welsh tradition. Fairy brides from lakes are best known from other parts of Wales, and mountain fairy figures appear throughout Welsh folklore, but Eryri gives these motifs a particularly sharp setting: high lakes, sudden mist, deep cwms and paths where getting lost could be fatal even without supernatural help.

## Giants, Arthur and the mountain as a grave

Yr Wyddfa, the highest mountain in Wales, carries one of Caernarfonshire’s strongest giant legends. The summit is linked with Rhitta Gawr, a giant who defeats kings and takes their beards as trophies. In one popular version, Rhitta demands Arthur’s beard; Arthur fights and kills him, and the mountain becomes associated with the giant’s burial. Modern summaries of Snowdon’s folklore explain the Welsh name Yr Wyddfa through the idea of a grave or burial mound connected with Rhitta, though linguistic and legendary explanations should be handled carefully rather than treated as simple proof.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Snowdon" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wikipedia.org.</span></span></span>

Eryri National Park’s own storytelling keeps the Rhitta legend alive in public-facing heritage work, describing Rhitta Gawr as a fearsome giant embedded in the mountain’s slopes and pairing him with Arthurian associations such as Bwlch y Saethau, the Pass of Arrows, where Arthur is said in some traditions to have been mortally wounded.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://eryri.gov.wales/visit/snowdon/plastic-free-yr-wyddfa/bringing-legends-to-life-to-inspire-change/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: eryri.gov.wales">[Eryri National Park]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">eryri.gov.wales</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on gov.wales.</span></span></span> This is folklore doing landscape interpretation: ridges, passes and cairns become memorable because they are named through conflict.

The county also has darker giantess and witch material. Canthrig Bwt, associated with Nant Peris and Llanberis, is remembered as a witch, hag or giantess living beneath a stone and stealing or eating children. The National Library of Wales catalogue records *Llên Gwerin Sir Gaernarfon*, a county folklore collection linked to John Jones, Myrddin Fardd, while modern retellings and community arts projects continue to revisit Canthrig Bwt as a local “old woman” or cannibal witch legend.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://catalog.hathitrust.org/Record/100794773" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: catalog.hathitrust.org">[HathiTrust]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">catalog.hathitrust.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on hathitrust.org.</span></span></span><span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://lindseycolbourne.com/stori-canthrig-bwt" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: lindseycolbourne.com">[Lindsey Colbourne (Heledd Wen)]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">lindseycolbourne.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on lindseycolbourne.com.</span></span></span>

These giant stories are not gentle. They belong to a landscape where mountains dwarf people, where stones appear to have been placed by impossible hands, and where children are warned about danger through memorable horror. Arthur’s role as giant-slayer gives the county a heroic frame; Canthrig Bwt gives it a nursery-dark warning.

## Saints, wells and healing belief

Caernarfonshire’s sacred folklore is strongest around Clynnog Fawr and St Beuno. The National Churches Trust describes Clynnog Fawr as an important ecclesiastical site and a stopping place for pilgrims travelling to Bardsey Island. The church’s holy well was said to have curative properties, and people who spent the night on St Beuno’s tomb were said to be healed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nationalchurchestrust.org/church/st-beuno-clynnog-fawr" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nationalchurchestrust.org">[National Churches Trust]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nationalchurchestrust.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Churches Trust Clynnog Fawr St Beuno &#124; National Churches Trust</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Churches Trust Clynnog Fawr St Beuno &#124; National Churches Trust</span></span></span>

St Beuno’s Well, near the church, is one of the county’s clearest examples of the overlap between Christianity, healing custom and older water belief. Wellhopper calls the Clynnog Fawr well one of the finest wells dedicated to Beuno in Wales and connects it to the saint’s final church.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://wellhopper.wales/2013/01/23/ffynnon-beuno-clynnog-fawr/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wellhopper.wales">[Well Hopper]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wellhopper.wales</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Well Hopper Ffynnon Beuno, Clynnog Fawr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Well Hopper Ffynnon Beuno, Clynnog Fawr</span></span></span> Heritage accounts linked to the site describe children and sick people being dipped in the well and then taken to lie overnight at the saint’s tomb in hope of cure.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ffynnonbeuno.co.uk/our-heritage/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ffynnonbeuno.co.uk">[Ffynnon Beuno]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ffynnonbeuno.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on ffynnonbeuno.co.uk.</span></span></span>

This pattern is important because it shows how folklore is not always a “story” in the narrow sense. Sometimes it is a repeated action: wash here, sleep there, pray at this tomb, make an offering, follow this pilgrim route. St Beuno’s Chest, carved from a single piece of ash wood and used for offerings from Bardsey pilgrims, gives the belief culture a physical anchor inside the church.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nationalchurchestrust.org/church/st-beuno-clynnog-fawr" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nationalchurchestrust.org">[National Churches Trust]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nationalchurchestrust.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Churches Trust Clynnog Fawr St Beuno &#124; National Churches Trust</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Churches Trust Clynnog Fawr St Beuno &#124; National Churches Trust</span></span></span>

The county’s own folklore literature also points to a much wider watery tradition. A proofread page of *Llên Gwerin Sir Gaernarfon* lists a chapter on wells, lakes and rivers, naming many local wells, including Beuno’s Well, Mary’s Well, Garmon’s Well, Cybi’s Well, Llewelyn’s Well and others.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cy.wikisource.org/wiki/Tudalen%3ALlen_gwerin_Sir_Gaernarfon.djvu/17" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cy.wikisource.org">[Wikisource]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cy.wikisource.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Tudalen:Llen gwerin Sir Gaernarfon.djvu/17</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Tudalen:Llen gwerin Sir Gaernarfon.djvu/17</span></span></span> For readers, the takeaway is that Caernarfonshire’s holy wells were not isolated curiosities. They formed a network of named, remembered places where water, saints, healing and local identity met.

## Witches, omens and protective customs

The county’s printed folklore record shows a broad range of household and supernatural belief: mermaids, corpse birds, corpse candles, elf-fire, nightmares, dreams, charms, wart cures, the evil eye, eagle charming, divination and prayers for vengeance all appear in the contents summary of *Llên Gwerin Sir Gaernarfon*.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cy.wikisource.org/wiki/Tudalen%3ALlen_gwerin_Sir_Gaernarfon.djvu/17" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cy.wikisource.org">[Wikisource]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cy.wikisource.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Tudalen:Llen gwerin Sir Gaernarfon.djvu/17</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Tudalen:Llen gwerin Sir Gaernarfon.djvu/17</span></span></span> That list matters because it prevents a narrow “dragons and fairies only” view of Caernarfonshire. Local folklore also lived in illness, luck, animals, weather, death warnings and everyday remedies.

Witch figures in the county tend to be attached to place and social fear rather than formal witch-trial history. Canthrig Bwt is the most vivid example: a dangerous female being under a stone, associated with missing children and violent community response. Modern retellings sometimes soften or reinterpret her, but the older pattern is clear enough: she is a boundary figure, part giantess, part witch, part child-snatcher.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://lindseycolbourne.com/stori-canthrig-bwt" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: lindseycolbourne.com">[Lindsey Colbourne (Heledd Wen)]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">lindseycolbourne.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on lindseycolbourne.com.</span></span></span><span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://visitnorthwales.co.uk/the-cannibal-witch-of-llanberis/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: visitnorthwales.co.uk">[Visit North Wales]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">visitnorthwales.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Visit North Wales The Cannibal Witch of Llanberis</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Visit North Wales The Cannibal Witch of Llanberis</span></span></span>

Black-dog traditions are more difficult to pin firmly to Caernarfonshire than to some English counties or north-east Wales. The Welsh black dog figure is usually called the Dog of Darkness, a spectral hound with red eyes, and is part of a wider British and Welsh death-omen tradition.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gwyllgi" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wikipedia.org.</span></span></span> For Caernarfonshire, it is safer to treat black dogs as a wider Welsh motif that may illuminate local night-road fears, not as one of the county’s best-attested named legends unless a specific local account is being discussed.

## Seasonal customs and chapel culture

Caernarfonshire folklore is not only supernatural. It also includes the customs that shaped winter, worship and community performance. One important Welsh Christmas custom is the plygain, an early-morning or night-time carol service. A digitised copy of *Welsh Folk Customs* notes evidence from Caernarvonshire, Merioneth and Montgomeryshire that parish church plygain services attracted both church and chapel people, making them one of the occasions when religious divisions were temporarily set aside.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/stream/in.gov.ignca.29074/29074_djvu.txt" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Internet Archive Full text of &quot;Welsh folk customs</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Internet Archive Full text of &quot;Welsh folk customs</span></span></span>

That detail is valuable because it places the county’s custom culture in real social life. In a strongly religious and Welsh-speaking region, a Christmas carol service was not just entertainment. It was a meeting point between parish, chapel, song, local poets and seasonal festivity.

The Mari Lwyd, by contrast, is much more strongly associated with South Wales, especially Glamorgan, Monmouthshire and Carmarthenshire. Museum Wales describes it as a Christmas wassailing custom involving a decorated horse’s skull carried door to door, with improvised verse contests at the threshold, but also states that apart from one or two northern sightings it remained mainly a South Wales custom.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://museum.wales/articles/1187/Christmas-Traditions-The-Mari-Lwyd/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: museum.wales">[Museum Wales]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">museum.wales</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Wales Christmas Traditions: The Mari Lwyd &#124; Museum Wales</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wales Christmas Traditions: The Mari Lwyd &#124; Museum Wales</span></span></span> This distinction is useful for Caernarfonshire: modern Welsh culture may celebrate the Mari Lwyd nationally, but it should not be presented as a core old Caernarfonshire custom in the same way as plygain or local well traditions.

## The collectors who preserved the county’s folklore

Caernarfonshire is unusually lucky in having a named county folklore collection: *Llên Gwerin Sir Gaernarfon*, by John Jones, known as Myrddin Fardd. Library records describe it as a Welsh-language work on the folklore of Caernarfonshire, published at Caernarfon around 1908, and HathiTrust lists its subject directly as folklore of Caernarvonshire.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://catalog.hathitrust.org/Record/100794773" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: catalog.hathitrust.org">[HathiTrust]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">catalog.hathitrust.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on hathitrust.org.</span></span></span><span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://onlinebooks.library.upenn.edu/webbin/book/lookupid?key=olbp100998" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: onlinebooks.library.upenn.edu">[Online Books Page]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">onlinebooks.library.upenn.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on upenn.edu.</span></span></span>

The National Library of Wales also records an 1880 manuscript essay on Caernarfonshire folklore, submitted to the National Eisteddfod at Caernarfon and identified with the antiquary William Jones, “Bleddyn”.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archives.library.wales/index.php/llen-gwerin-sir-gaernarfon" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.library.wales">[NLW Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.library.wales</span><span class="citation-popover-title">llen gwerin sir gaernarfon</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">llen gwerin sir gaernarfon</span></span></span> This tells us that county folklore was being actively gathered, arranged and valued during the late nineteenth century, a period when oral memory, Welsh-language scholarship and local antiquarian pride overlapped.

## How Caernarfonshire retells its legends today

Modern Caernarfonshire folklore is highly visible because many of its places are visitable. National Trust pages tell the stories of Gelert and the dragons for walkers around Beddgelert and Craflwyn. This retelling is not merely commercial. It keeps local stories in circulation, but it also changes them. Tourism favours clear plots, named locations and walkable routes. A messy cluster of fairy beliefs becomes “look out for the fairies beside this lake”. A complex medieval dragon prophecy becomes a family-friendly hill walk. A tragic hound tale becomes a memorial path. That makes the stories easier to share, but it can flatten uncertainty.

The best way to enjoy Caernarfonshire folklore is therefore double-sighted. Let the stories animate the place, but keep the layers visible. Dinas Emrys is a hillfort, a literary dragon site, an archaeological puzzle and a national symbol. Beddgelert is a village, a tourist legend, a moral tale and a beloved dog story. St Beuno’s Well is a physical spring, a Christian healing site, a pilgrim stop and part of a broader water-belief network. Yr Wyddfa is a mountain, a national landmark, a giant’s grave in legend and a living focus for Welsh identity.

## Why the county’s folklore still matters

Caernarfonshire’s legends endure because they make landscape memorable. They turn a hill into the dragon’s lair, a grave into a lesson about rash judgement, a well into a place of healing hope, a summit into a giant’s cairn and a lonely pass into a threshold between ordinary travel and the supernatural.

They also show how Welsh identity is made locally. The red dragon is national, but Dinas Emrys gives it a particular hill. Arthur is pan-British, but Yr Wyddfa and Bwlch y Saethau give him a Caernarfonshire horizon. Fairy belief is found across Wales, but Llyn Cwellyn, the Carneddau and Eryri’s misted tracks make it feel native to this county’s terrain. St Beuno belongs to Welsh saintly tradition, but Clynnog Fawr gives his cult stone, water, chest and pilgrim road.

The result is a county folklore that is both intimate and large-scale. It can be as small as a child dipped in a well, as domestic as a Christmas carol gathering, as frightening as a witch beneath a stone, and as grand as dragons fighting beneath a mountain. Caernarfonshire’s mystery is not a single monster or legend. It is the way old stories keep finding exact places to live.

    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      var active = button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button') === marketId;
      button.setAttribute('aria-pressed', active ? 'true' : 'false');
    });
    section.setAttribute('data-ebay-selected-market', marketId);
    if (persist) {
      try { if (window.localStorage) localStorage.setItem('phoenix-ebay-market', marketId); } catch (err) {}
    }
    closeMenu(section);
  }
  sections.forEach(function (section) {
    if (section.getAttribute('data-ebay-localized-init') === '1') return;
    section.setAttribute('data-ebay-localized-init', '1');
    var available = availableMarkets(section);
    var marketId = defaultMarket;
    if (window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation && window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation.inferMarket) {
      marketId = window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation.inferMarket(available, {
        storageKey: 'phoenix-ebay-market',
        defaultMarket: defaultMarket
      });
    } else if (available.indexOf(defaultMarket)< 0) {
      marketId = available[0] || defaultMarket;
    }
    var select = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-select]');
    if (select) {
      select.addEventListener('change', function () {
        applyMarket(section, select.value, true);
      });
    }
    var trigger = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-trigger]');
    var menu = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-menu]');
    if (trigger && menu) {
      trigger.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
        event.stopPropagation();
        var expanded = trigger.getAttribute('aria-expanded') === 'true';
        menu.hidden = expanded;
        trigger.setAttribute('aria-expanded', expanded ? 'false' : 'true');
        var picker = trigger.closest('.fr-ebay-market-picker');
        if (picker) picker.classList.toggle('fr-ebay-market-picker--open', !expanded);
      });
      Array.prototype.slice.call(menu.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-option]')).forEach(function (button) {
        button.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
          event.stopPropagation();
          applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-option'), true);
        });
      });
      document.addEventListener('click', function () { closeMenu(section); });
    }
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      button.addEventListener('click', function () {
        applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button'), true);
      });
    });
    applyMarket(section, marketId, false);
