## Introduction

The Wrekin giant story is [Shropshire]({{ 'why-shropshires-hills-are-full-of/' | relative_url }})’s classic comic landscape legend: a furious Welsh giant sets out to flood Shrewsbury by blocking the River Severn, but a quick-thinking cobbler near Wellington tricks him into giving up. The giant drops his load of earth where he stands, making the Wrekin; the mud scraped from his boots becomes the smaller Ercall beside it. The tale matters because it turns one of Shropshire’s most visible hills into a joke about local cleverness, trade, distance and county pride rather than a grand heroic battle. Its best-known printed form goes back at least to Charlotte Sophia Burne’s Victorian collection *Shropshire Folk-Lore*, and it is still retold today in local heritage, tourism and children’s storytelling.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://sacred-texts.com/neu/eng/efft/efft17.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sacred-texts.com">[sacred-texts.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sacred-texts.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on sacred-texts.com.</span></span></span>

## The giant’s plan to flood Shrewsbury

In the traditional version, the giant has a grudge against Shrewsbury and decides to take revenge by damming the Severn. This detail is important: Shrewsbury’s old town is famously shaped by the river’s loop, so the legend uses a real feature of the town’s geography as the stage for an impossible act of giant engineering. The giant does not attack the town with a club or fight a saint. He tries to alter the landscape.

The nineteenth-century telling printed in *English Fairy and Other Folk Tales* begins with “a wicked old giant in Wales” who hates the mayor and people of Shrewsbury. He carries a huge spadeful of earth towards the town, intending to fill up the Severn and drown them out. But he loses his way and ends up near Wellington, already exhausted by his load.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://sacred-texts.com/neu/eng/efft/efft17.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sacred-texts.com">[Internet Sacred Text Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sacred-texts.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on sacred-texts.com.</span></span></span>

That route gives the tale its Shropshire shape. Wales is imagined as the giant’s home ground, Shrewsbury as the threatened county town, Wellington as the saving place, and the Wrekin as the visible proof left behind. The story works because all these places sit within a reader’s mental map of Shropshire and the Welsh border. It is not a vague “giant made a hill” tale; it is a local explanation for why this particular hill stands where it does.

Modern local retellings sometimes sharpen the motive. Discovering Shropshire’s History gives a darker variant in which the giant had demanded young women from Shrewsbury as tribute, then turns to revenge when the townspeople refuse. That version makes the giant more monstrous, but the ending remains comic: he is defeated not by strength but by a cobbler’s improvised lie.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.discovershropshire.org.uk/html/search/verb/GetRecord/theme-20061005103037" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: discovershropshire.org.uk">[Discover Shropshire]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">discovershropshire.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Discover Shropshire Welcome to Discovering Shropshire&#x27;s History</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Discover Shropshire Welcome to Discovering Shropshire&#x27;s History</span></span></span>

## The cobbler’s trick

The hero of the story is not a knight, a saint or a warrior. He is a working man with a bag of old shoes. In Burne’s collected version, the cobbler lives at Wellington and walks to Shrewsbury once a fortnight to collect boots and shoes for repair. When the giant asks how far it is to Shrewsbury, the cobbler realises the danger and pretends that the town is impossibly distant. To prove it, he shows the giant the sack of worn-out footwear and claims he has worn them all out since leaving Shrewsbury.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://sacred-texts.com/neu/eng/efft/efft17.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sacred-texts.com">[Internet Sacred Text Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sacred-texts.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on sacred-texts.com.</span></span></span>

The joke depends on several neat reversals. The giant is physically vast but mentally slow. The cobbler is small, tired and ordinary, yet he understands people better than the giant does. The sack that should mark him as a humble tradesman becomes his weapon. He does not need magic; he needs nerve, timing and a convincing prop.

The giant believes him. Too weary to go on, he drops the earth where he stands, scrapes his boots on the spade and returns to Wales. The pile of earth becomes the Wrekin, and the boot-scrapings become the Ercall. This is one of the pleasures of the tale: it explains not just the main hill but the smaller neighbouring hill as an afterthought, as though even a giant’s dirty boots could change the map.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://sacred-texts.com/neu/eng/efft/efft17.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sacred-texts.com">[Internet Sacred Text Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sacred-texts.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on sacred-texts.com.</span></span></span>

There is also a quietly local economic joke. In one version, the cobbler is horrified because Shrewsbury contains his customers; saving the town means saving his trade. Modern retellings keep that comic practicality alive, often presenting the cobbler as someone whose concern for ordinary business becomes unexpectedly heroic.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://originalshrewsbury.co.uk/myths-legends-and-folktales-of-shropshire/the-wrekin-giant-how-a-cobbler-saved-shrewsbury" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: originalshrewsbury.co.uk">[Original Shrewsbury]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">originalshrewsbury.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on originalshrewsbury.co.uk.</span></span></span>

## How old and well-attested is the story?

The Wrekin giant story is well attested as a Shropshire tradition, but readers should be careful about what “old” means here. The strongest documentary anchor is Victorian rather than medieval. Charlotte Sophia Burne’s *Shropshire Folk-Lore: A Sheaf of Gleanings*, published in 1883, is the key county collection associated with the tale; later online folklore indexes identify Burne’s book as the source for the Wrekin origin story on pages 2–3.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/details/shropshirefolkl00burngoog" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on archive.org.</span></span></span>

The tale also entered wider English folklore publishing. Joseph Jacobs and other late nineteenth-century compilers were interested in preserving and popularising English tales at a time when collectors worried that oral traditions were being lost through social change, schooling, print culture, railways and urbanisation. The Pitt Rivers Museum’s discussion of Jacobs notes that many of his tales came from fellow folklorists, including Charlotte Burne, and that Jacobs sometimes altered tales to make them readable for children.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://england.prm.ox.ac.uk/englishness-Joseph-Jacobs.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: england.prm.ox.ac.uk">[england.prm.ox.ac.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">england.prm.ox.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Joseph Jacobs</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Joseph Jacobs</span></span></span>

That matters because the Wrekin story sits between oral tradition and printed literary folklore. It was almost certainly not invented from nothing by a modern tourist board, but the exact wording now quoted online often descends through Victorian and later retellings. Names, motives and comic details vary. Some versions call the giant simply a Welsh giant; others name him Gwendol Wrekin ap Shenkin ap Mynyddmawr. Some say he plans to drown Shrewsbury; others say he means to bury it under a mound of soil or ruin it by damming the Severn.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://sacred-texts.com/neu/eng/efft/efft17.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sacred-texts.com">[sacred-texts.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sacred-texts.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on sacred-texts.com.</span></span></span>

So the fairest reading is this: the story is a strongly established Shropshire landscape legend with nineteenth-century printed evidence and likely older oral roots, but its familiar modern form is shaped by collectors, children’s books, heritage retellings and local pride.

## Why the Wrekin attracts giant stories

The Wrekin is exactly the kind of hill that invites a giant legend. It rises sharply from lower ground near Wellington and is treated today by the Shropshire Hills National Landscape as Shropshire’s iconic hill, surrounded by lower country and giving wide views across the county and beyond. Its visibility helps explain why the story feels so natural: a hill that dominates the horizon asks to be explained.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.shropshirehills-nl.org.uk/a-special-place/management-plan/wrekin-forest" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: shropshirehills-nl.org.uk">[Shropshire Hills National Landscape Team]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">shropshirehills-nl.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on shropshirehills-nl.org.uk.</span></span></span>

A Telford and Wrekin hillfort leaflet calls the Wrekin “Wellington’s little mountain” and describes it as rising from the flat ground of the Severn flood plain, visible from around the county and long valued for views, adventure and local belonging. The same leaflet also notes the local tradition that a person is not a true Salopian until they have passed through the Needle’s Eye, a cleft near the summit.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.telford.gov.uk/media/2ubaphe4/wrekin_hillfort_leaflet_v3.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: telford.gov.uk">[Telford and Wrekin Council]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">telford.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Telford and Wrekin Council</span></span></span>

That combination is powerful folklore fuel. The hill is prominent, climbable, named in local sayings, tied to identity and dotted with features that invite stories: the Ercall, the Needle’s Eye, Heaven Gate, Hell Gate and Raven’s Bowl. Discovering Shropshire’s History records several Wrekin stories, including the flood tale, a two-giants origin tale, and traditions attached to the hillfort and summit features.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.discovershropshire.org.uk/html/search/verb/GetRecord/theme-20061005103037" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: discovershropshire.org.uk">[Discover Shropshire]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">discovershropshire.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Discover Shropshire Welcome to Discovering Shropshire&#x27;s History</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Discover Shropshire Welcome to Discovering Shropshire&#x27;s History</span></span></span>

The geology tells a different story, but it does not make the legend less interesting. Shropshire Geological Society is clear that the Wrekin is not a volcano, although some of its rocks are volcanic in origin, and that the nearby Ercall exposes ancient rocks and later Cambrian sediments. Folklore does not compete with that geology as a scientific account; it answers a different human question: what kind of place does this hill feel like, and why does it matter to people who live around it?<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://shropshiregeology.org.uk/the-wrekin/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: shropshiregeology.org.uk">[Shropshire Geological Society]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">shropshiregeology.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Shropshire Geological Society The Wrekin &#124; Shropshire Geological Society</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Shropshire Geological Society The Wrekin &#124; Shropshire Geological Society</span></span></span>

## What the story says about Shropshire

The Wrekin giant story is funny, but it is not trivial. It gives Shropshire a landscape origin tale in which the county is saved by wit rather than force. The threatened place is Shrewsbury, the county town; the saving intelligence comes from Wellington; the failed attacker comes from over the Welsh border; the resulting landmark becomes a shared symbol for the county.

This border element should not be read too heavily as anti-Welsh hostility. Many English and Welsh border stories make comic use of neighbouring places, accents, roads and rivalries. In this tale, “Wales” is the giant’s distant home and Shropshire is the threatened everyday world of markets, customers and roads. The joke works because the border is near enough to be familiar and far enough to feel legendary.

The cobbler also gives the tale its social flavour. He is not noble, but he is useful. He travels between Wellington and Shrewsbury, knows the road, serves customers and carries visible evidence of labour. In a county whose folklore often clings to tracks, markets, hills and parish identities, that is a very Shropshire kind of hero.

The story’s afterlife also shows how a local legend becomes public identity. The Wrekin remains a major recreational and heritage landscape, with high levels of use by walkers and visitors, strong cultural links to Wellington, and a place in wider Shropshire Hills management. The legend gives that modern landscape an easy doorway for children, visitors and residents: climb the hill, see the Ercall, look towards Shrewsbury, and the comic geography suddenly makes sense.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.shropshirehills-nl.org.uk/a-special-place/management-plan/wrekin-forest" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: shropshirehills-nl.org.uk">[Shropshire Hills National Landscape Team]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">shropshirehills-nl.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on shropshirehills-nl.org.uk.</span></span></span>

## Old tale, modern retellings

Today the Wrekin giant is retold in heritage pages, local tourism, school-friendly folklore and public storytelling. Original Shrewsbury presents the story as “How a cobbler saved Shrewsbury”, keeping the giant’s comically oversized name and the cobbler’s shoe trick while adapting the dialogue for a modern reader. Discovering Shropshire’s History presents the tale in a more heritage-style summary, linking it with other Wrekin legends and local sayings such as “all around the Wrekin”.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://originalshrewsbury.co.uk/myths-legends-and-folktales-of-shropshire/the-wrekin-giant-how-a-cobbler-saved-shrewsbury" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: originalshrewsbury.co.uk">[Original Shrewsbury]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">originalshrewsbury.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on originalshrewsbury.co.uk.</span></span></span>

These modern versions are not all identical, and that is normal for folklore. The essentials remain stable: a giant wants to use earth or stones to interfere with the Severn; he asks the way near Wellington; a cobbler deceives him with worn-out shoes; the dumped load becomes the Wrekin; the boot-scrapings become the Ercall. Around that core, storytellers add motives, names, dialogue and comic business.

The best way to read the tale is not as evidence that people literally believed a giant made the hill, nor as a mere children’s story detached from place. It is a place-based traditional explanation, preserved in print by Victorian folklore collecting and still kept alive because the Wrekin is visible, beloved and easy to imagine as something too large for ordinary history. In Shropshire folklore, that is the hill’s special gift: it turns a skyline into a story about quick wits, local roads and a county saved by a sack of shoes.
