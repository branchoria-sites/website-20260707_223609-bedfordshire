## Introduction

Molly Leigh is remembered as the “witch of Burslem”, but the strongest evidence points to something more human and more troubling: a real eighteenth-century [Staffordshire]({{ 'why-staffordshire-feels-ancient-and/' | relative_url }}) woman whose grave, reputation and oddities were turned into legend after her death. Margaret “Molly” Leigh was buried at St John the Baptist’s churchyard in Burslem on 1 April 1748, and her tomb is still pointed out because it lies on a north-south axis, unlike the usual east-west Christian alignment. The local story says this was done to keep her ghost quiet; the documentary record suggests she was also a property-owning woman who left charitable provision for poor people in Burslem and Sneyd.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://saintjohnsburslem.co.uk/molly-leigh-the-witch-of-burslem/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: saintjohnsburslem.co.uk">[saintjohnsburslem.co.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">saintjohnsburslem.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">SJCCMolly Leigh</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Witch of Burslem - SJCCHer tomb is just outside the South porch of the church. Her burial is recorded in the registers as April 1st 1...</span></span></span>

That tension is why Molly Leigh matters in Staffordshire folklore. Her legend has all the memorable ingredients of a witch tale: a solitary woman, a black bird, sour ale, a hostile parson, a disturbed grave and a churchyard dare. Yet it also preserves the harsher social memory behind witch accusations: how eccentricity, independence, disability, gender and local resentment could combine to turn a neighbour into a monster in popular memory.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://saintjohnsburslem.co.uk/molly-leigh-the-witch-of-burslem/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: saintjohnsburslem.co.uk">[saintjohnsburslem.co.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">saintjohnsburslem.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">SJCCMolly Leigh</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Witch of Burslem - SJCCHer tomb is just outside the South porch of the church. ## The real woman in Burslem records

Molly Leigh belongs first to Burslem, one of the historic Staffordshire Potteries towns now within Stoke-on-Trent. St John the Baptist’s Church, on Cross Hill, is the physical centre of the legend. The church itself has deep local roots: Burslem was a chapelry of Stoke-upon-Trent before becoming a parish in the nineteenth century, and St John’s retained older churchyard memory even as the Potteries grew around it.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St_John_the_Baptist%27s_Church%2C_Burslem" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">St John the Baptist&#x27;s Church, Burslem</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">St John the Baptist&#x27;s Church, Burslem</span></span></span>

The church’s own account identifies her as “Margaret Leigh of Jackfields”, better known as Molly Leigh, and says her tomb stands just outside the south porch. It also states that her burial is recorded in the registers as 1 April 1748. That date matters because it anchors the story in parish record rather than pure invention: Molly was not just a name in a ghost tale, but a person buried in a specific Staffordshire churchyard.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://saintjohnsburslem.co.uk/molly-leigh-the-witch-of-burslem/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: saintjohnsburslem.co.uk">[SJCC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">saintjohnsburslem.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">SJCCMolly Leigh</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Witch of Burslem - SJCCHer tomb is just outside the South porch of the church. Her burial is recorded in the registers as April 1st 1...</span></span></span>

Local historical accounts commonly place her birth around 1685 and associate her with Jackfield or Hamil Road, on the edge of Burslem. Later retellings describe her as a dairy woman who sold milk, lived independently, and kept a blackbird, jackdaw, crow or raven-like companion. These details vary from version to version, which is typical of oral legend, but the broad shape is consistent: an unmarried or solitary woman, connected with animals and the margins of the town, became an object of suspicion.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://thelocalmythstorian.com/hex-hoax" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: thelocalmythstorian.com">[thelocalmythstorian.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">thelocalmythstorian.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">hex hoax</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Local MythstorianHex &amp; Hoaxher grave is there at St John&#x27;s Church in Burslem to this day, she died in late March 1748 and was buried...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 1748</span></span></span>

The strongest corrective to the “wicked witch” image is her will. Local reporting on the will presents Molly as a woman with property and charitable intentions, not as a destitute outcast. The will is reported to have included provision for bread for poor inhabitants and widows of Sneyd and Burslem, and later for a hospital in Burslem for poor women, though there is no evidence that such a hospital was ever built.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.stokesentinel.co.uk/news/history/full-details-last--testament-2269023" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: stokesentinel.co.uk">[stokesentinel.co.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">stokesentinel.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">full details last testament 2269023</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Stoke SentinelFull details of last will &amp; testament of Burslem witch Molly...2 Dec 2018 — Molly also wished for her fortune to be used t...</span></span></span>

This does not mean the whole legend can be neatly disproved. Folklore often grows in the space between a few hard facts and many remembered stories. In Molly Leigh’s case, the hard facts are unusually helpful: Burslem, St John’s, a recorded burial, an unusual grave, and a will showing property and charity. The supernatural explanation belongs to legend; the social reality belongs to local history.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://saintjohnsburslem.co.uk/molly-leigh-the-witch-of-burslem/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: saintjohnsburslem.co.uk">[saintjohnsburslem.co.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">saintjohnsburslem.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">SJCCMolly Leigh</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Witch of Burslem - SJCCHer tomb is just outside the South porch of the church. ## Blackbird, ale and grave stories

The most famous version of the legend centres on Parson Spencer, usually described as the minister of St John’s. In the story, he dislikes Molly and claims that her bird has been sent to spoil his beer at the Turk’s Head. Some accounts say the bird sat on the pub sign and turned the ale sour; others widen the accusation, blaming Molly for illness, misfortune or spoiled milk around Burslem.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://saintjohnsburslem.co.uk/molly-leigh-the-witch-of-burslem/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: saintjohnsburslem.co.uk">[SJCC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">saintjohnsburslem.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">SJCCMolly Leigh</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Witch of Burslem - SJCCHer tomb is just outside the South porch of the church. Her burial is recorded in the registers as April 1st 1...</span></span></span>

The bird is one reason the tale has lasted. A blackbird or jackdaw perched on a woman’s shoulder is a perfect folklore image: ordinary enough to be believable, strange enough to seem magical to suspicious neighbours. In English witch traditions, animals often become “familiars”, creatures imagined as supernatural helpers. In Molly’s story, however, the bird may simply have been a pet transformed by gossip into evidence.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molly_Leigh" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Molly Leigh</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Molly Leigh</span></span></span>

After Molly’s burial, the legend says the trouble did not stop. One widely repeated account says mourners returned to her house at Jackfield only to find her ghost sitting by the fire or knitting as if she had never died. Another says sightings of her spirit led Spencer to gather other clergy, open the grave, and rebury her body differently to stop the haunting.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.thepotteries.org/tour/049.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: thepotteries.org">[The Potteries]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">thepotteries.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on thepotteries.org.</span></span></span>

The grave is the story’s most durable object. St John’s Church says Molly’s tomb is outside the south porch, and local accounts repeatedly note that it lies at right angles to the other burials. The church’s page makes an important point: if Molly had truly been treated as a convicted witch, it would be odd for her to have received a Christian burial in consecrated ground. That does not disprove later fear around her grave, but it weakens the idea that she was formally recognised as a witch in any legal or church sense.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://saintjohnsburslem.co.uk/molly-leigh-the-witch-of-burslem/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: saintjohnsburslem.co.uk">[SJCC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">saintjohnsburslem.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">SJCCMolly Leigh</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Witch of Burslem - SJCCHer tomb is just outside the South porch of the church. Her burial is recorded in the registers as April 1st 1...</span></span></span>

The timing also matters. The Witchcraft Act of 1735 had changed the legal landscape before Molly’s death, replacing earlier witchcraft law with a focus on punishing people who claimed magical powers or pretended to practise witchcraft. St John’s own account notes that the old legal framework for condemning witches had already been repealed before Molly died. Molly’s “witchcraft” therefore belongs less to courtroom history than to reputation, gossip and posthumous legend.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://saintjohnsburslem.co.uk/molly-leigh-the-witch-of-burslem/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: saintjohnsburslem.co.uk">[SJCC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">saintjohnsburslem.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">SJCCMolly Leigh</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Witch of Burslem - SJCCHer tomb is just outside the South porch of the church. ## Was Molly Leigh a witch or a scapegoat?

The most responsible answer is that Molly Leigh was a real woman later remembered through a witch legend. There is no strong evidence that she practised witchcraft, cursed Burslem, or used a bird to sour ale. There is stronger evidence that she was socially unusual, independent, perhaps physically marked by illness in some retellings, and wealthy or secure enough to attract resentment.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.theredhairedstokie.co.uk/molly-leigh-the-true-history-of-an-independent-wealthy-land-owning-woman/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theredhairedstokie.co.uk">[theredhairedstokie.co.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theredhairedstokie.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Red Haired Stokie Molly Leigh</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Red Haired Stokie Molly Leigh</span></span></span>

The scapegoat reading fits the pattern of the tale. The accusations attach to everyday misfortunes: bad beer, spoiled milk, sickness, bad luck. These are exactly the kinds of events that communities often explain through blame when scientific, medical or economic explanations are weak, distant or unsatisfying. The “witch” label gives shape to anxiety, but it can also punish someone who does not fit social expectations.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://thelocalmythstorian.com/hex-hoax" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: thelocalmythstorian.com">[The Local Mythstorian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">thelocalmythstorian.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">hex hoax</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Local MythstorianHex &amp; Hoaxher grave is there at St John&#x27;s Church in Burslem to this day, she died in late March 1748 and was buried...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 1748</span></span></span>

Molly’s gender is central. The tale repeatedly presents her as solitary, sharp-tempered, unattractive, animal-associated and difficult. Those are not proofs of witchcraft; they are social warning labels. They show how a woman who was independent, elderly, unmarried, visibly different or unwilling to defer to local authority could become vulnerable to hostile storytelling.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molly_Leigh" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Molly Leigh</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Molly Leigh</span></span></span>

The role of Parson Spencer is equally revealing. Whether every detail about him is historically secure or not, the legend makes him the respectable male authority who interprets Molly’s behaviour as dangerous. The tale therefore stages a conflict between a marginal woman and the church-backed voice of the community. That is why modern retellings often turn the story around: Molly is no longer simply the witch of Burslem, but a woman wronged by rumour.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://saintjohnsburslem.co.uk/molly-leigh-the-witch-of-burslem/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: saintjohnsburslem.co.uk">[SJCC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">saintjohnsburslem.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">SJCCMolly Leigh</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Witch of Burslem - SJCCHer tomb is just outside the South porch of the church. ## Why the Burslem churchyard setting matters

Molly Leigh’s story would not have survived in the same way without a visible place to visit. The grave gives the legend a stage. Many Staffordshire legends attach to landscape features such as pools, wells, stones or old roads; Molly’s attaches to a churchyard in a pottery town. That makes it unusually urban and local: not a remote moorland apparition, but a story embedded in Burslem’s parish memory.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://saintjohnsburslem.co.uk/molly-leigh-the-witch-of-burslem/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: saintjohnsburslem.co.uk">[SJCC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">saintjohnsburslem.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">SJCCMolly Leigh</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Witch of Burslem - SJCCHer tomb is just outside the South porch of the church. The tomb’s alignment is crucial because it turns a burial into a question. Visitors do not need to know the full story to notice that the grave is different. Folklore thrives on visible oddities: a stone turned the wrong way, a pool with a dark reputation, a road said to be haunted, a churchyard dare whispered by children. The physical difference invites explanation, and the explanation becomes the legend.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://thejournalofantiquities.com/2022/08/04/st-johns-church-and-witchs-grave-at-burslem-stoke-on-trent-north-staffordshire/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: thejournalofantiquities.com">[The Journal Of Antiquities]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">thejournalofantiquities.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">st johns church and witchs grave at burslem stoke on trent north staffordshire</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">st johns church and witchs grave at burslem stoke on trent north staffordshire</span></span></span>

The setting also links Molly to Burslem’s identity as “the Mother Town” of the Potteries. Burslem became famous through ceramics, canals and industrial growth, but Molly’s story preserves a slightly earlier and rougher social world: smallholdings, parish authority, alehouses, local gossip and reputational danger. It gives Staffordshire folklore a human face within an industrial landscape.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Burslem" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wikipedia.org.</span></span></span>

There is a second layer of memory in the churchyard itself. Accounts of St John’s note other notable burials and monuments, and local descriptions place Molly’s tomb among a churchyard full of civic, religious and pottery-era associations. The witch legend therefore sits beside more respectable forms of memory, which is part of its force: Molly is both an outcast figure and one of the most visited names in the churchyard.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St_John_the_Baptist%27s_Church%2C_Burslem" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">St John the Baptist&#x27;s Church, Burslem</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">St John the Baptist&#x27;s Church, Burslem</span></span></span>

## How the legend is retold today

For many people in Stoke-on-Trent and north Staffordshire, Molly Leigh is not an obscure antiquarian reference but a childhood dare. That gives it the qualities of living folklore rather than dead legend: each generation can repeat it, alter it slightly, and test its fear in the same place.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://thelocalmythstorian.com/hex-hoax" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: thelocalmythstorian.com">[The Local Mythstorian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">thelocalmythstorian.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">hex hoax</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Local MythstorianHex &amp; Hoaxher grave is there at St John&#x27;s Church in Burslem to this day, she died in late March 1748 and was buried...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 1748</span></span></span>

## What the legend says about suspicion

Molly Leigh’s legend matters because it is not simply a spooky story with a grave attached. It is a Staffordshire case study in how suspicion works. The tale takes ordinary ingredients — a woman living alone, a pet bird, a dispute with a clergyman, a disliked personality, a visible grave — and turns them into a supernatural explanation for communal unease.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molly_Leigh" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Molly Leigh</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Molly Leigh</span></span></span>

The most revealing part is the mismatch between record and reputation. The record gives us a burial, property, charity and a named place. The legend gives us haunting, exhumation, a bird sealed in a coffin and a grave turned against the Christian norm. Neither side can be ignored. The record prevents the tale from floating away into pure fantasy; the legend shows how powerfully a community can mythologise a real person.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://saintjohnsburslem.co.uk/molly-leigh-the-witch-of-burslem/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: saintjohnsburslem.co.uk">[saintjohnsburslem.co.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">saintjohnsburslem.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">SJCCMolly Leigh</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Witch of Burslem - SJCCHer tomb is just outside the South porch of the church. Her burial is recorded in the registers as April 1st 1...</span></span></span>

For Staffordshire folklore, Molly Leigh stands between older witch-belief and modern local identity. She is not a Pendle-style trial figure and not a purely invented Halloween witch. She is a remembered Burslem woman whose life was filtered through rumour, churchyard storytelling and later heritage. That makes her especially valuable for readers trying to understand county folklore as lived local memory rather than a catalogue of monsters.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://thelocalmythstorian.com/hex-hoax" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: thelocalmythstorian.com">[The Local Mythstorian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">thelocalmythstorian.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">hex hoax</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Local MythstorianHex &amp; Hoaxher grave is there at St John&#x27;s Church in Burslem to this day, she died in late March 1748 and was buried...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 1748</span></span></span>

The fairest way to tell the story is therefore not to ask whether Molly Leigh really haunted Burslem, but why Burslem needed a story in which she did. The answer lies in the uncomfortable overlap between fear and social control. Molly Leigh’s grave keeps the legend visible; her will keeps the accusation in doubt; and the continuing retellings keep forcing the same question back into view: was Staffordshire remembering a witch, or remembering what it did to a woman it chose to call one?
