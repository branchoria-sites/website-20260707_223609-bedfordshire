## Introduction

Antrim’s fairy traditions in the Glens are not just stories about tiny people in green fields. They are place-rules: do not cut that thorn, do not damage that hill, do not build across that hidden route, do not linger at night where the “Gentry” are believed to pass. The clearest example is Tiveragh above Cushendall, remembered as a fairy hill with hawthorns, lights, music and warnings about disturbing what lives inside the land. Modern heritage pages, local oral-history work and older Ulster folklore collections all preserve the same basic pattern: the fairies of the Glens are attached to named hills, trees, banks, fields and routes, and the stories explain how people should behave around them.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.visitcausewaycoastandglens.com/explore/picture-this-trail/culture/stories" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: visitcausewaycoastandglens.com">[visitcausewaycoastandglens.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">visitcausewaycoastandglens.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Causeway Coast &amp; Glens Stories</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Causeway Coast &amp; Glens Stories</span></span></span>

That makes the Glens of Antrim one of [County Antrim]({{ 'county-antrim/' | relative_url }})’s strongest fairy landscapes. Its folklore is rooted in a dramatic working countryside of steep-sided valleys, farms, old roads, glacial glens, rough upland and coast. The Antrim Coast and Glens Area of Outstanding Natural Beauty was designated in 1988, and official descriptions stress its plateau cut by deep glens, sheltered valleys, moorland, bays, headlands and long cultural history — exactly the sort of threshold landscape in which fairy belief tends to fasten onto particular natural and man-made features.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.daera-ni.gov.uk/articles/antrim-coast-and-glens-aonb" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: daera-ni.gov.uk">[daera-ni.gov.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">daera-ni.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">antrim coast and glens aonb</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">antrim coast and glens aonb</span></span></span>

## Tiveragh Hill and the Gentry

Tiveragh, near Cushendall, is the best-known fairy hill in this slice of Antrim folklore. Local tourism material describes it as a distinctive hill shaped like a camel’s hump; geologically it is treated as the remains of a volcanic plug, but in local story it is “the fairy hill”. The same account says one side is sheep pasture and the other rough ground, and that both good and bad fairies live inside the hill. Halfway up the “good” side stands a fairy tree that people would not dare cut down, especially because the fairies are said to appear around it at Halloween.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.visitcausewaycoastandglens.com/explore/picture-this-trail/culture/stories" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: visitcausewaycoastandglens.com">[Causeway Coast &amp; Glens]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">visitcausewaycoastandglens.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Causeway Coast &amp; Glens Stories</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Causeway Coast &amp; Glens Stories</span></span></span>

The point of the Tiveragh tradition is not simply that fairies live “somewhere nearby”. The hill itself is a dwelling. The tree is a marker. The slope has moral geography: one side safer, one side rougher or more dangerous. The story turns an ordinary visible feature into a map of caution. The warning that bad fairies might lure a visitor into the hill with enchanting music, never to return, belongs to a wider Irish and Ulster pattern in which fairy music is beautiful but risky: to hear it is to be drawn close to another world, and to follow it is to lose ordinary time and safety.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.visitcausewaycoastandglens.com/explore/picture-this-trail/culture/stories" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: visitcausewaycoastandglens.com">[Causeway Coast &amp; Glens]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">visitcausewaycoastandglens.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Causeway Coast &amp; Glens Stories</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Causeway Coast &amp; Glens Stories</span></span></span>

A modern collected source, *Lore of the Land*, gives Tiveragh extra weight because it preserves verse and local memory rather than only a tourist summary. A poem printed there begins, “Tiveragh is a fairy hill and near to Cushendall,” then warns that nobody goes there at night and describes flickering lights moving in and out while people are in bed. It also names two hawthorns growing on the hill, “a white one and a red”.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://niarchive.org/wp-content/uploads/2019/07/Lore-of-the-Land-web-ready.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: niarchive.org">[niarchive.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">niarchive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Lore of the Land web ready</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Lore of the Land web ready</span></span></span>

Those details matter. Lights going in and out make the hill appear inhabited. Night avoidance turns belief into behaviour. The hawthorns give the invisible Gentry a visible address. The poem does not ask the reader to solve whether fairies are “real”; it shows how Tiveragh was understood locally as a place where the land had occupants whose privacy should not be tested.

Another Tiveragh poem in the same collection imagines fairy folk playing hurling by moonlight. The narrator looks over a wall, sees a tiny game under way, and is threatened by a small figure who tells him to get away or risk being blinded. This is a sharp little social rule disguised as comedy: do not stare, do not trespass, do not presume that a human observer has the right to watch fairy business.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://niarchive.org/wp-content/uploads/2019/07/Lore-of-the-Land-web-ready.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: niarchive.org">[niarchive.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">niarchive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Lore of the Land web ready</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Lore of the Land web ready</span></span></span>

## Fairy thorns and rural warnings

The fairy thorn is one of the most practical pieces of Antrim fairy belief. A lone hawthorn or “skeagh” bush in a field, beside a farm, on a mound or near an old site was often treated as more than vegetation. It could be a fairy dwelling, a meeting place, a dancing place or a sign that the ground itself should not be interfered with. In oral-history material from the Glens of Antrim Historical Society, contributors remember thorn bushes that people would not cut because “wild bad luck” would follow, and one account links a large skeagh bush beside a hill and mound to fairy music and dancing on summer evenings.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://antrimhistory.net/oral-history-project/superstitions-customs/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: antrimhistory.net">[antrimhistory.net]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">antrimhistory.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Superstitions &amp; Customs – Glens Of Antrim Historical Society</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Superstitions &amp; Customs – Glens Of Antrim Historical Society</span></span></span>

These warnings were not vague countryside atmosphere. They shaped behaviour. One speaker recalled that certain thorn bushes in isolated fields were “fairy bushes” and would not be cut. Another remembered a tree in the middle of a field that “never was touched” because it was thought unlucky. The same collection includes a story of a family with a skeagh bush at the side of the house: after someone was said to have burnt part of the bush, misfortune was connected with burnt cows. The narrator does not present the tale as courtroom evidence; he frames it as remembered belief, hearsay and family superstition. That uncertainty is part of the tradition’s texture.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://antrimhistory.net/oral-history-project/superstitions-customs/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: antrimhistory.net">[antrimhistory.net]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">antrimhistory.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Superstitions &amp; Customs – Glens Of Antrim Historical Society</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Superstitions &amp; Customs – Glens Of Antrim Historical Society</span></span></span>

The Glens material is especially useful because it shows belief and scepticism sitting side by side. In one Glenariffe story, people thought fairy music was coming from a tree where fairies were supposed to live, but the explanation turned out to be a sailor playing a mouth organ up in the branches. That does not weaken the folklore. It shows how local storytelling worked: people could believe, doubt, laugh and still preserve the warning that certain trees were not ordinary trees.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://antrimhistory.net/oral-history-project/superstitions-customs/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: antrimhistory.net">[antrimhistory.net]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">antrimhistory.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Superstitions &amp; Customs – Glens Of Antrim Historical Society</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Superstitions &amp; Customs – Glens Of Antrim Historical Society</span></span></span>

Older printed folklore confirms that this was not only a late modern memory. Elizabeth Andrews’s *Ulster Folklore*, first published in 1913, records the “respect paid to the fairy thorn” and includes an Antrim railway example from Lenagh, near the Belfast-to-Ballymena line, where an old fort with fairy bushes stood on the intended route. The workers were unwilling to meddle with the fort or bushes; when one man began to cut down a thorn, an accident strengthened the refusal, and the fort was eventually blown up by railway officials, revealing a spearhead and other implements underneath.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/files/37187/37187-h/37187-h.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on gutenberg.org.</span></span></span>

That Lenagh example is just outside the narrow Glens focus, but it helps explain the mechanism at work in Antrim as a whole. Fairy belief could protect old earthworks, bushes and awkward field features because it made interference socially risky. A thorn was not merely inconvenient; it belonged to someone else. A mound was not simply unused land; it might be occupied.

## Fairy roads, houses and land use

The strongest Antrim fairy stories are often about movement. Fairies are not imagined as static decorations in the landscape; they travel, dance, pass through fields, object to blocked ways and react when people build in the wrong place. This is why “fairy roads” and hidden routes matter. A house, barn, byre, path, drain or road can become troublesome if it interrupts unseen traffic.

*Lore of the Land* preserves a striking example involving Jack McBurney, who lived at the foot of Knocklayde in County Antrim. A member of the Gentry is said to have sent word that the door of his barn should be changed because its south-facing position caused “noise and stour” — dust — that troubled him. Jack changed the door to open north, and after that, the story says, everything prospered with him.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://niarchive.org/wp-content/uploads/2019/07/Lore-of-the-Land-web-ready.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: niarchive.org">[niarchive.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">niarchive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Lore of the Land web ready</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Lore of the Land web ready</span></span></span>

The practical lesson is clear: prosperity depends on not aggravating the hidden neighbours. It is a fairy story, but it is also a land-use story. Doors, drains, roofs and paths must be aligned with more than human convenience. Another tale in the same source tells of a builder whose house kept being knocked down by the wee Gentry because the building diverted water down a fairy chimney. He altered the work, and the problem ceased.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.academia.edu/39970452/Lore_of_the_Land_Exploring_folklore_and_the_supernatural_across_the_Causeway_Coast_and_Glens" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: academia.edu">[Academia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">academia.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on academia.edu.</span></span></span>

Glens oral history gives a similar byre example. A fairy woman asks a household to change the arrangement of the byre because water is falling on her children; the family complies, and the fairy later returns in thanks. This is one of the gentler forms of the tradition. The fairies are not simply malicious. They are neighbours with claims of their own, and trouble comes when people ignore the fact that the land has older or hidden patterns of use.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://antrimhistory.net/oral-history-project/superstitions-customs/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: antrimhistory.net">[antrimhistory.net]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">antrimhistory.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Superstitions &amp; Customs – Glens Of Antrim Historical Society</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Superstitions &amp; Customs – Glens Of Antrim Historical Society</span></span></span>

There is a strong heritage consequence here. Across Ireland, folklore around fairy forts, thorns and hidden dwellings has often discouraged people from damaging archaeological or old landscape features. Scholarship and heritage discussion have noted that ringforts and similar sites may have survived partly because people feared disturbing fairy places, even if the exact origin of that fear remains debated.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/343749116_The_Fear_of_Fairy_Forts_Archaeological_Preservation_by_Plague_and_Superstition" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Research Gate(PDF) The Fear of Fairy Forts: Archaeological Preservation</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Research Gate(PDF) The Fear of Fairy Forts: Archaeological Preservation</span></span></span>

For the Glens, the same idea works on a smaller and more intimate scale. A thorn in a field, a mound beside an old road, a hill above Cushendall or a tunnel exposed by quarrying could become part of a moral landscape. The stories did not need a formal law to be effective. They worked through memory, embarrassment, fear of bad luck, family warnings and the repeated phrase: do not touch it.

## Why the Glens make fairy belief feel local

Fairy traditions appear across Ireland and Britain, but the Glens give Antrim’s version a distinctive shape. The landscape is narrow, steep, enclosed and then suddenly open to sea or moor. Official descriptions of the AONB emphasise the Antrim Plateau cut by rivers into deep glens, with enclosed farmland giving way to exposed moorland and coastal headlands. That geography helps explain why fairy stories here often focus on thresholds: the foot of a hill, the edge of a field, the side of a road, the mouth of a tunnel, the wall someone looks over, the door that must be moved.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.daera-ni.gov.uk/articles/antrim-coast-and-glens-aonb" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: daera-ni.gov.uk">[daera-ni.gov.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">daera-ni.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">antrim coast and glens aonb</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">antrim coast and glens aonb</span></span></span>

Tiveragh is powerful because it can be seen and pointed to. A reader does not have to imagine a vague fairy realm; the story names a hill above Cushendall, a tree on its slope, lights at night and music that might lure a person in. Oral-history accounts from the Glens similarly keep belief attached to named or recognisable rural features: Glenariffe trees, Carey thorn bushes, Craigabann, Ballintoy, byres, fields, old roads and farm places.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.visitcausewaycoastandglens.com/explore/picture-this-trail/culture/stories" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: visitcausewaycoastandglens.com">[Causeway Coast &amp; Glens]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">visitcausewaycoastandglens.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Causeway Coast &amp; Glens Stories</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Causeway Coast &amp; Glens Stories</span></span></span>

The Glens also preserve the fairy name “the Gentry”, a respectful term that says a great deal about the relationship. It avoids blunt naming and treats the fairy people as socially superior, or at least as beings who should be handled carefully. *Lore of the Land* places the Gentry at the centre of its fairy section and notes that the collection draws on sources ranging from nineteenth-century *Fairy Annals of Ulster* and Andrews’s early twentieth-century work to Sam Henry, Jim Hunter and twenty-first-century oral-history contributors.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://niarchive.org/wp-content/uploads/2019/07/Lore-of-the-Land-web-ready.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: niarchive.org">[niarchive.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">niarchive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Lore of the Land web ready</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Lore of the Land web ready</span></span></span>

This long chain of collection matters because it shows that the tradition is not simply a recent tourist invention. Tourism now retells Tiveragh in a visitor-friendly way, and modern local-history projects preserve memories in print and online, but the habits behind the stories — avoiding fairy thorns, respecting mounds, treating lights and music with caution, and explaining misfortune as fairy displeasure — belong to a much older Ulster pattern.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.visitcausewaycoastandglens.com/explore/picture-this-trail/culture/stories" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: visitcausewaycoastandglens.com">[visitcausewaycoastandglens.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">visitcausewaycoastandglens.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Causeway Coast &amp; Glens Stories</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Causeway Coast &amp; Glens Stories</span></span></span>

## What readers should take from the tradition today

The fairy hills and thorn trees of the Glens are best understood as folklore of careful behaviour. They are not just charming tales about magical beings. They ask how people live with a landscape that is older than them, stranger than them and not fully theirs to command. In that sense, they sit naturally beside County Antrim’s other place-based legends: the Giant’s Causeway gives geology a giant’s story, while Tiveragh and the fairy thorns give ordinary hills, trees and fields a code of respect.

The stories also show why old folklore remains useful even when belief changes. A modern reader may not believe that fairies live inside Tiveragh or dance around a hawthorn, but the old rules still carry meaning: notice the lone tree before you cut it; ask why a mound has been left untouched; listen to local memory before dismissing a feature as useless; understand that roads, farms and houses were once planned not only around engineering and ownership, but around story, fear, courtesy and inherited caution.

That is why Tiveragh’s fairy tree, the Glens’ skeagh bushes and the warnings about fairy routes still matter within County Antrim folklore. They preserve a way of reading the countryside in which landscape is not empty background. Hills have inhabitants, thorns have guardians, roads have unseen traffic, and the safest person is the one who knows when to leave well alone.
