## Introduction

[Wiltshire]({{ 'wiltshires-legends-written-across-the/' | relative_url }})’s white horses are among the most recognisable examples of a landscape tradition in Britain: enormous hillside figures created by cutting away turf to reveal the bright chalk beneath. They are not simply monuments to look at; they are landmarks where local memory, countryside identity and folklore meet. The county has had at least thirteen recorded white horses, with eight surviving today, and many of the best-known examples date from the late 18th and 19th centuries rather than from prehistoric times.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.wiltshirewhitehorses.org.uk/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wiltshirewhitehorses.org.uk">[Wiltshire White Horses]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wiltshirewhitehorses.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wiltshirewhitehorses.org.uk.</span></span></span>

The horses have gathered stories around them for generations. The most famous legend links the Westbury White Horse with King Alfred the Great and his victory over the Vikings at the Battle of Ethandun in AD 878, although historians have found no firm evidence that the hill figure itself dates from Alfred’s time. This mixture of uncertain origins, local pride and powerful storytelling is exactly what makes Wiltshire’s chalk horses important within the county’s folklore.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Westbury_White_Horse" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Westbury White Horse</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Westbury White Horse</span></span></span>

## The surviving hill figures and their locations

Wiltshire’s chalk downs provide an ideal setting for hill figures. The underlying chalk can be exposed by removing the grass-covered surface, creating a bright image that can be seen from great distances. The figures require continued care because grass, weather and erosion gradually reclaim the exposed chalk.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.wiltshirewhitehorses.org.uk/faqs.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wiltshirewhitehorses.org.uk">[Wiltshire White Horses]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wiltshirewhitehorses.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wiltshirewhitehorses.org.uk.</span></span></span>

The surviving horses form a chain of landmarks across the county:

* **Westbury White Horse** – Cut into Westbury Hill above Bratton, this is the oldest surviving Wiltshire white horse. The present design dates from 1778, when an earlier figure was recut into a new form under the direction of George Gee, replacing what may have been an older local design. It stands beside the Iron Age earthworks of Bratton Camp, a setting that has helped strengthen its association with ancient history and legend.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.hows.org.uk/personal/hillfigs/west/west.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: hows.org.uk">[Hows]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">hows.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Hows Westbury White Horse</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Hows Westbury White Horse</span></span></span>
* **Cherhill White Horse** – Created in 1780 on Cherhill Down near Calne, this is one of the earliest surviving Wiltshire examples. Local tradition credits Dr Christopher Alsop with directing the work, supposedly shouting instructions through a megaphone while workers shaped the figure on the hillside. The horse sits near Oldbury hillfort and the Lansdowne Monument, creating a landscape where prehistoric earthworks, later monuments and folklore overlap.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nationaltrust.org.uk/visit/wiltshire/calstone-and-cherhill-downs/exploring-the-estate-at-calstone-and-cherhill-downs" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nationaltrust.org.uk">[National Trust]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nationaltrust.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Trust Visit Calstone &amp; Cherhill Downs &#124; Wiltshire &#124; National Trust</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Trust Visit Calstone &amp; Cherhill Downs &#124; Wiltshire &#124; National Trust</span></span></span>
* **Marlborough, Alton Barnes, Hackpen, Broad Town and Pewsey horses** – These later figures reflect the popularity of chalk horses as statements of local identity and landscape design during the 19th and 20th centuries. The Pewsey White Horse, created in 1937, replaced an earlier figure from the 18th century, while the Devizes White Horse was created in 1999 as a millennium landmark inspired by the lost earlier horse on Roundway Hill.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.insidewiltshire.co.uk/explore-wiltshire/wiltshire-white-horses/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: insidewiltshire.co.uk">[Inside Wiltshire]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">insidewiltshire.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on insidewiltshire.co.uk.</span></span></span>

The pattern is important: Wiltshire’s horses are not one continuous ancient tradition. They are a collection of different creations made across several centuries, later joined together in public imagination as a single county symbol.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.wiltshirewhitehorses.org.uk/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wiltshirewhitehorses.org.uk">[Wiltshire White Horses]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wiltshirewhitehorses.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wiltshirewhitehorses.org.uk.</span></span></span>

## Legends of kings and ancient memory

The strongest folklore attached to Wiltshire’s white horses is the story of royal victory. The Westbury White Horse has often been said to commemorate Alfred the Great’s defeat of the Viking army of Guthrum at Ethandun in 878. Local versions sometimes add further details, such as stories about the figure’s creation being ordered by Alfred himself.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.visitwiltshire.co.uk/things-to-do/bratton-camp-and-westbury-white-horse-p133343" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: visitwiltshire.co.uk">[Visit Wiltshire]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">visitwiltshire.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Visit Wiltshire Bratton Camp and Westbury White Horse</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Visit WiltshireBratton Camp and Westbury White Horse - Visit Wiltshire...</span></span></span>

The problem is that the evidence does not support the horse being an Anglo-Saxon monument. The earliest known documentary references to the Westbury figure appear much later, and the present horse is an 18th-century recutting. The Alfred connection appears to belong to a later layer of local tradition rather than the original creation of the figure.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Westbury_White_Horse" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Westbury White Horse</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Westbury White Horse</span></span></span>

This does not make the legend meaningless. Folklore often works by attaching older landscapes to memorable stories. Westbury Hill already carried associations with ancient earthworks, military history and dramatic views across the surrounding countryside. Linking the horse with Alfred transformed it from a hillside carving into a symbol of English history and local belonging.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.visitwiltshire.co.uk/things-to-do/bratton-camp-and-westbury-white-horse-p133343" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: visitwiltshire.co.uk">[Visit Wiltshire]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">visitwiltshire.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Visit Wiltshire Bratton Camp and Westbury White Horse</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Visit WiltshireBratton Camp and Westbury White Horse - Visit Wiltshire...</span></span></span>

Other horses gained different meanings. The Cherhill horse became associated with the character of its unusual creator, Christopher Alsop, while later horses could represent village pride, commemoration or community effort. The tradition shows how landscapes accumulate stories: a figure may begin as a local project and later become part of a much broader county identity.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.wiltshirewhitehorses.org.uk/cherhill.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wiltshirewhitehorses.org.uk">[Wiltshire White Horses]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wiltshirewhitehorses.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wiltshirewhitehorses.org.uk.</span></span></span>

## Why the chalk horses became Wiltshire symbols

The white horses became powerful symbols because they combine visibility with participation. Unlike a statue placed in a town square, a hill figure belongs to the landscape around it. It can be seen by travellers passing through the county, becoming a marker of place long before modern signs and maps existed.

Their popularity also reflects a wider 18th- and 19th-century interest in reshaping and interpreting the countryside. During this period, landowners and local communities created or renewed hillside figures as striking features of the downs. Many were influenced by existing examples, especially the famous Westbury horse, helping create a regional tradition rather than isolated works.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.wiltshirewhitehorses.org.uk/cherhill.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wiltshirewhitehorses.org.uk">[Wiltshire White Horses]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wiltshirewhitehorses.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wiltshirewhitehorses.org.uk.</span></span></span>

For local communities, maintaining a horse became an act of collective memory. Re-chalking days, restoration projects and celebrations around the figures turned the horses into living traditions rather than fixed monuments. Their survival depends not only on the original makers but on later generations deciding that the landscape symbol still matters.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nationaltrust.org.uk/visit/wiltshire/calstone-and-cherhill-downs/exploring-the-estate-at-calstone-and-cherhill-downs" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nationaltrust.org.uk">[National Trust]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nationaltrust.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Trust Visit Calstone &amp; Cherhill Downs &#124; Wiltshire &#124; National Trust</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Trust Visit Calstone &amp; Cherhill Downs &#124; Wiltshire &#124; National Trust</span></span></span>

## Care, restoration and living tradition

A chalk horse is never completely finished. Without maintenance, grass gradually returns over the exposed surface and the figure disappears. Traditional upkeep involved renewing the chalk and keeping the outline clear, while modern restoration methods balance preservation with environmental concerns.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.wiltshirewhitehorses.org.uk/faqs.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wiltshirewhitehorses.org.uk">[Wiltshire White Horses]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wiltshirewhitehorses.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wiltshirewhitehorses.org.uk.</span></span></span>

The Westbury White Horse shows the difficulties involved in protecting these landmarks. Because of erosion on its steep hillside position, the figure underwent major changes in the 20th century, including being covered with concrete and painted white rather than maintained purely as exposed chalk. This solved some practical problems but also changed the character of the monument, creating debate about how best to preserve historic landscape features.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Westbury_White_Horse" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Westbury White Horse</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Westbury White Horse</span></span></span>

Cherhill demonstrates a more traditional approach. The horse is maintained through regular re-chalking and local involvement, with restoration groups helping preserve its appearance. The continued care of these figures is part of their meaning: every generation leaves a small mark on a tradition that depends on renewal.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nationaltrust.org.uk/visit/wiltshire/calstone-and-cherhill-downs/exploring-the-estate-at-calstone-and-cherhill-downs" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nationaltrust.org.uk">[National Trust]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nationaltrust.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Trust Visit Calstone &amp; Cherhill Downs &#124; Wiltshire &#124; National Trust</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Trust Visit Calstone &amp; Cherhill Downs &#124; Wiltshire &#124; National Trust</span></span></span>

Today the white horses remain among Wiltshire’s strongest cultural symbols. They appear on walking routes, heritage trails and local imagery, but their importance goes beyond tourism.
