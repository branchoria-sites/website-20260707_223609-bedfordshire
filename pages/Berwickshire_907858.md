## Introduction

The most enduring Berwickshire traditions are attached to particular places rather than abstract myths. Dryburgh Abbey has the tale of the helpful spirit Fatlips; Horndean has the story of the Witches’ Stone; wells across the county preserve older beliefs about healing and sacred water; and the Border landscape helped preserve ballads collected and reshaped by writers such as Sir Walter Scott. These traditions should be understood as folklore: stories, beliefs, and customs passed down, recorded, and retold, rather than evidence of supernatural events.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fat_Lips" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Fat Lips</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Fat Lips</span></span></span>

## The Border landscape behind Berwickshire folklore

Berwickshire’s folklore is closely tied to its geography. The historic county stretches from the Lammermuir Hills through the Merse farming country to the Tweed valley and the North Sea coast. This mixture of remote uplands, old tracks, rivers, churches, and fortified places created the ideal setting for stories about spirits, hidden beings, warnings, and strange encounters.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://gazetteer.org.uk/place/Berwickshire" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gazetteer.org.uk">[Gazetteer]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gazetteer.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Gazetteer Berwickshire, historiccounty &#124; Gazetteer of British Place Names</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Gazetteer Berwickshire, historiccounty &#124; Gazetteer of British Place Names</span></span></span>

The Borders were historically a place where memory mattered. Families preserved stories about battles, feuds, ruined buildings, unusual stones, and people who lived at the margins of society. Many legends are therefore less about fantasy creatures in isolation and more about explaining the character of a place: why a ruin feels unsettling, why a stone should not be moved, or why a certain road carries a reputation.

This is also why Berwickshire folklore often overlaps with neighbouring Border traditions. The same broad themes appear across the Scottish Borders and Northumberland: fairy-haunted hills, supernatural guardians, prophetic figures, ghostly animals, and ballads of love, betrayal, and violence. The county’s identity comes from being part of this wider Border storytelling world while retaining its own local landmarks.

## Dryburgh Abbey and the spirit of Fatlips

One of Berwickshire’s best-known legends is the story of Fatlips, a mysterious spirit associated with the ruins of Dryburgh Abbey. According to tradition, after the upheaval surrounding the 1745 Jacobite rising, a woman took shelter in a vault beneath the ruined abbey. She claimed that a small figure called Fatlips visited her, helping to tidy her living space and keeping the damp away by stamping the floor with heavy iron boots.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fat_Lips" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Fat Lips</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Fat Lips</span></span></span>

The tale is unusual because Fatlips is not presented simply as a frightening ghost. Instead, the figure resembles the helpful household spirits found elsewhere in Scottish folklore: beings associated with chores, hidden labour, and the strange relationship between humans and old buildings. The story transforms a lonely refuge in a ruined abbey into a place of companionship and mystery.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://spookyscotland.net/fatlips/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: spookyscotland.net">[Spooky Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">spookyscotland.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Spooky Scotland Fatlips is a spirit of brownie associated with Dryburgh Abbey</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Spooky Scotland Fatlips is a spirit of brownie associated with Dryburgh Abbey</span></span></span>

Dryburgh’s legend also shows how folklore develops over time. The original story appears connected to a real person living in unusual circumstances, but later retellings shifted attention towards the supernatural helper. The abbey’s ruined walls, religious history, and atmosphere of abandonment made it a natural home for a ghost story.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dryburgh_Abbey" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Dryburgh Abbey</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Dryburgh Abbey</span></span></span>

## Stones, wells and older sacred landscapes

### The Witches’ Stone at Horndean

Standing stones and unusual rocks frequently attracted stories in Scottish tradition because their original purpose was often forgotten while the stones themselves remained visible features of the landscape. In Horndean, Berwickshire, a stone known as the Witches’ Stone became linked with memories of witchcraft and punishment. Local accounts recorded in antiquarian sources describe it as an old upright stone associated with accusations against witches and later moved from its original location.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.thenorthernantiquarian.org/tag/legendary-rocks/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: thenorthernantiquarian.org">[The Northern Antiquarian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">thenorthernantiquarian.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Northern Antiquarianlegendary rocks</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Northern Antiquarianlegendary rocks</span></span></span>

Such stories reveal more about historical attitudes than about actual witch practices. Across Britain, places connected with alleged witches often became markers of fear, injustice, and community memory. A stone could become a reminder of old accusations even when the original events were uncertain or forgotten.

### Holy wells and healing traditions

Berwickshire contains many sites associated with wells, including places traditionally linked with saints, healing, or local religious customs. Recorded examples include St Andrew’s Well at Coldingham, St Bathan’s Well at Abbey St Bathans, and Fairy Well at Ladykirk.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.thenorthernantiquarian.org/sites/scotland/berwickshire/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: thenorthernantiquarian.org">[The Northern Antiquarian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">thenorthernantiquarian.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Northern Antiquarian Berwickshire</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Northern Antiquarian Berwickshire</span></span></span>

Holy wells were important because water occupied a boundary position in folk belief: it was ordinary and essential, but also associated with cleansing, health, and spiritual protection. Some customs involved visiting wells at particular times, leaving offerings, or seeking cures. While many practices faded as religious and medical ideas changed, the survival of well names shows how strongly these places remained embedded in local memory.

## Witches, charms and supernatural protection

Stories of witches in Berwickshire belong to a wider Scottish tradition in which certain individuals were believed to possess unusual knowledge of healing, curses, weather, or hidden forces. The historical reality of witchcraft accusations was often harsh, while later folklore sometimes turned accused figures into mysterious characters within local storytelling.

The county’s folklore also reflects common Border concerns: protecting livestock, avoiding bad luck, and explaining misfortune. Charms, warnings, and stories about dangerous places formed part of a practical tradition of living with uncertainty. A tale about a haunted road or a forbidden stone could function as entertainment, a moral warning, or a way of preserving memories of difficult events.

## Border ballads and the memory of old stories

Berwickshire belongs to the landscape of the Border ballads, a major tradition of narrative songs dealing with feuds, raids, romance, tragedy, and supernatural themes. These ballads were preserved through oral performance before being collected by antiquarians and writers.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.trove.scot/stories/features/the-border-ballads-collection-at-smailholm-tower" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: trove.scot">[Trove.scot]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">trove.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on trove.scot.</span></span></span>

Sir Walter Scott played a major role in recording and publishing Border traditions through *Minstrelsy of the Scottish Border*, first published in the early nineteenth century. His work helped preserve many ballads and local stories, although collectors like Scott also shaped the material by selecting, editing, and presenting it for new audiences.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/304688914_Collecting_Ballads_and_Resisting_Radical_Energies_Scott%27s_Minstrelsy_of_the_Scottish_Border" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ResearchGate(PDF) Collecting Ballads and Resisting Radical Energies: Scott’s Minstrelsy of the Scottish BorderJanuary 1, 2005...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 1, 2005</span></span></span>

For Berwickshire, this tradition matters because folklore was not only something whispered around firesides. It became part of a wider cultural identity, influencing literature, tourism, and how the Borders are imagined today.

## Ghost stories and haunted places

Berwickshire’s ghost traditions often centre on ruins, churches, towers, and old roads rather than dramatic tales of monsters. This reflects a common pattern in Scottish folklore: buildings carrying layers of human history become places where memories, tragedies, and supernatural stories meet.

The county’s ruined religious sites, including Dryburgh Abbey, are especially suited to ghost traditions because they combine visible remains with lost histories. A ruined building invites questions: who lived there, what happened there, and what stories might remain? Folklore provides imaginative answers where historical records are incomplete.

## What makes Berwickshire folklore distinctive today?

Berwickshire’s folklore survives because it is attached to real places. A visitor can stand near a ruined abbey, see an old stone, walk a Border road, or visit a village with centuries of memories behind it. The power of these traditions lies less in proving whether a legend happened exactly as told and more in understanding why communities kept telling it.

The county’s folklore combines several layers:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **Ancient landscape traditions**, preserved through stones, wells, and place names.
* **Medieval and early modern memories**, connected with churches, saints, conflict, and local families.
* **Border storytelling**, especially ballads and tales of rivalry, romance, and danger.<span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://www.trove.scot/stories/features/the-border-ballads-collection-at-smailholm-tower" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: trove.scot">[trove.scot]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">trove.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Source details in endnotes.</span></span></span>
* **Later literary preservation**, through collectors and writers who helped bring local traditions to wider audiences.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.trove.scot/stories/features/the-border-ballads-collection-at-smailholm-tower" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: trove.scot">[Trove.scot]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">trove.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on trove.scot.</span></span></span>

</div>

Berwickshire’s legends therefore form a record of how people have understood their surroundings over generations. The stories of spirits, witches, sacred waters, and mysterious stones are not separate from the landscape; they are part of the way the landscape has been remembered.
