## Introduction

[Peeblesshire]({{ 'where-peeblesshires-legends-still/' | relative_url }})’s saints, wells and witch-trial memories show a harder-edged side of the county’s folklore. This is not only a landscape of fairy hills and Merlin stories; it is also a place where Christian conversion legends, healing springs, civic pageantry and the memory of persecution are tied to very specific sites. Stobo links St Mungo with the Christianising of the upper Tweed. Innerleithen turns St Ronan’s battle with the Devil into spa heritage and summer ceremony. Peebles and Kirkurd preserve the darker record of witchcraft accusation, especially the cases remembered around the 1629 executions and Janet Coutts in 1649–50. The result is a local belief-world where sacred water, saints’ names and fear of the Devil do not sit in separate boxes. They overlap in kirks, wells, trial papers, memorials and modern retellings.

## Why Peeblesshire’s sacred stories feel so local

The useful starting point is scale. Peeblesshire is a compact historic county centred on the upper Tweed, with Peebles, Stobo, Innerleithen, West Linton, Drumelzier, Broughton and Tweedsmuir forming a closely connected religious and social landscape. Its holy and frightening traditions are rarely free-floating myths. They usually belong to a kirk, a spring, a hill, a court record or a named family.

That is why Stobo matters so much. The Corpus of Romanesque Sculpture in Britain and Ireland notes that St Mungo’s church at Stobo is mentioned in the Glasgow Inquisition of about 1120, was the most important church in the upper Tweed Valley in the early medieval period, and later had chaplaincies at Lyne, Broughton, Kingledoors, Dawyck and Drumelzier. In folklore terms, that gives Stobo a special weight: it was not merely a picturesque country kirk later decorated with legend, but a real ecclesiastical centre through which Christian authority was organised in the district.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.crsbi.ac.uk/view-item?i=15223" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: crsbi.ac.uk">[CRSBI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">crsbi.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">CRSBISt Mungo, Stobo, Peebleshire</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">CRSBISt Mungo, Stobo, Peebleshire</span></span></span>

The wells show the same local pattern. A modern holy-well gazetteer for the Scottish Borders lists several Peeblesshire-linked wells and their present condition, including Lady Well at Peebles, St Mungo’s Well at Peebles, St Mungo’s Well at Stobo, St Mungo’s Well at West Linton, St Paul’s Well at Romannobridge and St Ronan’s Well at Innerleithen. Many are recorded as destroyed or lost, while St Ronan’s Well is listed as extant. That uneven survival is important: Peeblesshire’s sacred geography is partly visible on the ground and partly recoverable through names, maps and antiquarian memory.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://britishholywells.co.uk/scotland/scottishborders/scottishborderslist.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: britishholywells.co.uk">[britishholywells.co.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">britishholywells.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Holy Wells in the Scottish Borders</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Holy Wells in the Scottish Borders</span></span></span>

## St Mungo, Stobo and conversion stories

Stobo’s St Mungo tradition belongs to the wider story of Kentigern, the missionary saint better known as Mungo and strongly associated with Glasgow. In Peeblesshire, however, the saint is not just a distant Glasgow figure. He becomes part of the upper Tweed’s own origin story: a church founder, a converter and, in later local legend, the holy man who brings even Merlin into the Christian fold.

The firmer historical ground is the church itself. Stobo’s dedication to St Mungo and its early importance are well attested, with the church confirmed to the bishop of Glasgow in the twelfth century and later tied to Glasgow Cathedral as a prebend until the Reformation. The building’s surviving medieval fabric and later restorations make it a physical anchor for this religious memory, rather than a legend attached to an empty field.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.crsbi.ac.uk/view-item?i=15223" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: crsbi.ac.uk">[CRSBI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">crsbi.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">CRSBISt Mungo, Stobo, Peebleshire</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">CRSBISt Mungo, Stobo, Peebleshire</span></span></span>

The more legendary layer is the conversion of Merlin. Local heritage accounts connect Stobo with the story that St Mungo baptised or converted Merlin, often linked to an “altar stone” and to the Drumelzier Merlin tradition nearby. Scotland Starts Here, for example, presents Stobo Kirk as a place where an altar associated with St Mungo was reused from an earlier sacred setting, and it recounts the story of a confrontation between Merlin and the Christian missionary.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://scotlandstartshere.com/point-of-interest/stobo-kirk/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: scotlandstartshere.com">[Scotland Starts Here]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">scotlandstartshere.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Scotland Starts Here Stobo Kirk &#124; History &amp; Heritage</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Scotland Starts HereStobo Kirk &#124; History &amp; Heritage - Scotland Starts Here...</span></span></span> The same tourism tradition notes that a stained-glass window at Stobo depicts Merlin being baptised by St Kentigern.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://scotlandstartshere.com/point-of-interest/stobo/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: scotlandstartshere.com">[Scotland Starts Here]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">scotlandstartshere.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Scotland Starts HereStobo &#124; Towns &amp; VillagesStobo is a small hamlet, 5 miles west of Peebles. For a reader, the key point is not that the conversion scene can be treated as a proven event. It is better understood as a local Christian interpretation of an older legendary landscape. Drumelzier gives Peeblesshire its wild-man Merlin; Stobo gives that wildness a Christian ending. The saint’s role is to civilise, baptise and fold an unsettling prophetic figure into the authority of the kirk.

This is a common pattern in medieval and later sacred storytelling. A saint does not simply preach doctrine; he tames dangerous places, rival powers and ambiguous figures. In Peeblesshire, that pattern is unusually vivid because the story can be walked between places: Stobo Kirk, the Tweed valley, Drumelzier and the Merlin sites all sit close enough to feel like one mythic district.

## St Ronan, the Devil and spa folklore

Innerleithen’s St Ronan tradition has a different flavour. It is less about a mother church and more about the way legend, mineral water, literature and civic festival can reinforce one another. The core local saying is that St Ronan “cleik’t the Deil by the hind leg” and banished him. Scotland Starts Here explains the line as the founding legend of an itinerant monk who came up the Tweed, with the story understood as a memory of Christian mission in the area.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://scotlandstartshere.com/point-of-interest/st-ronans-wells-visitor-centre/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: scotlandstartshere.com">[Scotland Starts Here]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">scotlandstartshere.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Scotland Starts Here St Ronan&#x27;s Wells Visitor Centre &#124; History &amp; Heritage</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Scotland Starts HereSt Ronan&#x27;s Wells Visitor Centre &#124; History &amp; Heritage - Scotland Starts Here...</span></span></span>

The well then gives the legend a body. St Ronan’s Wells Visitor Centre stands in a historic spa pavilion overlooking the Leithen valley, originally built by the Earl of Traquair as a retreat for spa visitors. Its permanent exhibition explains the well’s links with James Hogg and Sir Walter Scott, and the visitor-centre account notes that people still visit to sample the spring water.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://scotlandstartshere.com/point-of-interest/st-ronans-wells-visitor-centre/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: scotlandstartshere.com">[Scotland Starts Here]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">scotlandstartshere.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Scotland Starts Here St Ronan&#x27;s Wells Visitor Centre &#124; History &amp; Heritage</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Scotland Starts HereSt Ronan&#x27;s Wells Visitor Centre &#124; History &amp; Heritage - Scotland Starts Here...</span></span></span>

The Devil story is especially interesting because it survives not as a grim demon legend but as public pageantry. The official St Ronan’s Border Games site traces the Cleikum Ceremonies to Sir Walter Scott’s novel *St Ronan’s Well*, first published in December 1823, where the sign of the Cleikum Inn showed St Ronan catching the Devil’s leg with his crook. The same account explains that the annual games were founded by James Hogg in 1827, partly to entertain visitors during the spa season, and that the first formal Cleikum Ceremonies took place in 1901.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.stronansgames.org.uk/the-cleikum.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: stronansgames.org.uk">[St. Ronan&#x27;s Border Games]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">stronansgames.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">St. Ronan&#x27;s Border Games The Cleik&#x27;um</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">St. Ronan&#x27;s Border Games The Cleik&#x27;um</span></span></span>

That makes Innerleithen a strong example of layered folklore. There may be an older saintly or missionary memory behind the name Ronan. There is a mineral spring whose reputation grew in the age of health tourism. There is Scott’s literary handling of the spa village. There is Hogg’s role in shaping the games. And there is the twentieth-century formalisation of the ceremony. The modern result feels traditional because it is traditional — but not in the simple sense of being unchanged from the early Middle Ages.

The legend’s emotional tone also matters. St Ronan’s Devil is threatening enough to mark the place as spiritually contested, but comic enough to be paraded, dramatised and turned into local identity. In Peeblesshire belief, this is the brighter counterpart to the witch-trial Devil: one Devil is hooked and made festive; the other appears in confessions extracted under fear, pressure and violence.

## Holy wells as healing places and memory markers

Peeblesshire’s wells are not all equally well preserved, but the pattern is clear: saintly names and springs helped map holiness onto everyday geography. A well could be a water source, a healing place, a parish memory and a sign that Christian tradition had claimed a particular patch of land.

St Mungo’s wells are especially important for this page because they connect the Peebles area back to the Stobo tradition. The Scottish Borders holy-well list records St Mungo’s Well at Peebles, St Mungo’s Well at Stobo and St Mungo’s Well at West Linton, though each is marked as destroyed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://britishholywells.co.uk/scotland/scottishborders/scottishborderslist.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: britishholywells.co.uk">[britishholywells.co.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">britishholywells.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Holy Wells in the Scottish Borders</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Holy Wells in the Scottish Borders</span></span></span> That does not make them worthless as folklore evidence. On the contrary, destroyed wells often survive through place-name memory, old maps, Ordnance Survey notes, local histories and the habits of people who continued to speak of them after the physical feature had gone.

The Northern Antiquarian’s account of St Mungo’s Well in Peebles, using nineteenth-century Ordnance Survey and local-history material, places the lost well around the High Street area and notes that it was described in the OS Name Book as an excellent spring, although it had already been destroyed by the time that record was made.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.thenorthernantiquarian.org/2021/09/01/mungos-well-peebles/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: thenorthernantiquarian.org">[thenorthernantiquarian.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">thenorthernantiquarian.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">St. Mungo&#x27;s Well, Peebles, Peeblesshire</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">St. Mungo&#x27;s Well, Peebles, Peeblesshire</span></span></span> That small detail is telling. By the nineteenth century, the well could be absent as a working sacred spring but still present as a named feature worth recording.

St Ronan’s Well is the stronger surviving public example. It has a visitor centre, a spa history, gardens and a continuing association with local ceremony. Innerleithen’s own heritage material describes St Ronan’s Wells as established in the 1820s on the site of a natural spring, flourishing through the nineteenth century, and later expanded in 1896 for indoor bathing and bottling.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.innerleithen.org.uk/culture-and-heritage.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: innerleithen.org.uk">[Innerleithen]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">innerleithen.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Innerleithen Culture and Heritage</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Innerleithen Culture and Heritage</span></span></span>

The reader should be cautious, though, about treating every saintly well as evidence of a continuous pre-Reformation healing cult. Some springs may have had long local use; some were renamed, reshaped or romanticised in the spa age; some survive mainly through antiquarian recording. Peeblesshire’s holy wells matter because they show how water, health, holiness and local memory met — not because every claim attached to them can be pushed back unchanged into the early church.

## Janet Coutts and witchcraft memory

Peeblesshire’s witch memory is much harsher than its saintly legends. It moves from healing water and conversion stories into accusation, imprisonment, interrogation, execution and modern commemoration. The most focused individual case is Janet Coutts, also recorded as Jonet Coutts, from Kirkurd in Peeblesshire.

The University of Edinburgh’s Survey of Scottish Witchcraft identifies Janet Coutts as a woman aged 51 from Kirkurd, in the county of Peebles, with a case dated 1650.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://witches.hca.ed.ac.uk/quick/?fullname=&amp;page=239&amp;sort=-casecolumn" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: witches.hca.ed.ac.uk">[witches.hca.ed.ac.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">witches.hca.ed.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on ed.ac.uk.</span></span></span> The case record also links her to Peebles presbytery material and to George Cathie, a witch-pricker from Tranent.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://witches.hca.ed.ac.uk/case/C/EGD/1817" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: witches.hca.ed.ac.uk">[witches.hca.ed.ac.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">witches.hca.ed.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on ed.ac.uk.</span></span></span>

What makes the Coutts case so important is not simply that one woman was accused. It shows how an accusation could become a travelling engine of further accusations. The surviving case summary reports that Janet Coutts told commissioners George Cathie had asked her to name innocent people as witches.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://witches.hca.ed.ac.uk/case/C/EGD/1817" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: witches.hca.ed.ac.uk">[witches.hca.ed.ac.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">witches.hca.ed.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on ed.ac.uk.</span></span></span> Later summaries of the case describe her as being used as a witness against others, with suspicion growing when her statements became inconsistent.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Janet_Coutts" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Janet Coutts</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Janet Coutts</span></span></span>

The role of the witch-pricker is central. In Scottish witch trials, “pricking” meant searching the body for an alleged Devil’s mark, often by piercing the skin to find a place that supposedly did not bleed or feel pain. In practice, it could become a form of torture and a profitable trade for the pricker. Janet Coutts’ case is repeatedly used in modern discussion of Scottish witch-hunting because it exposes the machinery behind the supposed evidence: pressure, bodily testing, confession, accusation and the possibility of personal gain.

For Peeblesshire folklore, this changes how the Devil should be read. In St Ronan’s legend, the Devil is a symbolic opponent of Christian mission. In the witch trials, the Devil becomes part of a legal and religious language that could destroy real people. The “belief” was not just a tale told by the fireside. It could be written into accusations, tested on bodies and used to justify execution.

## The 1629 Peebles executions and modern remembrance

Janet Coutts belongs to the later wave of Peeblesshire witch memory, but Peebles is also strongly associated with the executions of 1629. Modern memorial work has made that history more visible to residents and visitors.

A motion in the Scottish Parliament supported campaigners seeking a Peebles memorial for 27 local people — 21 women and six men — understood to have been executed in 1629 under the Scottish Witchcraft Act of 1563. It also named the campaign leaders, Simon Ritchie and Elisa Smith, and connected the Peebles memorial effort with the wider Scottish campaign for remembrance of those accused under witchcraft law.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.parliament.scot/parliamentarybusiness/28877.aspx?ReferenceNumbers=S6M-04744&amp;SearchType=Advance" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: parliament.scot">[Scottish Parliament]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">parliament.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on parliament.scot.</span></span></span>

The memorial itself was reported in 2022 as a granite slab bearing the victims’ names, to be placed beside a copper beech tree in Tweed Green. ITV Border reported that the 27 were executed at Calf Knowe, now known as Venlaw Hill, after being accused of witchcraft.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.itv.com/news/border/2022-08-28/memorial-to-victims-executed-after-witchcraft-accusations-erected-in-peebles" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: itv.com">[ITVX]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">itv.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">XMemorial to victims executed after witchcraft accusations</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">XMemorial to victims executed after witchcraft accusations</span></span></span> Geograph records the installed Tweed Green plaque as commemorating 21 women and six men executed in Peebles in 1629 under the Witchcraft Act of 1563.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.geograph.org.uk/photo/7321146" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: geograph.org.uk">[Geograph]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">geograph.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Geograph Witch hunt commemorative plaque, Tweed Green Peebles</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Geograph Witch hunt commemorative plaque, Tweed Green Peebles</span></span></span>

This is modern folklore in the serious sense: a community deciding how to remember a frightening inherited story. The memorial does not turn the accused into supernatural figures. It does almost the opposite. It pulls them back from the category of “witches” and restores them as named people from Peebleshire who were persecuted under a law and belief system that later generations reject.

The recent stage production *Rope & Flame*, reported in 2025 as a community theatre work based on the Peebles Witch Trials of 1629, shows how that memory is still being reshaped. The report notes that the production drew on Mary W. Craig’s *Borders Witch Hunt* and involved people from Peebles, Stobo, Lyne, West Linton and the wider area.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://midlothianview.com/news/curtain-call-for-new-play-exploring-peebles-darkest-days" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: midlothianview.com">[Midlothian View]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">midlothianview.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Midlothian View Curtain call for new play exploring Peebles &#x27;darkest days</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Midlothian View Curtain call for new play exploring Peebles &#x27;darkest days</span></span></span> This kind of retelling matters because it shifts the old story from whispered shame or local dread into public reflection.

## What these traditions reveal about Peeblesshire belief

The saints, wells and witch memories of Peeblesshire are best read together, not as separate curiosities. They show a county where belief was tied to place and authority. A saint could claim a spring or kirk. A well could promise health. A Devil could be hooked by a saint in festival legend. The same Devil, in another register, could appear in confessions and accusations with fatal consequences.

Several patterns stand out:

**Sacred places were practical places.** Stobo was not only a holy name; it was an important church centre in the upper Tweed. St Ronan’s Well was not only a legend; it became a spa, visitor attraction and civic emblem.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.crsbi.ac.uk/view-item?i=15223" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: crsbi.ac.uk">[CRSBI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">crsbi.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">CRSBISt Mungo, Stobo, Peebleshire</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">CRSBISt Mungo, Stobo, Peebleshire</span></span></span>

**Old stories were repeatedly remade.** The St Ronan tradition moved through saintly legend, Scott’s fiction, Hogg’s games and twentieth-century ceremony. The result is neither fake nor purely ancient; it is a living local tradition made from several historical layers.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.stronansgames.org.uk/the-cleikum.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: stronansgames.org.uk">[St. Ronan&#x27;s Border Games]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">stronansgames.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">St. Ronan&#x27;s Border Games The Cleik&#x27;um</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">St. Ronan&#x27;s Border Games The Cleik&#x27;um</span></span></span>

**Witch memory has moved from accusation to commemoration.** Seventeenth-century records treated accused people as threats. Modern Peebles memorial work treats them as victims of injustice whose names should be visible in the town.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.parliament.scot/parliamentarybusiness/28877.aspx?ReferenceNumbers=S6M-04744&amp;SearchType=Advance" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: parliament.scot">[Scottish Parliament]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">parliament.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on parliament.scot.</span></span></span>

**Peeblesshire’s folklore is morally mixed.** It contains healing water and public festivity, but also coercion, fear and execution. That mixture is part of its honesty. The county’s belief culture is not just charming legend; it is also a record of how communities explained illness, misfortune, spiritual danger and social conflict.

## How the stories are retold today

Today, these traditions survive in different forms. Stobo Kirk keeps St Mungo’s name and the Merlin conversion legend in a church setting. Innerleithen keeps St Ronan through its wells, visitor centre and Cleikum Ceremonies. Peebles keeps witch memory through the Tweed Green memorial, local campaigning and new creative retellings.

For visitors, the safest way to understand them is to ask what kind of evidence each story rests on. Stobo’s ecclesiastical importance is historically strong, while the Merlin conversion is legendary. St Ronan’s Well is physically and institutionally present, while the saint’s early biography is much harder to pin down. Janet Coutts and the Peebles witch persecutions belong to documented legal and church history, though modern retellings inevitably add interpretation, empathy and public meaning.

That difference does not weaken the folklore. It makes it richer. Peeblesshire’s saints, wells and witch memories show how a small historic county can hold several kinds of truth at once: the truth of medieval church organisation, the truth of healing and spa culture, the truth of stories communities choose to perform, and the painful truth of people whose lives were ended by fear dressed up as certainty.

    return Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]'))
      .map(function (button) { return button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button'); })
      .filter(function (marketId) { return !!markets[marketId]; });
  }
  function applyMarket(section, marketId, persist) {
    var available = availableMarkets(section);
    if (available.indexOf(marketId)< 0) marketId = available[0] || defaultMarket;
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-localized-link]')).forEach(function (link) {
      var query = link.getAttribute('data-ebay-query') || '';
      var reference = link.getAttribute('data-ebay-reference') || '';
      link.setAttribute('href', buildUrl(query, reference, marketId));
    });
    var select = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-select]');
    if (select && available.indexOf(marketId) >= 0) select.value = marketId;
    var selectedText = marketId;
    if (select && select.selectedIndex >= 0) selectedText = select.options[select.selectedIndex].text;
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-selected-market-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = selectedText;
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-selected-market-flag], [data-ebay-trigger-market-flag]')).forEach(function (flag) {
      flag.className = flagClass(marketId);
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-trigger-market-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = selectedText;
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-domain-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = domainLabel(marketId);
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-option]')).forEach(function (button) {
      button.setAttribute('aria-selected', button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-option') === marketId ? 'true' : 'false');
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      var active = button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button') === marketId;
      button.setAttribute('aria-pressed', active ? 'true' : 'false');
    });
    section.setAttribute('data-ebay-selected-market', marketId);
    if (persist) {
      try { if (window.localStorage) localStorage.setItem('phoenix-ebay-market', marketId); } catch (err) {}
    }
    closeMenu(section);
  }
  sections.forEach(function (section) {
    if (section.getAttribute('data-ebay-localized-init') === '1') return;
    section.setAttribute('data-ebay-localized-init', '1');
    var available = availableMarkets(section);
    var marketId = defaultMarket;
    if (window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation && window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation.inferMarket) {
      marketId = window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation.inferMarket(available, {
        storageKey: 'phoenix-ebay-market',
        defaultMarket: defaultMarket
      });
    } else if (available.indexOf(defaultMarket)< 0) {
      marketId = available[0] || defaultMarket;
    }
    var select = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-select]');
    if (select) {
      select.addEventListener('change', function () {
        applyMarket(section, select.value, true);
      });
    }
    var trigger = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-trigger]');
    var menu = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-menu]');
    if (trigger && menu) {
      trigger.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
        event.stopPropagation();
        var expanded = trigger.getAttribute('aria-expanded') === 'true';
        menu.hidden = expanded;
        trigger.setAttribute('aria-expanded', expanded ? 'false' : 'true');
        var picker = trigger.closest('.fr-ebay-market-picker');
        if (picker) picker.classList.toggle('fr-ebay-market-picker--open', !expanded);
      });
      Array.prototype.slice.call(menu.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-option]')).forEach(function (button) {
        button.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
          event.stopPropagation();
          applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-option'), true);
        });
      });
      document.addEventListener('click', function () { closeMenu(section); });
    }
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      button.addEventListener('click', function () {
        applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button'), true);
      });
    });
    applyMarket(section, marketId, false);
  });
})();
