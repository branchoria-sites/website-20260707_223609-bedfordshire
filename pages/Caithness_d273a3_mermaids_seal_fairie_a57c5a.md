## Introduction

[Caithness]({{ 'where-caithness-folklore-meets-the-edge/' | relative_url }}) sea legends are not cosy beach stories. Along the county’s north coast, mermaids and seal-fairies belong to a working shoreline of storms, shipwrecks, seal-hunting, fishing, sudden profit and sudden loss. The strongest evidence clusters around three places: Reay and Sandside Bay, where reported mermaid sightings became a national talking point in 1809; the Dunnet Bay and Dwarwick Head tradition preserved by James T. Calder, where a mermaid’s gifts turn into a punishment; and the John o’ Groats seal-fairy tale, where a seal hunter is forced to recognise the creatures he kills as a people with claims of their own. The stories matter because they show Caithness looking outwards across dangerous water while also asking a local moral question: what happens when people treat the sea only as something to exploit?<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.erudit.org/en/journals/ronbrit/2022-n79-ronbrit010558/1123074ar/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: erudit.org">[erudit.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">erudit.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on erudit.org.</span></span></span>

## Why the Caithness Coast Makes Sea Legends Feel Plausible

Caithness sits at the exposed northern edge of mainland Scotland, facing the Pentland Firth, Orkney and the North Sea. That setting is not just scenery. The Scottish Government’s shipping study for the Pentland Firth and Orkney waters records repeated warnings from sailors and kayakers that tidal streams, weather, overfalls and route timing are decisive in this area; one respondent put the lesson simply: “You cannot beat the tides or the weather.”<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gov.scot/publications/shipping-study-pentland-firth-orkney-waters/pages/6/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gov.scot">[Scottish Government]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gov.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Scottish Government6. QUESTIONNAIRE FEEDBACK</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Scottish Government6. QUESTIONNAIRE FEEDBACK</span></span></span>

That physical world helps explain the tone of the folklore. These are not inland fairy stories moved to the beach. They grow from a coastline where people watched seals, cut seaweed, fished, scavenged wreckage, worried about boats, and understood that a calm surface could hide danger. Calder’s nineteenth-century account of Dunnet Bay calls it beautiful but dangerous, noting that before the lighthouse there were winters in which one or two shipwrecks might occur; he gives the 1811 wreck of the *Fingal* as a particularly grim example, with all sixteen crew lost after the vessel mistook the bay in storm and darkness.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/stream/sketchcivilandt00sincgoog/sketchcivilandt00sincgoog_djvu.txt" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on archive.org.</span></span></span>

In that setting, mermaids and seal-fairies do several kinds of work at once. They make strange sightings speakable; they turn animal behaviour into social drama; and they give moral shape to the hazards of a coast where wealth might arrive as fish, skins, wreck goods or jewels, but where greed and carelessness could be fatal.

## The Reay Mermaid: A Sighting That Became National News

The best-attested Caithness mermaid tradition begins not as a fireside tale but as written testimony. In January 1809, Elizabeth Mackay was walking near her home at Reay with her cousin C. Mackenzie when they saw other people on a rock showing alarm at something in the water. According to Katie Garner’s close study of the case, the women came near enough to observe what they described as the face, throat and arms of a creature only a few yards away in Sandside Bay.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.erudit.org/en/journals/ronbrit/2022-n79-ronbrit010558/1123074ar/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: erudit.org">[Érudit]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">erudit.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on erudit.org.</span></span></span>

The detail is important because the Reay case sits between folklore, natural history and social testimony. Mackay was not simply repeating an inherited legend. Her letter, written months later to Mary Innes of Sandside, tried to describe an “uncommon” marine sighting in careful observational terms. Garner notes that the account moved from private correspondence into print after it attracted Sir John Sinclair’s curiosity; by late 1809 it was circulating through British newspapers and beyond, with enquiries reaching Mackay’s father, the minister of Reay.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.erudit.org/en/journals/ronbrit/2022-n79-ronbrit010558/1123074ar/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: erudit.org">[Érudit]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">erudit.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on erudit.org.</span></span></span>

Simon Young’s study of “The Reay Mermaids” adds that the 1809 report was paired with an older account by William Munro, a schoolmaster who said he had seen a mermaid-like figure at Sandside Bay around 1797 or 1798. Young argues that the two letters, published without the authors’ permission in an Oxfordshire newspaper, made the Reay mermaids perhaps the most famous nineteenth-century British mer-folk.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.shimajournal.org/issues/v12n2/e.-Young-Shima-v12n2.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: shimajournal.org">[Shima Journal]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">shimajournal.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">e. Young Shima v12n2</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">e. Young Shima v12n2</span></span></span>

The striking thing is how seriously some readers treated the possibility. Young points out that in the early nineteenth century, mermaids could still be discussed as unknown marine creatures rather than only as fairy beings. That made them more respectable, in some circles, than ghosts or fairies: a person could wonder whether the sea hid an unfamiliar animal without openly claiming belief in magic.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.shimajournal.org/issues/v12n2/e.-Young-Shima-v12n2.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: shimajournal.org">[Shima Journal]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">shimajournal.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">e. Young Shima v12n2</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">e. Young Shima v12n2</span></span></span>

## Was the Reay Mermaid a Seal, a Hoax or Something Else?

The Reay evidence is strong as evidence of a reported sighting, not as proof of a mermaid. That distinction matters. The later debate quickly produced sceptical explanations, and the most plausible natural candidate was a seal. Garner notes that Elizabeth Mackay’s own account sits in a place where seals were common; her father’s earlier parish writing listed seals among the larger marine life around Reay, and a later Caithness critic, John Paterson, argued that the women had seen an old grey seal, perhaps with a salmon in its mouth.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.erudit.org/en/journals/ronbrit/2022-n79-ronbrit010558/1123074ar/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: erudit.org">[Érudit]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">erudit.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on erudit.org.</span></span></span>

Young’s work shows how the case became contested in the local press. When the *John o’ Groat Journal* revisited the story in 1849, it reported that the sighting had been widely believed for years but later came to be regarded by many as a hoax. Paterson claimed the creature was a grey seal and said the “mermaid seers” had been laughed at, while another correspondent, James Taylor, defended the honesty of Elizabeth Mackay, C. Mackenzie and William Munro.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.shimajournal.org/issues/v12n2/e.-Young-Shima-v12n2.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: shimajournal.org">[Shima Journal]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">shimajournal.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">e. Young Shima v12n2</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">e. Young Shima v12n2</span></span></span>

That argument is part of the story’s value. Caithness people were not presented as a simple, credulous crowd. The record shows curiosity, ridicule, social defence, natural explanation, and the prestige of local witnesses all pulling against one another. The Reay mermaid became folklore partly because nobody could quite settle what kind of story it was: a misidentified animal, a local joke, a sincere mistake, an unknown creature, or a coastal marvel amplified by print culture.

## Dunnet Bay and Dwarwick Head: The Mermaid’s Bargain

The Dwarwick Head legend is more clearly a traditional tale than a sighting report. Calder places it near the point of Dwarwick, on the Dunnet Bay side of the Caithness coast. In his version, a young man meets a mermaid bathing or playing in a sandy pool between Murkle and Castlehill. Their meetings continue, and he becomes mysteriously rich, giving costly gifts to local women. Then he grows greedy, asking the mermaid for more money and jewels rather than honouring the relationship.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/stream/sketchcivilandt00sincgoog/sketchcivilandt00sincgoog_djvu.txt" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on archive.org.</span></span></span>

The punishment is elegant and cruel. The mermaid tells him she can take him to a cave in Dwarwick Head where she keeps the wealth of ships lost in the Pentland Firth and on Dunnet sands. He hesitates, but greed wins. In the cave, the legend says, he remains bound by a gold chain, long enough to let him walk on a small strip of sand but not long enough to escape.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/stream/sketchcivilandt00sincgoog/sketchcivilandt00sincgoog_djvu.txt" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on archive.org.</span></span></span>

This is not the same sort of evidence as the Reay letters. Calder was preserving a tradition in a local history, not documenting named eyewitnesses and correspondence. But the tale is deeply Caithness in its geography. It turns Dunnet Bay’s real shipwreck danger into supernatural treasure, and it makes the mermaid neither a romantic prize nor a simple monster. She rewards, warns, judges and imprisons. The gold chain is the perfect image for the story’s moral: the young man gets exactly what he wanted, and it traps him.

Modern retellings often soften Caithness mermaids into picturesque figures combing their hair on rocks, especially around Dunnet Head or Dunnet Bay. Those versions are useful as living folklore, but the older Dwarwick tale is darker and more local. It is not just “a mermaid was seen”; it is “the sea gives wealth, and the sea may keep the greedy.”

## Seal-Fairies Near John o’ Groats

The seal-fairy tradition near John o’ Groats is one of the clearest Caithness examples of a sea legend with an ethical bite. William Grant Stewart’s *The Popular Superstitions and Festive Amusements of the Highlanders of Scotland*, first published in 1823 and later reprinted, includes the tale usually known as “The Seal-Catcher’s Adventure”. It begins near “John-o’-Groat’s House”, where a man makes his living catching fish and killing seals for their skins. The tale insists that many seals are not merely animals but “downright fairies”.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://readingroo.ms/6/9/5/9/69592/69592-h/69592-h.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: readingroo.ms">[Reading Roo]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">readingroo.ms</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on readingroo.ms.</span></span></span>

The plot is simple but powerful. The seal-catcher wounds a great seal, loses his knife in the animal’s body, and later meets a stranger who offers him a profitable bargain in seal-skins. The stranger takes him on horseback to the edge of a sea-cliff, then down into the sea, where the hunter is brought before the wounded seal and made to heal the injury he caused. He is allowed to return home only after swearing never again to maim or kill a seal.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://readingroo.ms/6/9/5/9/69592/69592-h/69592-h.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: readingroo.ms">[Reading Roo]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">readingroo.ms</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on readingroo.ms.</span></span></span>

Garner reads this tale beside the Reay mermaid because both belong to a Caithness world in which seals could appear almost human and human treatment of marine animals could become morally troubling. She notes that the John o’ Groats tale is set about forty-five miles from Reay and treats seal-killing not as a neutral livelihood but as an act whose victim can answer back.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.erudit.org/en/journals/ronbrit/2022-n79-ronbrit010558/1123074ar.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: erudit.org">[Érudit]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">erudit.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on erudit.org.</span></span></span>

The story is not anti-fishing in a modern campaign sense. It understands poverty, market value and the hard work of coastal life. Its force comes from making the hunter enter the seal’s world. Once he sees the wounded creature as a person among its own people, ordinary exploitation becomes harder to excuse.

## The Selkie’s Grave at Old Olrig

Caithness also has a local selkie tradition attached to Old Olrig churchyard near Castletown. Modern local accounts describe a grave known as the Selkie’s Grave, where visitors may find shells left on or near the stone. The story usually says that a fisherman found a baby girl wrapped in sealskin on the shore, raised her as a human child, and that she later died in childbirth and was buried at Olrig.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ferryview.scot/the-selkie-a-caithness-mermaid" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ferryview.scot">[ferryview.scot]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ferryview.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Selkie</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Selkie</span></span></span>

This material is less firmly anchored in early printed folklore than the Reay letters, Calder’s Dwarwick tale or Stewart’s John o’ Groats seal-catcher. Its importance lies in living place-memory. Old Olrig gives the selkie tradition a grave, a churchyard and a visitor practice, turning a wider northern Scottish seal-folk motif into a Caithness site people can still seek out.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ferryview.scot/the-selkie-a-caithness-mermaid" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ferryview.scot">[ferryview.scot]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ferryview.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Selkie</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Selkie</span></span></span>

The story also changes the usual selkie pattern. Many Scottish selkie tales focus on an adult seal-woman whose skin is stolen by a man who wants her as a wife. The Olrig version, as now told, centres on a found child, social unease and burial among humans. That makes it feel less like a romance and more like a local story about belonging: can a person from the sea ever be fully accepted on land?

## What These Stories Say About Caithness

The mermaids and seal-fairies of Caithness are unsettling because they blur categories that coastal life depended on. Is the thing in the water a seal, a woman, a fairy, a monster, a marketable skin, a witness, or a neighbour? The stories do not answer consistently, and that is why they have lasted.

Three patterns stand out.

**First, Caithness sea legends are unusually place-specific.** Reay, Sandside Bay, Dunnet Bay, Dwarwick Head, Castlehill, Murkle, John o’ Groats and Old Olrig are not decorative names. They are the structure of the tradition. The tales belong to beaches, caves, cliffs, bays and churchyards that made the supernatural feel locally grounded.

**Second, the evidence is mixed but revealing.** The Reay material is unusually well documented through named witnesses, letters, press circulation and later debate. The Dwarwick story is antiquarian folklore preserved in a nineteenth-century county history. The John o’ Groats seal-catcher is a printed folktale with an early nineteenth-century source. The Selkie’s Grave is strongest as modern local tradition and place-based retelling. Each has to be read at its own evidential level.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.shimajournal.org/issues/v12n2/e.-Young-Shima-v12n2.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: shimajournal.org">[shimajournal.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">shimajournal.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">e. Young Shima v12n2</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">e. Young Shima v12n2</span></span></span>

**Third, the stories carry a moral memory of the coast.** The Reay mermaid exposes uncertainty: how do people interpret what they see at sea? The Dwarwick mermaid punishes greed for wreck-wealth and jewels. The John o’ Groats seal-fairies condemn careless killing. The Olrig selkie turns the sea-person into someone buried within the human community but never quite ordinary.

## How the Legends Are Retold Today

Today, Caithness sea legends are kept alive by local heritage writing, storytelling projects, folklore blogs, tours and online retellings. Scotland’s Storybook, produced through TRACS, retells the Selkie Hunter against the Pentland Firth, emphasising dangerous tides, fishing hardship and the belief that selkies could take human form at certain states of the tide.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://tracscotland.org/wp-content/uploads/2019/05/Scotlands-Storybook.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: tracscotland.org">[TRACS]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">tracscotland.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on tracscotland.org.</span></span></span>

At the same time, academic work has made the Reay mermaid newly interesting. Garner’s study treats Elizabeth Mackay’s testimony not as a silly curiosity but as a rare coastal encounter shaped by women’s observation, local marine knowledge and the literary culture of the Romantic period. Young’s study shows how the same event travelled through newspapers, sceptical commentary and local afterlife.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.erudit.org/en/journals/ronbrit/2022-n79-ronbrit010558/1123074ar/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: erudit.org">[Érudit]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">erudit.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on erudit.org.</span></span></span>

That combination is exactly why Caithness is such a rich county for sea folklore. The legends still haunt because they are not only fantasies. They are stories about how people on a dangerous coast made sense of animals, tides, wrecks, profit, fear, compassion and doubt. In Caithness, the mermaid is never just a pretty face in the waves, and the seal is never just a seal.
