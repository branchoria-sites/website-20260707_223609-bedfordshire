## Introduction

The Bute witch trials were a series of seventeenth-century accusations centred on Rothesay parish on the Isle of Bute, where neighbours accused local women of using harmful magic and dealing with hidden supernatural forces. The main outbreak came in 1662, during the wider Scottish witch-hunt of 1661–62, and resulted in several executions. What makes the Bute cases especially important for folklore is the way accusations blended ideas of witchcraft with older island beliefs about fairies, charms, healing, and mysterious powers.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bute_witches" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Bute witches</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Bute witches</span></span></span>



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/Buteshire_f23202_bute_witches_114b64-overview.webp" | relative_url }}" alt="Overview image for Bute Witches" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

The surviving records do not show evidence that supernatural powers existed, but they do reveal how a seventeenth-century island community explained illness, misfortune, personal disputes, and unusual behaviour. The Bute trials preserve a rare picture of how fear, reputation, religion, and folklore could combine to create accusations of witchcraft.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ssns.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2021/11/11_Henderson_Bute_2012_pp_150-161.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ssns.org.uk">[Scottish Society for Northern Studies]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ssns.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Scottish Society for Northern Studies Historic Bute: Land and People</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Scottish Society for Northern Studies Historic Bute: Land and People</span></span></span>

## The Rothesay accusations of 1662

The best-known Bute witch cases took place in the parish of Rothesay during the height of the Great Scottish Witch Hunt. Six women were accused and investigated, with historians identifying Margaret McLevin, Margaret McWilliam, Janet Morrison, and Isobell McNicoll among those executed in 1662. Another accused woman, Jonet McNicoll, escaped imprisonment before execution but was later captured and executed after returning to Bute in 1673.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bute_witches" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Bute witches</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Bute witches</span></span></span>

The accusations followed patterns familiar from other Scottish witch trials. Suspicions often grew from personal disagreements and claims that someone had caused unexplained suffering. In the case of Margaret McWilliam, a neighbour named John McFie accused her after a dispute, claiming that she had caused him severe pains and was responsible for the death of a child. Such stories reflected a common feature of early modern witchcraft accusations: everyday conflicts could become interpreted as evidence of hidden magical harm.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bute_witches" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Bute witches</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Bute witches</span></span></span>

Margaret McWilliam appears to have carried a reputation for witchcraft long before the 1662 trials. Earlier accusations and community rumours had already linked her name with supernatural abilities. This long-standing reputation shows how accusations were not always sudden events; they could develop over many years through repeated stories, suspicions, and local memory.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bute_witches" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Bute witches</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Bute witches</span></span></span>

The scale of the Bute outbreak was unusual for a small island community. Research on Historic Bute notes that fifty-one people were accused during the 1662 episode, twenty-four went to trial, and four were executed at the time, with later records adding further executions connected with the same wave of persecution.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ssns.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2021/11/11_Henderson_Bute_2012_pp_150-161.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ssns.org.uk">[Scottish Society for Northern Studies]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ssns.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Scottish Society for Northern Studies Historic Bute: Land and People</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Scottish Society for Northern Studies Historic Bute: Land and People</span></span></span>

## Fairies, magic, and community fears

The Bute trials are particularly valuable because they show how witchcraft accusations could overlap with fairy belief. Modern readers often separate witches and fairies into different categories, but seventeenth-century communities did not always make such clear distinctions. A person suspected of witchcraft might also be imagined as someone who had contact with hidden beings or supernatural powers.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ssns.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2021/11/11_Henderson_Bute_2012_pp_150-161.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ssns.org.uk">[Scottish Society for Northern Studies]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ssns.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Scottish Society for Northern Studies Historic Bute: Land and People</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Scottish Society for Northern Studies Historic Bute: Land and People</span></span></span>

One of the most memorable traditions connected with the Bute witches concerns Margaret McWilliam and claims that she had links with a fairy. Earlier accused women imprisoned in Rothesay Castle were said to have blamed her and associated her with bringing home a fairy companion. Whether these claims were literal beliefs, hostile accusations, or later retellings, they reveal the importance of [fairy traditions]({{ 'fairy-traditions/' | relative_url }}) in the island’s understanding of danger and mystery.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bute_witches" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Bute witches</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Bute witches</span></span></span>

Other Bute records from the same period also show concern about people who were believed to have unusual knowledge or healing abilities. In 1660, Jeane Campbell of Ambrismore was accused of going with fairies and learning charms from them. Local church records show that practices involving cures and magical language could attract suspicion, even when they were connected to attempts to help rather than harm.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ssns.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2021/11/11_Henderson_Bute_2012_pp_150-161.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ssns.org.uk">[Scottish Society for Northern Studies]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ssns.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Scottish Society for Northern Studies Historic Bute: Land and People</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Scottish Society for Northern Studies Historic Bute: Land and People</span></span></span>

This connection between folk healing, charms, fairies, and witchcraft is one of the most important parts of the Bute story. The accusations were not simply about dramatic ideas of evil magic; they also reflected uncertainty over who could safely hold special knowledge within a close-knit community.

## Why the Bute cases mattered

The Bute witch trials show how island communities responded when ordinary explanations seemed insufficient. Illness, death, arguments, failed relationships, and strange personal behaviour could all be interpreted through a supernatural framework. In this sense, the trials belong both to Scottish legal history and to the folklore of [Buteshire]({{ 'the-strange-stories-behind-buteshires/' | relative_url }}), because they preserve the stories and fears that shaped local ideas about hidden powers.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ssns.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2021/11/11_Henderson_Bute_2012_pp_150-161.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ssns.org.uk">[Scottish Society for Northern Studies]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ssns.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Scottish Society for Northern Studies Historic Bute: Land and People</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Scottish Society for Northern Studies Historic Bute: Land and People</span></span></span>

The island setting also mattered. Bute sat between different cultural worlds, with connections to both Gaelic-speaking traditions and Lowland Scotland. Scholars of Historic Bute have highlighted that the island’s position encouraged the meeting of different customs and beliefs, including attitudes towards fairies, charms, and supernatural explanations.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ssns.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2021/11/11_Henderson_Bute_2012_pp_150-161.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ssns.org.uk">[Scottish Society for Northern Studies]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ssns.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Scottish Society for Northern Studies Historic Bute: Land and People</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Scottish Society for Northern Studies Historic Bute: Land and People</span></span></span>

The tragedy of the trials continued beyond the executions themselves. Families of the accused could carry the stigma of witchcraft accusations for generations. Records from Rothesay show that, even decades later, descendants of accused people could still face insults and accusations connected with the old trials.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ssns.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2021/11/11_Henderson_Bute_2012_pp_150-161.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ssns.org.uk">[Scottish Society for Northern Studies]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ssns.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Scottish Society for Northern Studies Historic Bute: Land and People</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Scottish Society for Northern Studies Historic Bute: Land and People</span></span></span>

## How the witch trials are remembered

Today, the Bute witches are remembered as one of the most significant folklore-linked historical events in Buteshire. The story survives because it combines several themes that remain central to island folklore: mysterious landscapes, fairy belief, community memory, and the uneasy boundary between traditional knowledge and accusations of magic.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://folklorefinder.uk/legends/the-bute-witches" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: folklorefinder.uk">[Folklore Finder]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">folklorefinder.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Folklore Finder The Bute Witches — Folklore Finder</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Folklore Finder The Bute Witches — Folklore Finder</span></span></span>

The modern retelling of the Bute witches requires care. The women accused in 1662 were real people caught in a period of fear and persecution, not simply characters from a ghost story. Their story is valuable because it shows how supernatural beliefs operated in everyday life and how powerful local rumours could become when supported by legal and religious authorities.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ssns.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2021/11/11_Henderson_Bute_2012_pp_150-161.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ssns.org.uk">[Scottish Society for Northern Studies]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ssns.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Scottish Society for Northern Studies Historic Bute: Land and People</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Scottish Society for Northern Studies Historic Bute: Land and People</span></span></span>

Within the wider folklore of Buteshire, the Bute witch trials stand as a reminder that legends often grow from real events. The supernatural elements attached to the cases — fairies, charms, and magical accusations — reveal the imaginative world of the past, while the trial records reveal the very human conflicts and anxieties behind the stories.
