## Introduction

Oakham Castle hangs horseshoes because [Rutland]({{ 'why-rutlands-folklore-is-much-in-little/' | relative_url }})’s best-known custom says that royalty and peers of the realm, on their first visit to Oakham, should forfeit a ceremonial horseshoe to the Lord of the Manor. The result is not a few lucky charms over a doorway, but a whole Great Hall lined with more than 230 oversized, decorated horseshoes, the oldest surviving example traditionally linked to Edward IV in 1470. Oakham Castle itself says the exact origin of the custom is uncertain, but that it continues today.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.oakhamcastle.org/about/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: oakhamcastle.org">[Oakham Castle]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">oakhamcastle.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Oakham Castle About Oakham Castle &#124; Historic Venue in Rutland</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Oakham Castle About Oakham Castle &#124; Historic Venue in Rutland</span></span></span>



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/Rutland_424243_oakham_horseshoes_623301-overview.webp" | relative_url }}" alt="Overview image for Horseshoes" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

For folklore readers, the interest lies in the way the custom joins three things that do not usually sit so neatly together: a manorial right, the protective and lucky reputation of iron, and Rutland’s county identity. The horseshoe is no longer just an object given by aristocratic visitors. It has become the public emblem of Rutland, appearing in local heraldry and on the county flag, where the golden horseshoe is described by the Flag Institute as a traditional Rutland emblem recorded since at least 1784.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.flaginstitute.org/wp/flags/rutland-flag/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: flaginstitute.org">[The Flag Institute]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">flaginstitute.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on flaginstitute.org.</span></span></span>

## The de Ferrers story and manorial forfeits

The usual explanation begins with the de Ferrers family, the Norman lords associated with Oakham after the Conquest. Oakham Castle’s Great Hall was built in the late twelfth century, and Rutland County Council describes it as built in 1190 by Walchelin de Ferrers, with the surviving Great Hall now treated as one of the great examples of Norman domestic architecture in Europe.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.rutland.gov.uk/rutland-information-service/directory/hgrddsxuq4a" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: rutland.gov.uk">[Rutland County Council]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">rutland.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on rutland.gov.uk.</span></span></span> The horseshoe link is often explained through the Ferrers name, its association with iron or farriery, and the family’s heraldic use of horseshoes. Discover Rutland gives the familiar local version: Henry de Ferrers, connected with Duke William’s horses, was granted Oakham and used arms with six black horseshoes on a silver field.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://discover-rutland.co.uk/blog/rutlands-horseshoe-history/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: discover-rutland.co.uk">[Discover Rutland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">discover-rutland.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on discover-rutland.co.uk.</span></span></span>

That story should be read as tradition rather than a fully provable origin document. The castle’s own wording is careful: the custom’s exact beginning is “lost to the mists of time”.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.oakhamcastle.org/about/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: oakhamcastle.org">[Oakham Castle]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">oakhamcastle.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Oakham Castle About Oakham Castle &#124; Historic Venue in Rutland</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Oakham Castle About Oakham Castle &#124; Historic Venue in Rutland</span></span></span> The Flag Institute is similarly cautious, calling the horseshoe custom one “of uncertain origin”.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.flaginstitute.org/wp/flags/rutland-flag/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: flaginstitute.org">[The Flag Institute]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">flaginstitute.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on flaginstitute.org.</span></span></span> This matters because the custom is often presented as if it can be traced neatly back to the Norman Conquest, when the stronger evidence is that it is very old, strongly localised, and historically attached to the lordship of Oakham, but not easy to pin to one founding moment.

The mechanism of the custom is clearer than its origin. A high-ranking visitor to Oakham was expected to provide a horseshoe as a kind of forfeit or token owed to the Lord of the Manor. In older tellings, this can sound like a literal shoe taken from the visitor’s horse, unless redeemed with money; in modern practice it is a symbolic, specially made ceremonial horseshoe. The Royal Family’s account of the Duke and Duchess of Edinburgh’s 2024 visit describes them being asked at Oakham Castle to “forfeit a symbolic horseshoe”, presenting an unusual double horseshoe to join others already displayed in the hall.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.royal.uk/news-and-activity/2024-05-16/the-duke-and-duchess-of-edinburgh-visit-rutland" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: royal.uk">[The Royal Family]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">royal.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on royal.uk.</span></span></span>

What makes the rite memorable is that it turns rank into a local joke with teeth. A duke, duchess, prince, bishop or peer may arrive with national status, but in Oakham the visitor is folded into Rutland’s own rule: first visit, pay the horseshoe. The gesture is polite and ceremonial now, but it preserves the outline of an older manorial world in which rights, dues and honours could be made visible through objects.

## Why Rutland hangs horseshoes open-end down

The Oakham horseshoes are famous not only for their number, size and donors, but for their direction. In much British lucky-horshoe belief, a horseshoe is commonly hung with the open ends upward, so the luck is imagined as being held in the bowl of the shoe. Rutland’s custom turns that expectation upside down. Local accounts repeatedly stress that Rutland horseshoes are hung with the open ends downward, and that this is not treated locally as bad luck. Discover Rutland gives two explanations: the downward shoe prevents the devil from making a nest in the hollow, and it allows luck to fall out on those who pass beneath.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://discover-rutland.co.uk/blog/rutlands-horseshoe-history/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: discover-rutland.co.uk">[Discover Rutland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">discover-rutland.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on discover-rutland.co.uk.</span></span></span>

This is a good example of how folk belief often works. There is no single national rule that everyone follows. A charm can keep its power while its explanation changes from place to place. In one village or household, a horseshoe may be hung open-end up to “hold” luck; in Rutland, the same object can be hung open-end down to “pour” luck or to deny the devil a resting place. The contradiction is part of the tradition, not a problem to be solved.

The devil explanation gives the Oakham custom a protective edge. Horseshoes are iron, and iron has long been treated in British and Irish folk belief as a material that repels harmful forces, especially in stories about fairies, witches and the devil. Oakham does not need a dramatic ghost story for the horseshoes to belong in a folklore map of Rutland. Their folklore lies in the everyday logic of protection: put iron in the right place, turn it the right way, and it guards the threshold, the hall, the town or the people walking beneath it.

The “luck falling down” explanation gives the custom a more public, generous feel. It is not just the private luck of the castle or the donor; it is imagined as luck shared with people below. That makes sense in Oakham Castle’s Great Hall, where the visitor looks up at objects given by rulers and nobles but experiences them as part of a local public story. The charm is aristocratic in origin, but popular in use.

## How the Great Hall became a display of rank

A normal horseshoe would be a working object, shaped for a horse’s hoof and quickly worn, replaced or forgotten. Oakham’s horseshoes are different. They are deliberately ceremonial: oversized, painted, gilded, armorial, dated or decorated to mark the giver’s status. Oakham Castle describes the collection as “ornate ceremonial horseshoes” given by aristocracy and peers of the realm, and says each one has its own story.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.oakhamcastle.org/about/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: oakhamcastle.org">[Oakham Castle]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">oakhamcastle.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Oakham Castle About Oakham Castle &#124; Historic Venue in Rutland</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Oakham Castle About Oakham Castle &#124; Historic Venue in Rutland</span></span></span>

This shift from working shoe to display object is important. The custom may preserve the idea of a forfeit from a passing horse, but the surviving collection is closer to a public register of visits. A donor’s rank, coat of arms, coronet or royal connection becomes fixed to the wall. The hall therefore works almost like a heraldic guest book, except that the entries are iron horseshoes rather than signatures.

The oldest surviving horseshoe is said by Oakham Castle to have been given by Edward IV in 1470.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.oakhamcastle.org/about/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: oakhamcastle.org">[Oakham Castle]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">oakhamcastle.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Oakham Castle About Oakham Castle &#124; Historic Venue in Rutland</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Oakham Castle About Oakham Castle &#124; Historic Venue in Rutland</span></span></span> Local and visitor-facing accounts often connect this with the Battle of Losecoat Field, fought near Empingham in Rutland in the same year, giving the shoe a sharper historical setting than a routine royal call.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.britainexpress.com/attraction-articles.htm?article=19" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: britainexpress.com">[Britain Express]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">britainexpress.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on britainexpress.com.</span></span></span> Whether a visitor comes through war, court duty, hunting, ceremony or modern royal engagement, the end result is the same: the visit is translated into Rutland’s own material language.

Modern examples show the custom still being curated rather than merely remembered. The Duke and Duchess of Edinburgh’s 2024 double horseshoe was presented at Oakham Castle and described by the Royal Family as joining earlier royal horseshoes, including examples from Queen Elizabeth II in 1967 and King Charles III when Prince of Wales in 2003.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.royal.uk/news-and-activity/2024-05-16/the-duke-and-duchess-of-edinburgh-visit-rutland" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: royal.uk">[The Royal Family]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">royal.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on royal.uk.</span></span></span> Rutland County Council’s account of the visit also shows the horseshoe presentation as a central image of the day at Oakham Castle.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.rutland.gov.uk/council-news/2024/05/rutland-welcomes-duke-duchess-edinburgh" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: rutland.gov.uk">[Rutland County Council]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">rutland.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">rutland welcomes duke duchess edinburgh</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">rutland welcomes duke duchess edinburgh</span></span></span>

## How well attested is the custom?

The custom is unusually well attested for a county folklore topic because it is not just an oral tale. It survives as a visible collection, a managed heritage display, a continuing civic ceremony and a county emblem. Oakham Castle is Grade I listed on the National Heritage List for England, with Historic England recording it at Market Place, Oakham, and listing it from 1950.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://historicengland.org.uk/listing/the-list/list-entry/1073277" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: historicengland.org.uk">[Historic England]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">historicengland.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Historic England Oakham Castle, Oakham</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Historic England Oakham Castle, Oakham</span></span></span> The horseshoes themselves are not a rumour attached to the building; they are the central visitor-facing feature of the Great Hall.

There is also a local historical literature around the castle and the collection. A Rutland Local History and Record Society publication on Oakham Castle’s archaeology lists specialist works including T. H. McK. Clough’s *The Horseshoes of Oakham Castle*, first published by Leicestershire Museums in 1978 and later revised, as well as J. D. The uncertainty lies not in whether the custom exists, but in how far back its exact form can be proven. ## How the custom became Rutland’s emblem

The horseshoe has escaped the castle walls. It is now one of the quickest visual ways to say “Rutland”. The Flag Institute states that the Rutland flag is a banner of the arms granted to Rutland County Council in 1950, and that the golden horseshoe on green has been a traditional emblem of the county since at least 1784.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.flaginstitute.org/wp/flags/rutland-flag/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: flaginstitute.org">[The Flag Institute]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">flaginstitute.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on flaginstitute.org.</span></span></span> The same page directly links the emblem to the Oakham Castle custom: first-time peer visitors must donate a ceremonial horseshoe, and the Great Hall now displays the collection.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.flaginstitute.org/wp/flags/rutland-flag/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: flaginstitute.org">[The Flag Institute]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">flaginstitute.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on flaginstitute.org.</span></span></span>

That transformation is what makes Oakham Castle’s horseshoes more than a castle curiosity. Rutland is England’s smallest historic county, and its identity has often been expressed through compact, memorable symbols. A single golden horseshoe does a great deal of work: it recalls Oakham, the de Ferrers tradition, aristocratic visits, luck, iron, local pride and the county’s long struggle to be recognised as itself rather than absorbed into a larger neighbour.

The emblem also makes the tradition unusually portable. A visitor can see the horseshoes in the Great Hall, then notice the same idea repeated in flags, local ironwork, souvenirs, signs and door furniture around Oakham. The castle contains the collection, but the town and county carry the sign.

## Why this is Rutland’s clearest folklore landmark

Oakham Castle’s horseshoes stand out because they are both story and thing. Many local legends depend on a remembered tale: a haunted room, a fairy road, a witchcraft accusation, a vanished custom. Oakham’s horseshoes can be seen, counted, conserved and added to. At the same time, they keep the shape of folklore because their meaning depends on custom, luck, local explanation and repeated telling.

They also show how folklore can live inside formal institutions. This is not an underground or marginal tradition. It is preserved by a council-run heritage site, recognised through flag and arms, performed during royal visits, and explained to school groups and visitors. Yet it still uses the language of old belief: forfeits, lucky iron, the devil, first visits, and the right way up for a charm.

For Rutland, the custom matters because it gives the county a public ritual that is unmistakably its own. The horseshoes turn the Great Hall into a memory-wall of power passing through a small place and being made to obey a local rule. That is why the answer to “Why does Oakham Castle hang horseshoes?” is richer than “for luck”. Oakham hangs them because a manorial forfeit became a charm, a charm became a county badge, and a county badge became Rutland’s most visible folklore tradition.
