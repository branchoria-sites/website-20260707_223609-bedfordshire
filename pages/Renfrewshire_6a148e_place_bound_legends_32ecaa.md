## Introduction

[Renfrewshire]({{ 'where-renfrewshires-legends-still/' | relative_url }})’s place-bound legends are best understood as stories fastened to crossings: a carved cross beside a burn, saintly stones beside an old church route, the meeting of the Cart and Clyde, a ferry slip, a crossroads memorial, and walking routes through Paisley that turn history into local memory. The county’s folklore is not mainly a catalogue of creatures. It is a map of named spots where older religious belief, travel, punishment, healing, trade and remembrance have gathered into story.



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/Renfrewshire_6a148e_place_bound_legends_32ecaa-overview.webp" | relative_url }}" alt="Overview image for Legendary Places" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

The key example is the Barochan Cross, now in Paisley Abbey but originally associated with the Barochan landscape near Houston. Historic Environment Scotland dates it to about AD 900–1100 and treats it as one of the most important surviving carved stones from early medieval Strathclyde. Its significance depends not just on its carving, but on the route, ford, burn, hill and lordly landscape around its former site.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://app-hes-pubs-prod-neu-01.azurewebsites.net/api/file/19185dfd-12b4-402b-893e-b09800ca4f90" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: app-hes-pubs-prod-neu-01.azurewebsites.net">[HES Publications]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">app-hes-pubs-prod-neu-01.azurewebsites.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">HES Publications Barochan Cross Statement of Significance</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">HES Publications Barochan Cross Statement of Significance</span></span></span>

## Why Renfrewshire legends cling to places

Historic Renfrewshire sits on the south side of the Clyde and Firth of Clyde, with Paisley, Renfrew, the Cart waters, the Clyde crossings and the old coastal towns forming a compact but heavily travelled landscape. In historic-county terms, the area is broader than today’s Renfrewshire Council area, because the older county also relates to places now in Inverclyde and East Renfrewshire; that matters because roads, rivers, ferry rights, parish boundaries and oral traditions do not stop at modern council borders.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://wikishire.co.uk/wiki/Renfrewshire" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wikishire.co.uk">[Wikishire]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wikishire.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Renfrewshire6 May 2022 — Renfrewshire lies along the broad waters of the River Clyde and along the Firth of Clyde, and along thi...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 2022</span></span></span>

This helps explain the character of the county’s folklore. Renfrewshire’s legends often attach themselves to public or semi-public markers: a cross base, a ferry green, a stone enclosure, a river bend, a gallows site, a town crossroads. These are not remote mythic settings. They are the kinds of places people passed on the way to church, market, work, harbour, mill, court or ferry.

That makes the county’s legendary geography unusually legible. The same site can hold several layers at once: an early Christian monument, an antiquarian puzzle, a local outing, a conservation problem, and a modern visitor stop. The story is not just “what happened here?” but “why did this place keep being remembered?”

## The Barochan Cross and Strathclyde memory

The Barochan Cross is Renfrewshire’s strongest example of a monument whose meaning is inseparable from place. Historic Environment Scotland describes it as a free-standing sandstone cross of roughly tenth-century date, now in Paisley Abbey after being moved there in 1981 for protection. It is said to have stood originally near Barochan Mill, near Houston, before being shifted in the nineteenth century to a nearby knoll as a landscape feature.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://app-hes-pubs-prod-neu-01.azurewebsites.net/api/file/19185dfd-12b4-402b-893e-b09800ca4f90" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: app-hes-pubs-prod-neu-01.azurewebsites.net">[HES Publications]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">app-hes-pubs-prod-neu-01.azurewebsites.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">HES Publications Barochan Cross Statement of Significance</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">HES Publications Barochan Cross Statement of Significance</span></span></span>

The cross is not just a decorative Christian object. HES records that it is carved on all four sides, with interlace, key pattern and figural scenes including a mounted warrior with a spear, a man carrying a drinking horn, men with weapons, opposed animals, long-robed figures, and figures with trumpets and spears. That mixture of Christian form and martial imagery is one reason the cross is so important for understanding early medieval Strathclyde.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://app-hes-pubs-prod-neu-01.azurewebsites.net/api/file/19185dfd-12b4-402b-893e-b09800ca4f90" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: app-hes-pubs-prod-neu-01.azurewebsites.net">[HES Publications]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">app-hes-pubs-prod-neu-01.azurewebsites.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">HES Publications Barochan Cross Statement of Significance</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">HES Publications Barochan Cross Statement of Significance</span></span></span>

What makes the Barochan Cross especially valuable for this page is the way its original landscape setting changes the interpretation. HES notes that the cross did not appear to be directly attached to a known church. Instead, its original location was near a burn ford, close to a waterfall, at a point where low rolling land begins to rise more steeply. The nearby route could lead towards a Clyde crossing and onwards to Dumbarton Rock, a political centre of British Strathclyde.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://app-hes-pubs-prod-neu-01.azurewebsites.net/api/file/19185dfd-12b4-402b-893e-b09800ca4f90" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: app-hes-pubs-prod-neu-01.azurewebsites.net">[HES Publications]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">app-hes-pubs-prod-neu-01.azurewebsites.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">HES Publications Barochan Cross Statement of Significance</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">HES Publications Barochan Cross Statement of Significance</span></span></span>

That is why the cross belongs in a discussion of routes as much as religion. In a churchyard, a cross might seem mainly devotional. Beside a ford and routeway, near a lordly centre, it may also have acted as a marker of power, approach and territorial memory. HES is careful not to claim a final interpretation, but its statement of significance stresses that the cross’s biography, original setting and relationship to communication routes are central to appreciating its full meaning.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://app-hes-pubs-prod-neu-01.azurewebsites.net/api/file/19185dfd-12b4-402b-893e-b09800ca4f90" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: app-hes-pubs-prod-neu-01.azurewebsites.net">[HES Publications]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">app-hes-pubs-prod-neu-01.azurewebsites.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">HES Publications Barochan Cross Statement of Significance</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">HES Publications Barochan Cross Statement of Significance</span></span></span>

The cross also shows how local folklore and heritage can shift when an object is moved. Its original base still lies at Barochan, while the cross itself stands in Paisley Abbey. HES records that local feeling favoured keeping it in the parish, and that the move to Paisley gave the object a new value for the Abbey congregation while separating it from its older landscape.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://app-hes-pubs-prod-neu-01.azurewebsites.net/api/file/19185dfd-12b4-402b-893e-b09800ca4f90" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: app-hes-pubs-prod-neu-01.azurewebsites.net">[HES Publications]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">app-hes-pubs-prod-neu-01.azurewebsites.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">HES Publications Barochan Cross Statement of Significance</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">HES Publications Barochan Cross Statement of Significance</span></span></span>

For visitors, this creates a double place-memory. Paisley Abbey now offers safe access to the cross itself, while Barochan keeps the ghost of the original setting: the base, the burn, the route and the hill. The legend-like power of the monument lies in that split. The stone can be seen in town, but fully imagined only by remembering the route-country from which it came.

## St Conval’s stones and the river-crossing imagination

Renfrewshire’s stone legends are not limited to Barochan. The Argyll Stones at Renfrew, including the stone known as St Conval’s Chariot, preserve a classic saintly arrival story tied to the Clyde and Cart landscape. A Renfrewshire Council heritage leaflet says that, according to tradition, St Conval was resting on a stone in sixth-century Ireland when it floated out to sea, carried him up the Firth of Clyde, and came ashore at Renfrew. He then founded his church across the River Cart at Inchinnan.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www1.renfrewshire.gov.uk/media/2706/Argyll-Stones/pdf/ArygllStones.pdf?m=1467367028317" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: www1.renfrewshire.gov.uk">[Renfrewshire Council]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">www1.renfrewshire.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Renfrewshire Council</span></span></span>

The same account says the miracle stone later became the base of a cross at the church, and that travellers and sick people made pilgrimages to drink rainwater gathered in its hollow, believing it had healing properties. This is exactly the sort of tradition that turns geography into sacred memory: sea crossing, landfall, river crossing, church foundation, stone hollow and healing practice all join in one place-bound story.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www1.renfrewshire.gov.uk/media/2706/Argyll-Stones/pdf/ArygllStones.pdf?m=1467367028317" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: www1.renfrewshire.gov.uk">[Renfrewshire Council]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">www1.renfrewshire.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Renfrewshire Council</span></span></span>

The second Argyll Stone carries a different kind of memory. It is linked in local tradition to Archibald Campbell, 9th Earl of Argyll, who was captured in 1685 after his failed rising against the king. The council account says legend claimed the stone was stained with his blood for many years after his death, though that belongs to later political memory rather than early saintly tradition.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www1.renfrewshire.gov.uk/media/2706/Argyll-Stones/pdf/ArygllStones.pdf?m=1467367028317" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: www1.renfrewshire.gov.uk">[Renfrewshire Council]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">www1.renfrewshire.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Renfrewshire Council</span></span></span>

Together, these stones show how Renfrewshire places can hold competing time-depths. One tradition imagines a saint floating across the sea on a miraculous stone; another recalls a fugitive nobleman and a failed rebellion. Both are attached to stones beside routes and crossings, and both make the landscape feel storied without needing a single dominant county myth.

## Rivers, ferries and old parish routes

The Clyde and Cart are not just background scenery in Renfrewshire folklore. They are the county’s main engines of movement and memory. The River Cart itself is short, formed by the meeting of the Black Cart Water and White Cart Water before joining the Clyde, but the Cart and White Cart were historically navigable as far as Paisley. Wikishire describes the river as only three-quarters of a mile after the waters meet, while also noting its importance as a navigation route to Paisley.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://wikishire.co.uk/wiki/River_Cart" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wikishire.co.uk">[Wikishire]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wikishire.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">River Cart</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">River Cart</span></span></span>

Paisley’s relationship with the White Cart gives the town a watery edge as well as an industrial one. Paisley People’s Archive notes that the White Cart once flowed around the boundary of Paisley before entering the town from the east and winding towards Seedhill, where antiquarian writers admired a picturesque waterfall above Seedhill Bridge.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.paisleypeoplesarchive.org/paisley-snapshots/white-cart-waters.aspx" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: paisleypeoplesarchive.org">[paisleypeoplesarchive.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">paisleypeoplesarchive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on paisleypeoplesarchive.org.</span></span></span>

Renfrew’s name and identity also belong to this meeting of waters. Paisley.is explains the town’s name as deriving from old British words meaning “point of the current”, linked to the place where the River Cart joins the Clyde. The same account places Renfrew’s medieval importance in the age of Walter Fitz Alan, the High Steward of Scotland, and the royal castle built there.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://paisley.is/stories-from-renfrewshire/history-and-heritage-of-renfrew/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: paisley.is">[Paisley]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">paisley.is</span><span class="citation-popover-title">History and heritage of Renfrew</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">History and heritage of Renfrew</span></span></span>

The Renfrew Ferry is a particularly vivid route-memory. A Renfrewshire Council heritage leaflet says Renfrew had the “right of ferry” from medieval times, with records of a ferry service as early as 1614. The present crossing point developed in the 1780s after Alexander Speirs, a Glasgow tobacco merchant, arranged for the ferry to be moved west and offered quays, a ferry house and a new road.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www1.renfrewshire.gov.uk/media/2705/Ferry-Green/pdf/FerryGreen.pdf?m=1467366974237" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: www1.renfrewshire.gov.uk">[Renfrewshire Council]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">www1.renfrewshire.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Renfrewshire Council</span></span></span>

That ferry route became part of everyday lore because it was used by ordinary people in extraordinary numbers. The same leaflet records that when the Queen Mary was docked at Clydebank on Sunday 8 March 1936, a census counted 21,386 passengers, 946 motor cars, 141 motorcycles, 819 pedal cycles and 67 vans crossing there. The ferry was not a supernatural site, but it was a place where generations experienced the Clyde as a boundary to be crossed rather than merely viewed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www1.renfrewshire.gov.uk/media/2705/Ferry-Green/pdf/FerryGreen.pdf?m=1467366974237" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: www1.renfrewshire.gov.uk">[Renfrewshire Council]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">www1.renfrewshire.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Renfrewshire Council</span></span></span>

Recent changes add a modern layer to this route-memory. The Renfrew to Yoker ferry ceased operation on 31 May 2025 after the opening of the new Renfrew Bridge made passenger numbers unviable. Reports described the closure as the end of a long-running Clyde crossing tradition, while the new bridge turned a formerly ferry-bound route into a road, cycle and pedestrian connection.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Renfrew_Ferry" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Renfrew Ferry</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Renfrew Ferry</span></span></span>

## Crossroads, gallows and the Paisley witch memory

Renfrewshire’s most painful place-bound legend is attached not to an ancient cross but to a town crossroads. The Paisley or Bargarran witch hunt of 1697 is a broader story than this page can retell in full, but its survival in public memory depends heavily on two Paisley places: Gallow Green, where the executions took place, and Maxwellton Cross, where the remains were remembered. Paisley Tours’ witch walk describes the route as a research-led tour through real locations connected with the 1697 events, including Gallow Green and Maxwellton Cross.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.paisleytours.org.uk/witches-walking-tour?srsltid=AfmBOooOjfVGIv0uVBRYEchoA7oxqDvqrsarasM2QUcMo6JNW48BUbsG" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: paisleytours.org.uk">[Paisley Tours]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">paisleytours.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on paisleytours.org.uk.</span></span></span>

Maxwellton Cross has become especially important because of the horseshoe tradition. Paisley’s Enchanted Threads says that, some time after the executed people’s remains were buried there, a horseshoe was placed on the site, supposedly to protect the local community from curses cast before the hangings. The site therefore combines legal violence, fear of witchcraft, folk protection and later remembrance in one urban marker.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.paisleysenchantedthreads.co.uk/the-story/the-places/maxwellton-cross/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: paisleysenchantedthreads.co.uk">[Paisley&#x27;s Enchanted Threads]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">paisleysenchantedthreads.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">maxwellton cross</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">maxwellton cross</span></span></span>

The modern memorial reframes the story away from fear of witches and towards recognition of injustice. Paisley Tours notes that the horseshoe became a marker of remembrance and that the site was later marked by a carved granite tondo, replaced in 2008 by a bronze memorial designed by Alexander Stoddart.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.paisleytours.org.uk/witches-walking-tour?srsltid=AfmBOooRklvtENQ0qEywy9kxFuP6GZc5TInXa7qLYwvV-BO_DAqpNYDf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: paisleytours.org.uk">[Paisley Tours]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">paisleytours.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">witches walking tour</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">witches walking tour</span></span></span>

This is a good example of how modern visitors retell place-bound legend responsibly. The old story says the horseshoe held down a curse; the modern interpretation asks why a community came to believe that, and what it reveals about panic, accusation, punishment and memory. The place still matters, but its meaning has changed.

## How modern visitors retell place-bound legends

Renfrewshire’s legendary places are now encountered in several different ways. Some are preserved as formal heritage: Barochan Cross is in the care of Historic Environment Scotland and displayed inside Paisley Abbey for conservation reasons. Some are explained through local council heritage material, such as the Argyll Stones and Ferry Green. Others are carried through guided walks, local history groups, online visitor accounts and town-centre memorials.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.historicenvironment.scot/visit/all/barochan-cross/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: historicenvironment.scot">[historicenvironment.scot]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">historicenvironment.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-title">barochan cross</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">barochan cross</span></span></span>

This mixture can be powerful, but it also requires care. A carved cross from early medieval Strathclyde is not the same kind of evidence as a saint’s floating stone, a bloodstain legend or a protective horseshoe. The strongest reading keeps the categories separate:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **Material heritage:** Barochan Cross, its base, carvings, relocations and conservation history.
* **Saintly tradition:** St Conval’s Chariot and the healing water associated with the stone’s hollow.
* **Route memory:** the Clyde and Cart crossings, ferry rights, roads, quays and parish approaches.
* **Dark civic folklore:** Maxwellton Cross, Gallow Green and the protective horseshoe tradition.
* **Modern retelling:** walking tours, memorial plaques, local history interpretation and visitor storytelling.

</div>

The value of Renfrewshire’s place-bound legends is that they make the county readable on foot. A visitor can begin in Paisley Abbey with the Barochan Cross, think westwards to Houston and Barochan, follow the Cart and Clyde imagination towards Renfrew and Inchinnan, and then return to Paisley’s streets where Maxwellton Cross and Gallow Green hold a very different kind of memory.

## Why these places matter

Renfrewshire’s crosses, routes and place-bound legends matter because they show how folklore can survive without a single famous monster or tidy heroic myth. Here, story lives in the stubborn attachment between memory and location. The cross is important because it once stood by a route. The saint’s stone matters because it arrives by water and founds a church across a river. The ferry becomes memorable because it carried people through centuries of work and travel. The witch memorial matters because a crossroads turned punishment, fear and remorse into a lasting civic symbol.

That is why Renfrewshire’s folklore feels so local. It asks readers not simply to believe a tale, but to notice where the tale has lodged: in stone, road, river, burn, ferry slip, parish site and town crossing. The county’s legendary map is made of places people used, passed, feared, protected and revisited.
