## Introduction

The North Berwick witch trials of 1590–92 made [East Lothian]({{ 'where-east-lothians-legends-still-have/' | relative_url }}) one of the most famous places in Scotland’s witchcraft imagination. The story is rooted in real prosecutions, real torture and real deaths, but the folklore that survived is the lurid storm-raising narrative: witches meeting the Devil at North Berwick kirk, using sea magic to sink King James VI and Anne of Denmark as they returned from Scandinavia. The most important point for modern readers is that this was not a charming local legend first and a court case second. It was a violent panic in which forced confessions, royal fear, printed propaganda and older ideas about cunning folk and harmful magic fused into a story powerful enough to outlive the people destroyed by it.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gla.ac.uk/myglasgow/library/files/special/exhibns/month/aug2000.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gla.ac.uk">[gla.ac.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gla.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">University of Glasgow Newes from Scotland</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">University of Glasgow Newes from Scotland</span></span></span>

For East Lothian, the trials attach supernatural memory to a recognisable coastal landscape: Tranent, Haddington, Prestonpans, North Berwick, the Firth of Forth, and the ruined St Andrew’s Auld Kirk beside the harbour. For Scottish folklore more widely, they helped fix an unforgettable image of witches as weather-workers, sea-cursers and servants of a Devil who supposedly saw the king as his chief enemy.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gla.ac.uk/myglasgow/library/files/special/exhibns/month/aug2000.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gla.ac.uk">[University of Glasgow]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gla.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">University of Glasgow Newes from Scotland</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">University of Glasgow Newes from Scotland</span></span></span>

## The 1590–92 Panic and the People Accused

The panic began locally before it became royal theatre. One of the first named figures was Geillis Duncan, a maidservant in Tranent, suspected by her employer, David Seton or Smeaton, because she was said to go out secretly at night and to heal illness in apparently miraculous ways. The University of Glasgow’s account of *Newes from Scotland* describes how she was tortured with pilliwinks on her fingers and with a rope or cord about her head before being treated as marked by the Devil and pushed into confession. Once imprisoned, she named others, including Agnes Sampson, Doctor Fian, Barbara Napier and Euphame MacCalzean; around 70 people were implicated in the case.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gla.ac.uk/myglasgow/library/files/special/exhibns/month/aug2000.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gla.ac.uk">[University of Glasgow]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gla.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">University of Glasgow Newes from Scotland</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">University of Glasgow Newes from Scotland</span></span></span>

That pattern matters because it shows how the North Berwick story was built. It did not begin with a proven coven discovered at midnight. It began with suspicion, coercion and the early modern habit of interpreting healing knowledge as potentially diabolical. Agnes Sampson, later one of the most famous accused women, was known around the east of Edinburgh, North Berwick and Haddington as the “Wise Wife of Keith”. She was associated with healing wounds, childbirth, treatment of people and animals, weather prediction and protection against harmful spells. In a community that both used and feared such skills, a helpful reputation could become dangerous very quickly.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://origins.osu.edu/read/agnes-sampson-witchcraft-edinburgh" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: origins.osu.edu">[Origins]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">origins.osu.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Origins The Execution of Agnes Sampson for Witchcraft in Edinburgh &#124; Origins</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Origins The Execution of Agnes Sampson for Witchcraft in Edinburgh &#124; Origins</span></span></span>

The people caught in the trials were not all of one social type. The surviving legal material discussed by Lawrence Normand and Gareth Roberts includes dittays, or formal indictments, for John Fian, Agnes Sampson, Barbara Napier and Euphame MacCalzean, as well as later material connected with Francis Stewart, Earl of Bothwell. This matters because the trials were not simply village gossip: they entered the machinery of royal politics, high treason, court faction and printed public narrative.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.jstor.org/stable/j.ctt5vjmvw" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: jstor.org">[JSTOR]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">jstor.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on jstor.org.</span></span></span>

The human cost is often blurred by the famous imagery. Some popular retellings speak loosely of 70 or more executions, while better-cautious accounts stress that the exact number tried, tortured and executed is uncertain. What is secure is that Agnes Sampson and Doctor Fian were executed, that Geillis Duncan’s confession was extracted under torture, and that many others were pulled into the investigation by accusation and forced testimony.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gla.ac.uk/myglasgow/library/files/special/exhibns/month/aug2000.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gla.ac.uk">[gla.ac.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gla.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">University of Glasgow Newes from Scotland</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">University of Glasgow Newes from Scotland</span></span></span>

## Devil Meetings, Sea Magic and the Storm Legend

The scene that made North Berwick famous is the alleged witches’ meeting at the kirk. In the printed pamphlet *Newes from Scotland*, Agnes Sampson’s confession becomes a highly coloured story of as many as 200 witches meeting the Devil at North Berwick kirk. The Devil supposedly instructed them in spells, including the throwing of a dead cat into the sea to raise a storm against King James VI as he travelled back from Denmark with Anne. The pamphlet gives the Devil a pointed political line: the king was “the greatest enemie” he had in the world.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gla.ac.uk/myglasgow/library/files/special/exhibns/month/aug2000.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gla.ac.uk">[University of Glasgow]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gla.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">University of Glasgow Newes from Scotland</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">University of Glasgow Newes from Scotland</span></span></span>

This storm-raising folklore had several parts that made it unforgettable. It was coastal, theatrical and easy to picture: a ruined kirk by the water, a nocturnal gathering, music and dance, a cat, corpse-parts, the sea, and a royal ship threatened by supernatural weather. In folklore terms, the tale turns ordinary North Sea danger into a magical conspiracy. Storms were not rare in late autumn and winter, but in this story they became evidence of intentional harm.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://origins.osu.edu/read/agnes-sampson-witchcraft-edinburgh" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: origins.osu.edu">[Origins]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">origins.osu.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Origins The Execution of Agnes Sampson for Witchcraft in Edinburgh &#124; Origins</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Origins The Execution of Agnes Sampson for Witchcraft in Edinburgh &#124; Origins</span></span></span>

The royal voyage gave the accusation unusual force. James VI had travelled to Scandinavia to marry Anne of Denmark after storms delayed her voyage to Scotland; the couple eventually returned in April 1590 after further difficult weather. Later, as the North Berwick accusations unfolded, the storms were reinterpreted as witchcraft aimed at the king and queen. This royal connection made the case far more explosive than a local quarrel about illness, healing or neighbourly malice.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://origins.osu.edu/read/agnes-sampson-witchcraft-edinburgh" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: origins.osu.edu">[Origins]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">origins.osu.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Origins The Execution of Agnes Sampson for Witchcraft in Edinburgh &#124; Origins</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Origins The Execution of Agnes Sampson for Witchcraft in Edinburgh &#124; Origins</span></span></span>

The Danish connection also matters. Claims about witches raising storms against royal ships were circulating in Denmark before the Scottish panic reached its full force. Modern discussion of the case has often stressed that James encountered a Scandinavian witch-hunting atmosphere at the same time as he was dealing with political insecurity at home. The North Berwick trials therefore sit at a crossroads: East Lothian local accusation, North Sea weather anxiety, Danish witch-hunt ideas and Scottish royal fear.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_Berwick_witch_trials" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">North Berwick witch trials</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">North Berwick witch trials</span></span></span>

## Why North Berwick Became a Folklore Landmark

North Berwick became central to Scottish witchcraft folklore partly because the story was printed early and vividly. The University of Glasgow identifies *Newes from Scotland*, published in London in 1591, as the earliest tract on Scottish witchcraft. It claimed to give a true account of the North Berwick trials and appeared soon after the events, with woodcuts that gave readers a visual language for imagining Scottish witches, Devil meetings and royal judgement.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gla.ac.uk/myglasgow/library/files/special/exhibns/month/aug2000.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gla.ac.uk">[University of Glasgow]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gla.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">University of Glasgow Newes from Scotland</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">University of Glasgow Newes from Scotland</span></span></span>

Yet the same source must be handled critically. *Newes from Scotland* was not a neutral transcript of events. It was sensational print culture, shaped by the appetite for extraordinary stories and by the political value of presenting James VI as a godly ruler resisting diabolical conspiracy. Normand and Roberts describe the pamphlet in the context of witch-hunt propaganda, and their edition notes that it was the first work printed in Scotland or England devoted solely to Scottish witchcraft.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.jstor.org/stable/j.ctt5vjmvw" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: jstor.org">[JSTOR]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">jstor.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on jstor.org.</span></span></span>

The trials also became famous because James himself became part of the story. According to the Glasgow account, James first treated some of the tales as exaggerated, reportedly calling the accused “extreame lyars”, but changed his attitude after Agnes Sampson was said to have told him private words exchanged between him and Anne on their wedding night. Whether that episode reflects fact, performance, fear or later narrative shaping, it helped turn the trial record into legend: the accused woman appears not merely as a suspect but as someone with impossible knowledge of the royal bedchamber.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gla.ac.uk/myglasgow/library/files/special/exhibns/month/aug2000.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gla.ac.uk">[University of Glasgow]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gla.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">University of Glasgow Newes from Scotland</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">University of Glasgow Newes from Scotland</span></span></span>

Doctor Fian added another enduring thread. He was described as a schoolmaster at Saltpans and, in the pamphlet, as a clerk or register to the Devil’s service. The tale even includes a grotesquely comic love-spell episode involving hair allegedly taken from a cow’s udder rather than from the intended woman. Glasgow’s commentary rightly notes the disturbing contrast: a story that reads like farce sits inside a process that ended in torture and execution.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gla.ac.uk/myglasgow/library/files/special/exhibns/month/aug2000.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gla.ac.uk">[University of Glasgow]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gla.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">University of Glasgow Newes from Scotland</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">University of Glasgow Newes from Scotland</span></span></span>

## Folklore, Propaganda and the Problem of “Confession”

The North Berwick material is rich folklore, but it is dangerous evidence if read too literally. The core stories come from confessions extracted in a culture where torture, sleep deprivation, bodily searching and leading questions could produce the answers investigators expected. Normand and Roberts note that early modern witchcraft procedure admitted rumour and reputation alongside alleged words and actions, and that repeated interrogations and confrontations between witnesses formed part of the process.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.jstor.org/stable/j.ctt5vjmvw" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: jstor.org">[JSTOR]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">jstor.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on jstor.org.</span></span></span>

That does not make the stories worthless. It changes what they are evidence for. They do not prove that witches met the Devil at North Berwick kirk or raised storms with cats and corpse-parts. They do show what late sixteenth-century authorities, neighbours, pamphleteers and readers thought witchcraft could look like. The folklore value lies in the imagery, fears and accusations: women and men gathering in a liminal coastal place, the Devil parodying church order, music and dance becoming suspicious, healing knowledge becoming diabolical, and the sea becoming a magical weapon.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gla.ac.uk/myglasgow/library/files/special/exhibns/month/aug2000.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gla.ac.uk">[University of Glasgow]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gla.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">University of Glasgow Newes from Scotland</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">University of Glasgow Newes from Scotland</span></span></span>

The “storm” motif is especially revealing. In a maritime county like East Lothian, weather was never abstract. The Firth of Forth, North Sea crossings, fishing, pilgrimage routes and royal travel all depended on wind and water. The North Berwick legend turned that environmental vulnerability into a story of intentional supernatural attack. In that sense, the storm-raising folklore belongs to the same landscape as the harbour, Bass Rock and the exposed coast: it is a story about what people feared the sea could do, and how they explained danger before modern meteorology.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://origins.osu.edu/read/agnes-sampson-witchcraft-edinburgh" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: origins.osu.edu">[Origins]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">origins.osu.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Origins The Execution of Agnes Sampson for Witchcraft in Edinburgh &#124; Origins</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Origins The Execution of Agnes Sampson for Witchcraft in Edinburgh &#124; Origins</span></span></span>

There is also a sharp social lesson. Cunning folk were often useful until they became suspect. Agnes Sampson’s healing and weather knowledge could be valued in one setting and treated as proof of demonic power in another. That ambiguity is one reason witchcraft stories endure in local folklore: they sit at the uneasy boundary between help and harm, medicine and magic, neighbourly trust and communal fear.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://origins.osu.edu/read/agnes-sampson-witchcraft-edinburgh" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: origins.osu.edu">[Origins]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">origins.osu.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Origins The Execution of Agnes Sampson for Witchcraft in Edinburgh &#124; Origins</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Origins The Execution of Agnes Sampson for Witchcraft in Edinburgh &#124; Origins</span></span></span>

## The East Lothian Landscape Behind the Story

North Berwick’s St Andrew’s Auld Kirk gives the legend a physical focus. The supposed Devil meeting was attached to the old kirk and kirkyard around the harbour, a place already layered with religious and maritime history. Later local and visitor accounts still point to the ruined kirk as the place where the accused were said to have gathered, even though the trial machinery itself also ran through Edinburgh prisons and courts.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://fabulousnorth.com/st-andrews-auld-kirk/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: fabulousnorth.com">[fabulousnorth.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">fabulousnorth.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on fabulousnorth.com.</span></span></span>

Tranent is just as important to the beginning of the story. Geillis Duncan’s accusation arose there, in a household and local-official context, before the narrative widened into North Berwick meetings and royal storms. Haddingtonshire, the historic county name for East Lothian, therefore matters not as a decorative label but as the social geography of the panic: servants, healers, schoolmasters, local lairds, kirk spaces, coastal routes and royal politics were all drawn together across a relatively compact Lowland landscape.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gla.ac.uk/myglasgow/library/files/special/exhibns/month/aug2000.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gla.ac.uk">[University of Glasgow]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gla.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">University of Glasgow Newes from Scotland</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">University of Glasgow Newes from Scotland</span></span></span>

The ruin of St Andrew’s Auld Kirk has its own later folklore afterlife. Accounts of the site note that a storm in 1656 caused much of the old church to collapse into the sea, and some people later interpreted that as divine retribution for the alleged witch meetings of the 1590s. That later interpretation shows how folklore grows by layering events: a seventeenth-century storm damages a medieval church, and memory connects it back to the earlier storm-raising legend.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.undiscoveredscotland.co.uk/northberwick/standrewsoldkirk/index.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: undiscoveredscotland.co.uk">[Undiscovered Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">undiscoveredscotland.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Undiscovered Scotland St Andrew&#x27;s Old Kirk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Undiscovered Scotland St Andrew&#x27;s Old Kirk</span></span></span>

For a modern visitor, the place can look almost too peaceful for the story attached to it. That contrast is part of the power of East Lothian folklore. The legend is not set in a remote fantasy realm; it is tied to a harbour, a kirk ruin, nearby towns and a stretch of coast that people still walk through. The visible landscape makes the tale memorable, while the legal history prevents it from becoming merely picturesque.

## How the Story Is Retold Today

Today the North Berwick trials are retold in several overlapping ways: as Scottish witchcraft history, as East Lothian dark heritage, as storm-raising folklore, as feminist memorial culture, and as an example of how propaganda can turn persecution into legend. The most responsible retellings keep two truths together: the imagery is culturally powerful, and the accusations were produced by a system that tortured people into naming others.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gla.ac.uk/myglasgow/library/files/special/exhibns/month/aug2000.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gla.ac.uk">[University of Glasgow]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gla.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">University of Glasgow Newes from Scotland</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">University of Glasgow Newes from Scotland</span></span></span>

The story also feeds wider Scottish witch imagery. James VI’s later *Daemonologie*, published in 1597, belongs to the same intellectual and political climate, and the North Berwick affair helped fix the association between Scottish witches, storms, cats, corpse magic and royal danger. Popular claims about influence on *Macbeth* should be made carefully, but it is fair to say that the trials, James’s demonological interests and the printed imagery of Scottish witchcraft form part of the cultural background from which later witch scenes drew energy.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gla.ac.uk/myglasgow/library/files/special/exhibns/month/aug2000.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gla.ac.uk">[University of Glasgow]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gla.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">University of Glasgow Newes from Scotland</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">University of Glasgow Newes from Scotland</span></span></span>

Modern retellings also increasingly restore the accused as people rather than monsters. Agnes Sampson is no longer only the “witch” who supposedly knew the king’s secrets; she is also a healer, midwife and local woman destroyed by fear. Geillis Duncan is no longer only the first confessor in a lurid pamphlet; she is also a tortured servant whose statements under duress set off a chain of accusations. Doctor Fian’s strange comic episodes are no longer just entertaining folklore; they expose how absurd material could become deadly when placed inside a witchcraft court.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://origins.osu.edu/read/agnes-sampson-witchcraft-edinburgh" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: origins.osu.edu">[Origins]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">origins.osu.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Origins The Execution of Agnes Sampson for Witchcraft in Edinburgh &#124; Origins</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Origins The Execution of Agnes Sampson for Witchcraft in Edinburgh &#124; Origins</span></span></span>

Within East Lothian’s folklore map, North Berwick sits alongside other local supernatural places such as Yester’s [Goblin Ha]({{ 'goblin-ha/' | relative_url }})’, Spott’s witch memories, saintly coastal traditions and haunted or enchanted ruins. What makes North Berwick different is the density of documentation and harm. It is folklore formed under pressure: not a fireside tale freely embroidered over generations, but a story produced by interrogation, fear, royal self-protection and print. That is why it remains one of the county’s most compelling and uncomfortable legends.

    });
    section.setAttribute('data-ebay-selected-market', marketId);
    if (persist) {
      try { if (window.localStorage) localStorage.setItem('phoenix-ebay-market', marketId); } catch (err) {}
    }
    closeMenu(section);
  }
  sections.forEach(function (section) {
    if (section.getAttribute('data-ebay-localized-init') === '1') return;
    section.setAttribute('data-ebay-localized-init', '1');
    var available = availableMarkets(section);
    var marketId = defaultMarket;
    if (window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation && window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation.inferMarket) {
      marketId = window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation.inferMarket(available, {
        storageKey: 'phoenix-ebay-market',
        defaultMarket: defaultMarket
      });
    } else if (available.indexOf(defaultMarket)< 0) {
      marketId = available[0] || defaultMarket;
    }
    var select = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-select]');
    if (select) {
      select.addEventListener('change', function () {
        applyMarket(section, select.value, true);
      });
    }
    var trigger = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-trigger]');
    var menu = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-menu]');
    if (trigger && menu) {
      trigger.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
        event.stopPropagation();
        var expanded = trigger.getAttribute('aria-expanded') === 'true';
        menu.hidden = expanded;
        trigger.setAttribute('aria-expanded', expanded ? 'false' : 'true');
        var picker = trigger.closest('.fr-ebay-market-picker');
        if (picker) picker.classList.toggle('fr-ebay-market-picker--open', !expanded);
      });
      Array.prototype.slice.call(menu.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-option]')).forEach(function (button) {
        button.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
          event.stopPropagation();
          applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-option'), true);
        });
      });
      document.addEventListener('click', function () { closeMenu(section); });
    }
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      button.addEventListener('click', function () {
        applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button'), true);
      });
    });
    applyMarket(section, marketId, false);
  });
})();
