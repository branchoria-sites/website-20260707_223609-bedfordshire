## Introduction

[Nottinghamshire]({{ 'where-nottinghamshire-turns-history-into/' | relative_url }})’s darker folklore is not a single grand legend like [Robin Hood]({{ 'robin-hood/' | relative_url }}). It is a cluster of smaller, sharper traditions: a phantom black dog on Crow Lane near South Muskham and Bathley, witchcraft fears preserved in court and church records, protective “witch marks” at Creswell Crags, and haunted-house stories attached to places such as Wollaton Hall, Annesley Hall and Rufford Abbey. These tales matter because they show a different Nottinghamshire from the heroic greenwood: a county of lonely lanes, anxious neighbours, dangerous reputations, old houses and unexplained misfortune.



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/Nottinghamshire_a5a895_dark_folklore_witche_e8167c-overview.webp" | relative_url }}" alt="Overview image for Dark Tales" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

The evidence is uneven, and that is part of the story. The Crow Lane black dog is unusually specific but seems to survive from a single recorded family memory. Witchcraft traditions are better documented, though they often reveal fear, accusation and social pressure more than “witches” in any simple storybook sense. The haunting traditions are often modern retellings layered onto real historic buildings. Read together, they show how Nottinghamshire people have used supernatural stories to make sense of night travel, illness, death, inheritance, local status and the strange atmosphere of old places.

## Crow Lane and Nottinghamshire’s lone black dog

The county’s best-known black dog tradition belongs to Crow Lane, the road running from South Muskham towards Bathley, north of Newark. A folklore note preserved from a 1952 manuscript in Nottingham County Library records the recollections of Mrs Smalley, then about 75. Her grandfather, born in 1804 and died in 1888, was said to have seen a black dog trotting beside his pony and trap when travelling between Southwell and Bathley. The same apparition was later reported by her son Sidney, who rode home from Newark dances on a motorcycle around 1915 and repeatedly saw the dog on Crow Lane. On one occasion, Sidney took his father with him specially, and both were said to have seen it.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.indigogroup.co.uk/edge/bdogfl.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: indigogroup.co.uk">[indigogroup.co.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">indigogroup.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">At the Edge archive: Black dogs folklore</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">At the Edge archive: Black dogs folklore</span></span></span>

That detail makes the story feel unusually local. It is not just “a black dog somewhere in Nottinghamshire”, but a creature tied to a precise lane, a pattern of night travel and a family line of witnesses stretching from the nineteenth century into the motor age. The dog does not attack, speak, vanish in flames or foretell a clearly recorded death. It simply trots alongside the traveller and cannot be run down. That restraint is one reason the story feels closer to oral memory than to Gothic invention.

It also stands out because Nottinghamshire appears to have fewer black dog traditions than some neighbouring counties. The same black-dog survey notes only one Nottinghamshire example, then contrasts it with Lincolnshire, where Ethel Rudkin and later collectors recorded many more black dog localities.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.indigogroup.co.uk/edge/bdogfl.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: indigogroup.co.uk">[indigogroup.co.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">indigogroup.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">At the Edge archive: Black dogs folklore</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">At the Edge archive: Black dogs folklore</span></span></span> For readers mapping county folklore, this is important: Nottinghamshire’s black dog is not a dense regional cycle like East Anglia’s Black Shuck or Lincolnshire’s Hairy Jack. It is a narrow, road-bound survival.

Crow Lane also fits the broader pattern of British black dog folklore. Across England, phantom dogs often belong to liminal places: lanes, bridges, churchyards, riverbanks, moorland paths and the edges of settlements. They appear at night or dusk, when ordinary travel becomes uncertain. Some are death omens; others act more like unsettling companions or guardians. Nottinghamshire’s Crow Lane dog is best read in that second, more ambiguous category: frightening because it should not be there, but not obviously malicious.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.icysedgwick.com/black-dogs/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: icysedgwick.com">[Icy Sedgwick]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">icysedgwick.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Icy Sedgwick Black Dogs and Englishmen: Black Shuck in English Folklore</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Icy Sedgwick Black Dogs and Englishmen: Black Shuck in English Folklore</span></span></span>

The landscape matters. South Muskham and Bathley sit in the Trent-side countryside, where lanes, drains, low fields and night journeys would have made a strong setting for uncanny repetition. A later local retelling by John Knifton notes the watery character of the South Muskham area and repeats the key detail that Sidney sometimes tried to run the dog over but never could.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://johnknifton.com/2014/10/28/halloween-nights-3-black-shuck-in-notts/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: johnknifton.com">[John Knifton]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">johnknifton.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">John Knifton Hallowe&#x27;en Nights (3) Black Shuck in Notts &#124; John Knifton</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">John Knifton Hallowe&#x27;en Nights (3) Black Shuck in Notts &#124; John Knifton</span></span></span> That does not prove the apparition, but it does show how the tale continues to be interpreted through local topography: a dark lane, a repeated route, a figure that keeps pace.

## Witches, accusations and cunning folk

Nottinghamshire’s witchcraft traditions are not best understood as tales of broomsticks and covens. They are more revealing as stories of blame, healing, suspicion and authority. In early modern England, accusations of witchcraft often emerged when illness, death, bad harvests, livestock losses or strange behaviour needed an explanation. Parliament’s history of the Witchcraft Acts notes that the 1542 Act made witchcraft a capital crime, that witchcraft legislation returned in 1562, and that the 1562 and 1604 Acts moved the trial of alleged witches from church courts to ordinary courts.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.parliament.uk/about/living-heritage/transformingsociety/private-lives/religion/overview/witchcraft/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: parliament.uk">[Parliament UK News]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">parliament.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on parliament.uk.</span></span></span>

Within Nottinghamshire and the East Midlands, the line between feared witch and useful magical practitioner could be blurry. A University of Nottingham Manuscripts and Special Collections piece notes that, in Lincolnshire and Nottinghamshire, “cunning men and women” were often consulted for healing, folk-medicine cures and sometimes divination. It also gives a concrete Nottinghamshire example from Mattersey in 1622: Anne Cook was presented in the Retford deanery church courts for claiming to speak of people’s deaths and foretell when they would die.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://sauonremweblog.blob.core.windows.net/%24web/The_Witches_of_East_Mids_-_Manuscripts_and_Special.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sauonremweblog.blob.core.windows.net">[sauonremweblog.blob.core.windows.net]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sauonremweblog.blob.core.windows.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Witches of East Mids</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Witches of East Mids</span></span></span>

That case is small but valuable. It shows witchcraft belief operating at parish level, not only in famous trials. Anne Cook’s alleged offence was not murder by magic. It was soothsaying: saying too much, or claiming to know too much, about neighbours’ fates. The same source explains that soothsaying and “superstitious practices” came under ecclesiastical jurisdiction, and that where no harm had been caused, church courts were more likely to demand repentance and cessation than execution.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://sauonremweblog.blob.core.windows.net/%24web/The_Witches_of_East_Mids_-_Manuscripts_and_Special.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sauonremweblog.blob.core.windows.net">[sauonremweblog.blob.core.windows.net]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sauonremweblog.blob.core.windows.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Witches of East Mids</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Witches of East Mids</span></span></span>

Cunning folk complicate the stereotype further. In British tradition, they were usually practical magical specialists: healers, charmers, diviners, finders of stolen goods and people who claimed to counteract witchcraft. Historic England’s discussion of protective marks and the scholarship of Owen Davies on magical healing both help explain this world: folk magic was not merely “belief” in the abstract, but a practical service market for people facing illness, theft, fear or misfortune. Davies describes cunning folk as an important part of a wider unorthodox medical marketplace that continued even after official attitudes changed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://uhra.herts.ac.uk/id/eprint/3509/1/906672.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: uhra.herts.ac.uk">[Hertfordshire Research Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">uhra.herts.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on herts.ac.uk.</span></span></span>

The risk was that the same reputation that made someone useful could also make them vulnerable. A healer might be welcomed when a charm seemed to work, then suspected when livestock died, a child sickened, or neighbours quarrelled. That is why Nottinghamshire’s witchcraft folklore should be read less as a catalogue of villains and more as a record of social pressure: who was trusted, who was feared, who had knowledge, and who could be blamed when ordinary explanations failed.

## John Darrell and the Nottingham possession panic

One of Nottinghamshire’s most dramatic witchcraft-related episodes centres not on an accused witch, but on an exorcist. John Darrell, born in or near Mansfield, became known in the 1590s for Puritan exorcisms. The most important Nottingham episode involved William Somers, a young apprentice in Nottingham who suffered fits that Darrell and his supporters interpreted as demonic possession.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Darrell" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">John Darrell</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">John Darrell</span></span></span>

The case caused fierce public controversy. A modern account in LeftLion summarises Darrell’s Nottingham preaching at St Mary’s in the Lace Market, his claims about the Devil’s activity, and his exorcism of Somers. It also notes that Somers later admitted to acting under Darrell’s instructions, prompting an investigation by church authorities.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://leftlion.co.uk/features/2020/10/witchcraft-in-nottingham-history/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: leftlion.co.uk">[LeftLion]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">leftlion.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Left Lion Out of Time: A History of Witchcraft in Nottingham</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Left Lion Out of Time: A History of Witchcraft in Nottingham</span></span></span> A scholarly preview of *The English Exorcist* describes how Darrell and his colleague George More were tried by the High Commission on fraud charges after the Nottingham case, with the authorities judging that the dispossessions were fraudulent and politically useful to Puritanism.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://api.pageplace.de/preview/DT0400.9781000096781_A39490110/preview-9781000096781_A39490110.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: api.pageplace.de">[PagePlace]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">api.pageplace.de</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Page Place The English Exorcist; John Darrell and the Shaping of Early</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Page Place The English Exorcist; John Darrell and the Shaping of Early</span></span></span>

This matters for Nottinghamshire folklore because possession stories sit at the crossing point between religion, medicine, performance and local rumour. Somers’ fits could be interpreted in several ways: illness, fraud, spiritual affliction, social drama, or a mixture of these. In 1590s Nottingham, that uncertainty became public theatre. People did not merely hear about supernatural danger; they watched it being argued over in churches, print and official investigation.

Darrell’s fall also had a literary afterlife. Samuel Harsnett’s attack on exorcism, published after these controversies, influenced the language of devils in Shakespeare’s *King Lear*, according to accounts of the Darrell case.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Darrell" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">John Darrell</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">John Darrell</span></span></span> Nottingham therefore contributed not only to local supernatural history, but to a wider English debate over whether apparent possession was divine proof, demonic assault, bodily illness or manipulation.

## Creswell Crags and the fear of what might come out of the dark

The most physically striking evidence for Nottinghamshire’s protective magic lies at Creswell Crags, on the Nottinghamshire-Derbyshire border. In 2019, the site announced the discovery of hundreds of protective marks, often called witch marks or apotropaic marks, scratched into the walls and ceilings of the caves. The marks were found over dark holes and crevices and had previously been dismissed as old graffiti. Creswell Crags described the discovery as potentially the largest collection of apotropaic marks in one place in the UK.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.creswell-crags.org.uk/news-archive/largest-discovery-of-witch-marks-in-britain-at-creswell-crags" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: creswell-crags.org.uk">[Creswell Crags]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">creswell-crags.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Creswell Crags Largest discovery of Witch Marks in Britain at Creswell Crags</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Creswell Crags Largest discovery of Witch Marks in Britain at Creswell Crags</span></span></span>

Historic England explains that such marks were usually carved near vulnerable points: doorways, windows, fireplaces and other openings. Their purpose was to avert witches, evil spirits or other harmful forces. The term “apotropaic” means protective or evil-averting, and the marks appear in houses, churches, barns and caves.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://historicengland.org.uk/whats-new/features/discovering-witches-marks/what-are-witches-marks/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: historicengland.org.uk">[Historic England]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">historicengland.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on historicengland.org.uk.</span></span></span>

At Creswell, the setting changes the emotional force of the practice. These were not just marks beside a domestic hearth. They were placed in caves, near cracks, holes and dark spaces that could be imagined as thresholds. Creswell Crags reported that the designs included double V marks associated with the Virgin Mary, PM marks, diagonal lines, boxes and maze-like devices thought to trap evil. The site suggested that repeated marking over time may indicate renewed anxiety during periods of sickness, death or poor crops.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.creswell-crags.org.uk/news-archive/largest-discovery-of-witch-marks-in-britain-at-creswell-crags" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: creswell-crags.org.uk">[Creswell Crags]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">creswell-crags.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Creswell Crags Largest discovery of Witch Marks in Britain at Creswell Crags</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Creswell Crags Largest discovery of Witch Marks in Britain at Creswell Crags</span></span></span>

The discovery is especially important for this page because it turns witchcraft fear from story into visible landscape. A black dog tale can fade into memory; an exorcism controversy survives in books and court records; but the Creswell marks are still scratched into stone. They show people actively defending a place against what they feared might emerge from darkness. Historic England’s chief executive, quoted by Creswell Crags, framed the discovery as a reminder that even two hundred years ago the countryside was a place where death, disease and evil forces could be imagined as everyday presences.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.creswell-crags.org.uk/news-archive/largest-discovery-of-witch-marks-in-britain-at-creswell-crags" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: creswell-crags.org.uk">[Creswell Crags]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">creswell-crags.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Creswell Crags Largest discovery of Witch Marks in Britain at Creswell Crags</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Creswell Crags Largest discovery of Witch Marks in Britain at Creswell Crags</span></span></span>

## Haunted houses and modern retellings

Nottinghamshire’s haunted-house traditions are often attached to real historic buildings, which gives them a different texture from the Crow Lane black dog or early modern witchcraft records. These stories usually blend architectural age, family memory, tragedy, tourism and modern ghost-hunting culture.

The Nottinghamshire Historic Environment Record notes that Annesley Hall, Wollaton Hall and Rufford Abbey are often quoted as places with roaming spirits. It also makes a useful point about modern ghost culture: the Victorian era helped popularise horror stories, haunted houses and Gothic literature, while modern media, tours and social platforms have spread ghost stories further.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://hbsmrweb-nottinghamshire.esdm.co.uk/whats-new/a-haunting-history" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: hbsmrweb-nottinghamshire.esdm.co.uk">[hbsmrweb-nottinghamshire.esdm.co.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">hbsmrweb-nottinghamshire.esdm.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">A Haunting History</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">A Haunting History</span></span></span>

Wollaton Hall’s “White Lady” is the clearest example. The HER account says the spirit is also known as Lady Jane Middleton. The story claims she was paralysed by accident, confined to her room until death, and then haunted the hallways; visitors and staff have reported lights or sensed her presence. The same account is careful, however, to separate story from history. Wollaton Hall was owned by the Willoughby family from its construction in the 1580s until 1925, and Henry Willoughby, 6th Baron Middleton, did marry Jane Lawley, who died around 1852, but the ghost story is likely embellished.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://hbsmrweb-nottinghamshire.esdm.co.uk/whats-new/a-haunting-history" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: hbsmrweb-nottinghamshire.esdm.co.uk">[hbsmrweb-nottinghamshire.esdm.co.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">hbsmrweb-nottinghamshire.esdm.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">A Haunting History</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">A Haunting History</span></span></span>

That careful wording is exactly how Nottinghamshire hauntings should be handled. The question is not whether the White Lady can be proven. The more useful question is why such a story attaches so well to Wollaton Hall. The building is already theatrical: an Elizabethan prodigy house on a hill, associated with aristocratic family history, later adapted as a museum and visitor site. Its official history describes it as built in the 1580s and as “the architectural sensation of its age”.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://wollatonhall.org.uk/hall-and-museum/history/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wollatonhall.org.uk">[Wollaton Hall]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wollatonhall.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wollatonhall.org.uk.</span></span></span> A tragic female ghost gives that grand public building a private emotional centre: a room, a light, a confined life, a repeated presence.

Rufford Abbey works differently. Its heritage record identifies it as a former Cistercian monastery founded in the medieval period, with surviving abbey remains, later domestic adaptation and post-medieval building phases.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://her.nottinghamshire.gov.uk/Monument/MNT15069" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: her.nottinghamshire.gov.uk">[her.nottinghamshire.gov.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">her.nottinghamshire.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nottinghamshire.gov.uk.</span></span></span> A ruined abbey naturally attracts haunting traditions because it already carries visible rupture: religious suppression, altered buildings, empty ranges, vaults and fragments of monastic space. The ghost story does not need to be ancient to feel plausible to visitors; the architecture itself invites a haunted reading.

Annesley Hall, too, is a building where layered history makes room for legend. The HER records medieval origins, later sixteenth- and seventeenth-century fabric, eighteenth- and nineteenth-century additions, and a long documentary trail through antiquarian and architectural sources.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://her.nottinghamshire.gov.uk/Monument/MNT15674" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: her.nottinghamshire.gov.uk">[her.nottinghamshire.gov.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">her.nottinghamshire.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nottinghamshire.gov.uk.</span></span></span> Where a house has stood, altered, decayed and been retold over centuries, haunting becomes one way of making continuity feel personal.

## What these darker traditions reveal about Nottinghamshire

Nottinghamshire’s darker folklore is most powerful when treated as social memory rather than as a list of scares. The Crow Lane dog preserves the anxiety of repeated night journeys. Witchcraft accusations preserve the danger of reputation in close communities. Cunning folk preserve the hope that someone local might heal, find, bless or protect when official medicine or law could not help. Creswell Crags preserves fear in stone, turning unseen danger into carved lines of defence. Haunted houses preserve emotional stories around old buildings whose documented histories can otherwise feel remote.

These traditions also correct a common misunderstanding of county folklore. Nottinghamshire is not only Sherwood Forest and Robin Hood. Its supernatural map includes low lanes by the Trent, parish courts in Retford deanery, caves on the county edge, Lace Market pulpits, great houses, abbey ruins and modern heritage sites. The darker tales do not replace the outlaw legend, but they widen the county’s imaginative geography.

They also show why evidence needs careful handling. Some traditions are well grounded in records, such as Anne Cook’s 1622 church-court presentment or the Darrell-Somers controversy. Others are oral survivals, like the Crow Lane dog. Others are modern visitor traditions attached to historic buildings. Treating all of them as the same kind of evidence would flatten the subject. Their differences are the point.

For a reader exploring Nottinghamshire folklore today, the best way to understand these stories is to ask what each one is doing. Is it warning travellers? Explaining illness? Marking a dangerous threshold? Turning a house into a memory palace? Selling a ghost tour? Preserving a family anecdote? Nottinghamshire’s darker folklore survives because it keeps answering those questions in places people can still name, visit and recognise.
