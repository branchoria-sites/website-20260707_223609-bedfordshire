## Introduction

For the folklore reader, the important point is that Radnorshire is not a generic “Welsh myths” page. Its strongest traditions are rooted in a particular landscape: the Radnor Forest dome, the St Michael churches said to hold down the last dragon in Wales, the [Four Stones]({{ 'four-stones/' | relative_url }}) of Old Radnor, magical protection charms at Cascob, uncanny spirit activity at Llangunllo, and farming customs preserved in local collections and later folklore scholarship. Roy Palmer’s *The Folklore of Radnorshire* remains the obvious modern county collection, published as a 272-page volume and reprinted in 2007.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.worldofbooks.com/en-gb/products/folklore-of-radnorshire-book-roy-palmer-9781873827178" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: worldofbooks.com">[World of Books]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">worldofbooks.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">World of Books The Folklore of Radnorshire By Roy Palmer</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">World of Books The Folklore of Radnorshire By Roy Palmer</span></span></span>

## Where Radnorshire folklore sits on the map

Radnorshire occupies a border position that matters enormously for its traditions. It meets Montgomeryshire and Shropshire to the north, Herefordshire to the east, Brecknockshire to the south and Cardiganshire to the west, with its identity organised around upland watersheds, the Wye valley and the hill country of Radnor Forest.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://realcounties.com/county/radnorshire/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: realcounties.com">[Historic Counties Institute]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">realcounties.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Historic Counties Institute Radnorshire</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Historic Counties Institute Radnorshire</span></span></span> That makes it a county of crossings: Welsh and English speech communities, old drovers’ routes, Marcher castles, remote chapels, sheep farms, market towns and parishes whose stories do not always respect modern council lines.

Radnorshire’s historic geography also helps explain why some of its folklore feels both Welsh and border-English. Wikishire notes that the county roughly corresponds to the older territory between the Wye and Severn and came under Marcher-lord control after the eleventh century; the same account highlights Radnorshire’s “historical backwater” reputation while also pointing to major memory-sites such as Cwmhir Abbey and the Battle of Bryn Glas during Owain Glyndŵr’s rising.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://wikishire.co.uk/wiki/Radnorshire" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wikishire.co.uk">[Wikishire]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wikishire.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Wikishire Radnorshire</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wikishire Radnorshire</span></span></span> In folklore terms, that mixture matters: a story may be told in English, collected by an antiquarian, attached to a Welsh saint’s church, and located beside a Norman earthwork or prehistoric stone.

For this UK historic-counties project, Radnorshire should therefore be treated as the historic county, not simply as a corner of present-day Powys. The modern visitor may arrive through Powys tourism, Natural Resources Wales forestry information or the Brecon and Radnor press, but the older story-map is Radnorshire: Radnor Forest, Old Radnor, New Radnor, Cascob, Llangunllo, Abbeycwmhir, Pilleth and the Wye-side routes around Rhayader.

## The last dragon under Radnor Forest

The most distinctive Radnorshire legend today is the sleeping dragon of Radnor Forest. In the common modern telling, Radnor Forest is the home of the last dragon in Wales, held asleep by a ring of churches dedicated to St Michael, the archangel traditionally shown defeating the dragon. Mid Wales My Way gives the usual four-church version: Llanfihangel Rhydithon, Llanfihangel Nant Melan, Llanfihangel Cefnllys and Llanfihangel Cascob form the ring, and if any of the churches were destroyed the dragon would wake.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.midwalesmyway.com/thedragonofradnorforest" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: midwalesmyway.com">[Mid Wales My Way]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">midwalesmyway.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on midwalesmyway.com.</span></span></span>

This is a good example of how a local legend works through landscape rather than plot. The story does not need a long heroic narrative. Its power lies in looking at the forest’s high ground, church dedications and encircling settlements as if they were the visible locks on a buried force. Friends of Radnor Forest gives a slightly different version, saying there are in fact five St Michael churches around the Radnor Forest, and links the legend to circular churchyards, ancient yews and older mounds.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.radnorfforest.co.uk/news-views/legands" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: radnorfforest.co.uk">[Friends of Radnor Forest]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">radnorfforest.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on radnorfforest.co.uk.</span></span></span> That difference is worth preserving rather than flattening: folklore often survives in variants, and the uncertainty over four or five churches is part of the way the tale has been retold locally.

The forest itself needs a small correction for first-time readers. “Forest” here originally means a medieval hunting area, not necessarily dense woodland. Mid Wales My Way explicitly explains that Radnor Forest took its name from the older hunting sense of the word and was once a royal hunting ground, even though much of the wooded area is now managed and visited as modern forest landscape.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.midwalesmyway.com/thedragonofradnorforest" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: midwalesmyway.com">[Mid Wales My Way]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">midwalesmyway.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on midwalesmyway.com.</span></span></span> That older sense matters because the dragon legend belongs to a high, open, hunted, watched landscape as much as to trees.

The Radnor dragon also connects naturally with other Welsh dragon traditions, but it should not be reduced to the national red dragon story. The Radnorshire version is local and topographic: churches, ridges, old roads, mounds and the threat of what might happen if the pattern is broken. The legend is especially well suited to modern walking, heritage trails and local storytelling because it gives visitors a way to read the county’s churches and hills as one connected mythic machine.

## Four Stones, Hindwell Pool and the moving-stone tradition

If the dragon legend is Radnorshire’s great hill story, the Four Stones near Old Radnor are its most memorable prehistoric folklore site. The Historic Environment Record describes the Four Stones as a scheduled Bronze Age stone setting in the community of Old Radnor: four stones forming a rectangular setting about 4 metres by 3 metres, with stones roughly 1.2 to 1.9 metres high and possible cup marks on the south-west stone.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archwilio.org.uk/arch/query/page.php?dbname=cpat&amp;tbname=core&amp;watprn=CPAT1072" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archwilio.org.uk">[Archwilio]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archwilio.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on archwilio.org.uk.</span></span></span>

The folklore is richer than the archaeology alone. The same record notes a local belief that the font in nearby Old Radnor Church may once have filled a gap in the stone setting, turning a church object into a possible displaced prehistoric stone in popular imagination.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archwilio.org.uk/arch/query/page.php?dbname=cpat&amp;tbname=core&amp;watprn=CPAT1072" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archwilio.org.uk">[Archwilio]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archwilio.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on archwilio.org.uk.</span></span></span> Other collected or repeated traditions say the stones mark the graves of four kings killed in battle, or that the stones go down to Hindwell Pool at night to drink. A modern antiquarian page preserves the “four kings” version from Ella M. Leather’s 1913 *Folklore* notes, while also recording the midnight-drinking tradition and the older habit of leaving the hay unmown around the stones.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.themodernantiquarian.com/site/106/four-stones" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: themodernantiquarian.com">[The Modern Antiquarian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">themodernantiquarian.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on themodernantiquarian.com.</span></span></span>

The Hindwell Pool story is particularly valuable because archaeology and folklore illuminate each other without one cancelling the other. A Clwyd-Powys Archaeological Trust panel explains that the Walton basin contains streams and springs which may have had religious significance; water draining from Radnor Forest disappears into gravel and re-emerges near Hindwell Pool, and the panel suggests this natural behaviour is probably the origin of the tale that the Four Stones go to the pool at night to drink.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://heneb.org.uk/archive/cpat/wp-content/uploads/2018/12/How-the-West-was-Won-Panel-5.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: heneb.org.uk">[Heneb]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">heneb.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on heneb.org.uk.</span></span></span>

That is exactly the sort of Radnorshire folklore that deserves careful handling. The point is not that stones literally walk. The point is that people noticed a strange water landscape, a prehistoric monument, a pool, church bells and old fields, and made a story that joined them. Similar “moving stones” and “stones that drink” traditions occur elsewhere in Britain, so the Four Stones can be internally linked to wider standing-stone folklore, but the Hindwell setting gives the Radnorshire version its local character.

## Witchcraft, charms and practical magic

Radnorshire’s magical tradition is not only about spectacular beings. It also includes practical protection: charms, written formulae, fear of witchcraft, and the everyday problem of what people did when they believed misfortune had been sent against them. The most striking local example is the [Cascob Charm]({{ 'cascob-charm/' | relative_url }}) at Llanfihangel Cascob, one of the St Michael churches linked with the [dragon ring]({{ 'dragon-ring/' | relative_url }}).

Friends of Radnor Forest describes the Cascob Charm as an “ABRACADABRA” charm made from a mixture of Christian prayer and occult formula, said to protect an Elizabeth Lloyd from witchcraft; the charm is now framed inside Llanfihangel Cascob after reportedly being found in the churchyard long after it was made.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.radnorfforest.co.uk/news-views/legands" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: radnorfforest.co.uk">[Friends of Radnor Forest]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">radnorfforest.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on radnorfforest.co.uk.</span></span></span> A 2024 Aberystwyth University article on written charms in mid Wales gives the broader context: paper charms were used to ward off evil and bad luck, often combining Christian language, Latin, astrological symbols and requests for protection from “witchcraft, evil men and women, and hardness of heart.”<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.aber.ac.uk/en/news/archive/2024/08/title-274668-en.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: aber.ac.uk">[Aberystwyth University]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">aber.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Aberystwyth University August: News, Aberystwyth University</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Aberystwyth University August: News, Aberystwyth University</span></span></span>

This matters because it prevents a modern misunderstanding. “Witchcraft” in Radnorshire folklore was not simply a Halloween category or a theatrical villain. It could be a social explanation for illness, animal disease, bad luck, sour relationships or unexplained failure. The same Aberystwyth account explains that people who experienced misfortune might consult a “cunning man” or local wise practitioner, who could diagnose witchcraft or ill-wishing and provide a written charm to be hidden, folded, bottled or kept unread.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.aber.ac.uk/en/news/archive/2024/08/title-274668-en.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: aber.ac.uk">[Aberystwyth University]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">aber.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Aberystwyth University August: News, Aberystwyth University</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Aberystwyth University August: News, Aberystwyth University</span></span></span>

Cascob therefore sits at the meeting point of church, magic and anxiety. The charm’s Christian invocations do not make it less magical; they show how protection magic in Wales could use religious language as part of its power. For Radnorshire, a county of remote parishes and border churches, that mixture is one of the most revealing forms of folklore: not a separate “pagan survival” neatly opposed to Christianity, but a lived practice in which prayer, fear, writing and local authority overlapped.

## Ghosts, spirits and uncanny houses

Radnorshire’s supernatural tradition includes domestic hauntings and spirit disturbances, not just outdoor legends. One of the clearest older printed examples appears in Wirt Sikes’s nineteenth-century *British Goblins*, which records a troublesome spirit on the farm of Edward Roberts in the parish of Llangunllo. In the story, a servant’s flail is snatched during threshing, women’s knitting and yarn are thrown about, dishes move and break, stones and iron are cast at people, and the disturbance ends with the house burning down.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/files/34704/34704-h/34704-h.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Project Gutenberg eBook of British Goblins: Welsh Folk-lore, Fairy Mythology, Legends and Traditions, by Wirt Sikes...</span></span></span>

Sikes’s account should be read as a collected supernatural narrative, not as neutral evidence that such events happened. Its value lies in what it shows about the kind of story Radnorshire could preserve: a workaday farm, a barn, threshing, women’s domestic labour, neighbours coming to see, and a person from Knighton attempting prayer or exorcism.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/files/34704/34704-h/34704-h.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Project Gutenberg eBook of British Goblins: Welsh Folk-lore, Fairy Mythology, Legends and Traditions, by Wirt Sikes...</span></span></span> The haunting is frightening, but it is also social. People gather, test the story, repeat it, and connect it to market routes and local memory.

This is a different texture from the grand dragon legend. The Llangunllo spirit belongs to the folklore of houses, barns, labour and reputation. Such tales often survive because they explain why a place was avoided, why ruins had a story, or why a family or farm became memorable. In a county of scattered farms and small settlements, that sort of localised supernatural narrative may have mattered as much as better-known national myth.

## Death omens, hounds and the Welsh otherworld

Radnorshire also belongs to the wider Welsh and border tradition of death omens, spectral hounds and uncanny sounds in the air. Sikes’s *British Goblins* gives a broad account of the hounds of the otherworld as death-portents: a pack heard howling through the air, sometimes sounding less loud when near and louder when far away, and linked in learned interpretation to the hounds of Arawn in the story of Pwyll.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/files/34704/34704-h/34704-h.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Project Gutenberg eBook of British Goblins: Welsh Folk-lore, Fairy Mythology, Legends and Traditions, by Wirt Sikes...</span></span></span>

For Radnorshire specifically, later folklore summaries and retellings often place terrible hounds in the Radnor Forest mountains and ravines around the road to Llanfihangel Nant Melan. That detail fits the county’s geography: steep roads, hollow valleys, night travel, hunting landscapes and the old fear of hearing something before seeing anything. The Welsh hound tradition should not be confused too quickly with the East Anglian “Black Shuck” type of black dog legend. In Welsh material, the otherworldly pack is often heard in the sky or wild country and may be bound up with death, pursuit and the otherworld rather than a single named dog haunting one lane.

There is also a practical interpretive point here. A modern reader may ask whether such stories began with real animal calls, wind, hunting dogs, owls or fear on lonely roads. Sikes himself notes comparative explanations involving wind, wild huntsmen and natural sounds, while still presenting the Welsh belief as a living death-omen tradition in his sources.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/files/34704/34704-h/34704-h.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Project Gutenberg eBook of British Goblins: Welsh Folk-lore, Fairy Mythology, Legends and Traditions, by Wirt Sikes...</span></span></span> The most grounded answer is that the folklore is not weakened by possible natural triggers. The tradition tells us how people interpreted frightening sound in a dark landscape, especially where death, funerals and night journeys were already part of the mental map.

## Customs, harvest and the farming year

Radnorshire folklore is not only supernatural. Like many rural counties, it also includes customs tied to labour, harvest, seasonal visiting, churchgoing and communal entertainment. A study of Welsh harvest customs cites Palmer’s *The Folklore of Radnorshire* for Radnorshire material on harvest practice, placing the county inside the wider Welsh pattern of harvest suppers, shared labour, food, drink and changing nineteenth-century rural relations.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.academia.edu/34635964/Cynhaeaf_Customs_Practices_and_Folklore_associated_with_the_Traditional_Harvest_in_Wales" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: academia.edu">[Academia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">academia.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on academia.edu.</span></span></span>

That matters because folklore is sometimes wrongly reduced to fairies, ghosts and monsters. In a farming county such as Radnorshire, beliefs and customs around harvest, luck, animals, weather, tools and neighbourly obligation were part of the same traditional culture. Harvest customs were not just quaint celebrations; they helped organise work, reward, status and seasonal release. The same harvest study notes that by the nineteenth century drink, unruly behaviour, temperance pressure, mechanisation and changing labour relations all affected the older harvest supper tradition.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.academia.edu/34635964/Cynhaeaf_Customs_Practices_and_Folklore_associated_with_the_Traditional_Harvest_in_Wales" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: academia.edu">[Academia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">academia.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on academia.edu.</span></span></span>

Radnorshire’s seasonal culture should therefore be read alongside its social history. A charm for a sick cow, a harvest supper, a churchyard gathering, a stone avoided after dark and a dragon held down by churches all belong to communities trying to manage uncertainty: weather, illness, poverty, work, death and the power of place.

## Saints, churches and older ground

Radnorshire folklore often gathers around churches because churches were not only religious buildings. They were burial places, landmarks, social centres, bells in the soundscape, and sometimes the visible Christian layer on older-feeling ground. The St Michael churches around Radnor Forest are the clearest example: in the dragon legend their dedications become protective stations around the sleeping beast.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.midwalesmyway.com/thedragonofradnorforest" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: midwalesmyway.com">[Mid Wales My Way]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">midwalesmyway.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on midwalesmyway.com.</span></span></span>

Cascob sharpens the point. In one local account, the church is associated both with the dragon ring and with the Cascob Charm; Friends of Radnor Forest also says the surrounding St Michael churches appear to stand on pre-existing mounds with circular yards and old yew trees.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.radnorfforest.co.uk/news-views/legands" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: radnorfforest.co.uk">[Friends of Radnor Forest]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">radnorfforest.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on radnorfforest.co.uk.</span></span></span> Such claims need care, because “church on an ancient site” can be overused in popular folklore writing. But as folklore, the pattern is important: people read circular yards, yews, mounds and dedications as signs that a place has older power.

Old Radnor offers another church-and-stone example. The Historic Environment Record for the Four Stones notes the local suggestion that a nearby Old Radnor church font may have originated as a missing stone from the prehistoric setting.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archwilio.org.uk/arch/query/page.php?dbname=cpat&amp;tbname=core&amp;watprn=CPAT1072" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archwilio.org.uk">[Archwilio]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archwilio.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on archwilio.org.uk.</span></span></span> Whether or not that is archaeologically secure, it is a classic piece of folk interpretation: a Christian object is imagined as carrying a deeper prehistoric past into the church.

## What is old, what is collected, and what is modern retelling?

A good Radnorshire folklore page needs to distinguish levels of evidence. Some traditions are old in print but still mediated by collectors; some are modern heritage retellings; some are archaeological facts with folklore attached; and some are living local interpretations repeated through walking routes, tourism pages and community writing.

The Four Stones are archaeologically recorded as a scheduled Bronze Age stone setting, while their “four kings”, church-font and drinking-at-Hindwell traditions are folklore attached to the monument.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archwilio.org.uk/arch/query/page.php?dbname=cpat&amp;tbname=core&amp;watprn=CPAT1072" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archwilio.org.uk">[Archwilio]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archwilio.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on archwilio.org.uk.</span></span></span> The Hindwell explanation from Clwyd-Powys Archaeological Trust gives a plausible landscape reason for the drinking-stones tale, connecting disappearing and re-emerging water with sacred-spring imagination.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://heneb.org.uk/archive/cpat/wp-content/uploads/2018/12/How-the-West-was-Won-Panel-5.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: heneb.org.uk">[Heneb]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">heneb.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on heneb.org.uk.</span></span></span>

The Llangunllo haunting is an older printed supernatural account in Sikes, valuable as a nineteenth-century collection but still shaped by the collector’s style and assumptions.<span well-attested mid-Wales practice of written protection charms, recently studied by historians of Welsh magic and belief.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" Radnorshire’s traditions show how folklore is preserved: in books, church objects, archaeological records, tourism trails, local websites, family memory, antiquarian notes and the stories people still tell when a road bends under a dark ridge.

## Why Radnorshire’s folklore matters

Radnorshire’s folklore matters because it gives a small historic county a strong imaginative identity. The county has no single huge city and is often absorbed administratively into Powys, but its stories are sharply local: a dragon under Radnor Forest, stones walking to Hindwell Pool, a charm at Cascob, a spirit in a Llangunllo barn, hounds in the hills, harvest customs on farms, and churches acting as both sacred buildings and mythic boundary markers.

The traditions also show how border counties work in folklore. Radnorshire is Welsh, Marcher and rural at once. Its stories cross into wider Welsh motifs such as otherworld hounds, written charms and dragon imagery, but they also sit beside English-border patterns of haunted lanes, cunning folk, churchyard antiquities and standing-stone legends. That makes it especially valuable for cross-linking with neighbouring Herefordshire, Shropshire, Montgomeryshire and Brecknockshire pages without losing the county’s own centre of gravity.

Above all, Radnorshire folklore is landscape memory. It asks readers to look again at what might otherwise seem like quiet country: a church dedication, a yew, a mound, a pool, a stone setting, a lonely road, a farm ruin, a harvest meal. In Radnorshire, the supernatural is rarely floating free. It is pinned to place.
