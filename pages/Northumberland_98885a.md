## Introduction

The most useful way to read Northumberland folklore is not as a list of monsters, but as a map of memory. Border warfare, medieval lordship, mining communities, pilgrimage, exposed moorland, dangerous night travel, and older prehistoric monuments all shaped the county’s supernatural imagination. Some traditions are strongly documented in ballads, archives, antiquarian collections, or local custom; others survive mainly as later retellings, tourism stories, or modern folklore pages. Both matter, but they do not carry the same evidential weight.

## What area does “Northumberland” cover here?

This page treats Northumberland as the historic county: the rugged northern English county running from the Tyne towards the Tweed, with the North Sea coast to the east and the Cheviot uplands against the Scottish border. Wikishire’s historic-county description places Northumberland between the Tweed and the Tyne, bordered by Berwickshire, Roxburghshire, Cumberland, County Durham and the North Sea, and notes its long role as an Anglo-Scottish borderland before the Union of the Crowns changed its political position in 1603.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://wikishire.co.uk/wiki/Northumberland" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wikishire.co.uk">[Wikishire]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wikishire.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wikishire.co.uk.</span></span></span>

That historic frame matters because folklore rarely follows modern council boundaries. Newcastle upon Tyne, for example, is historically part of Northumberland, although modern administrative arrangements place it outside the present Northumberland unitary authority. Wikishire notes Newcastle on the north bank of the Tyne as the county’s largest town in the historic sense, while the modern county page distinguishes the historic county from later administrative changes.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://wikishire.co.uk/wiki/Northumberland" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wikishire.co.uk">[Wikishire]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wikishire.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wikishire.co.uk.</span></span></span>

Northumberland’s folklore also crosses edges. The Border ballads belong to a wider Anglo-Scottish frontier culture; rapper sword dance belongs to the Northumberland and Durham coalfield; black-dog lore overlaps with Yorkshire and County Durham; and saintly routes connect Lindisfarne, Heavenfield, Durham and the wider Northumbrian church. The centre of gravity, however, remains the historic county’s own landscapes: Bamburgh, Spindleston Heugh, Simonside, the Cheviots, Lindisfarne, Allendale, Duddo and the Northumberland coalfield.

## Why Northumberland’s folklore feels so landscape-bound

Northumberland has the kind of terrain that makes stories feel plausible even when the supernatural claim itself is not being treated as fact. The Simonside Hills rise above Rothbury; the Cheviots make a high, misty border zone; Bamburgh Castle dominates the coast; Lindisfarne is reached by a tidal causeway; and the county is scattered with castles, peel towers, Roman remains, prehistoric carvings and lonely upland paths. Wikishire describes Northumberland as a county of fells, dales, castles and peel towers, with Hadrian’s Wall crossing its south and Lindisfarne standing as an early Christian centre off the coast.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://wikishire.co.uk/wiki/Northumberland" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wikishire.co.uk">[Wikishire]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wikishire.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wikishire.co.uk.</span></span></span>

This gives Northumberland folklore a recurring pattern. Many stories warn against danger in the landscape: lights leading travellers into bogs, fairies dancing on hills, redcaps haunting ruins, black dogs appearing on roads and thresholds, and dragons occupying crags and troughs. The supernatural often explains a risky place or makes a memorable landmark more meaningful.

There is also a historical reason for the mood. Northumberland was long shaped by border conflict, raiding, lordship and divided loyalties. Ballads such as “Chevy Chase” and “The Battle of Otterburn” turn violent frontier memory into song, while later ghost and fairy traditions often settle around castles, battlefields and ruined places.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Ballad_of_Chevy_Chase" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Ballad of Chevy Chase</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Ballad of Chevy Chase</span></span></span>

## The Simonside Duergars: lights on the hill and a warning after dark

The Duergars of Simonside are probably Northumberland’s most distinctive fairy or dwarf tradition. The story places a race of dangerous beings in the Simonside Hills near Rothbury. They appear after dark, carrying lights that lure travellers away from safe ground and towards bogs or precipices. Northumberland National Park’s own account describes the Simonside Duergars as old-legend figures who prey on lost travellers at night by drawing them with a small light towards danger.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.northumberlandnationalpark.org.uk/spooky-tales-from-northumberland-national-park/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: northumberlandnationalpark.org.uk">[Northumberland National Park]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">northumberlandnationalpark.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Northumberland National Park Spooky tales from Northumberland National Park</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Northumberland National Park Spooky tales from Northumberland National Park</span></span></span>

The tale works because it belongs exactly where it is told. Simonside is a real walking landscape, beautiful but exposed, with rocky edges, mist and wet ground. A folklore warning about deceptive lights is also a practical warning about night travel. The Duergars resemble will-o’-the-wisp traditions elsewhere in Britain, but the Northumberland form has its own local vocabulary and setting.

The tradition is not just a modern spooky-tour invention. Later summaries point to nineteenth-century newspaper references and to twentieth-century retellings such as Frederick Grice’s *Folk Tales of the North Country*. The figure is also linked with dialect words for “dwarf” in the Anglo-Scottish border area, although modern explanations of the name vary and should be treated cautiously.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simonside_Dwarfs" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Simonside Dwarfs</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Simonside Dwarfs</span></span></span>

What makes the Duergars valuable for a county folklore page is that they bring together several Northumberland themes at once: dangerous upland walking, border dialect, fairy-like beings that are not cute or harmless, and a landscape where legend still affects how visitors imagine the place.

## The Laidly Worm: Northumberland’s dragon who is saved, not slain

The Laidly Worm of Spindleston Heugh is Northumberland’s great dragon story. It is usually connected with Bamburgh and Spindleston Heugh, where a princess named Margaret is transformed into a monstrous worm by a jealous witch-stepmother. Her brother, Childe Wynd, returns and breaks the enchantment not by killing the creature but by kissing it, restoring the princess to human form.

The story is important partly because it is not a simple dragon-slaying tale. In many English worm legends, a male hero destroys a monster. Here the “monster” is an enchanted woman, and the rescue depends on recognition, courage and affection rather than violence. That gives the Northumberland ballad a different emotional texture from neighbouring worm traditions such as the Lambton Worm of County Durham.

The evidential history is also interesting. Northumberland Archives states that the legend was first recorded by William Hutchinson in 1778 and that Hutchinson received the ballad from the Reverend Robert Lambe of Norham. The archive notes that Lambe was almost certainly involved in its composition, despite claims of an ancient manuscript source, and that the tale may have drawn on local songs and stories with wider Scottish and Icelandic parallels.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://northumberlandarchives.com/2020/06/01/the-laidley-worm-of-spindlestone-heugh/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: northumberlandarchives.com">[Northumberland Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">northumberlandarchives.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Northumberland Archives The Laidley Worm of Spindlestone Heugh</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Northumberland Archives The Laidley Worm of Spindlestone Heugh</span></span></span>

That does not make the Laidly Worm worthless as folklore. It makes it a good example of how folklore, literary antiquarianism and local identity mix. The tale has a firm Northumberland setting, a memorable crag-and-castle landscape, and a long afterlife in printed ballads, children’s retellings and visual art. But it should not be presented naïvely as an untouched medieval oral survival.

## Fairy hills, wells and small beings: not all Northumberland fairies are alike

Northumberland’s fairy traditions are varied. Some are perilous, like the Duergars; some are courtly or literary, like Queen Mab at Old Fawdon Hill; some are tied to wells, dancing places or mills. A modern survey of Northumberland fairy sites lists singing and dancing fairies at the Hurl Stane and Dancing Green Hill, a wish-granting fairy at Wooler Well, pottage-cooking fairies at Rothley Mill, mine goblins such as Cutty Soams and the Shilbottle Bluecap, Queen Mab at Old Fawdon Hill, and a fairy graveyard at Brinkburn.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://betweenworldsdurham.wordpress.com/2018/03/08/the-fairy-folk-of-northumberland/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: betweenworldsdurham.wordpress.com">[Between Worlds]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">betweenworldsdurham.wordpress.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Between Worlds The Fairy Folk of Northumberland &#124; Between Worlds</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Between Worlds The Fairy Folk of Northumberland &#124; Between Worlds</span></span></span>

These examples should be handled with care because they do not all rest on the same kind of evidence. Some are rooted in older antiquarian notes or named local sites; others are later compilations and retellings. Still, they show a key feature of Northumberland belief culture: fairy lore here is not just one creature-type. It includes hill fairies, mine spirits, domestic or mill beings, deceptive lights, dancing companies, and wish-related water sites.

Old Fawdon Hill is a useful example of the literary edge of the tradition. One folklore summary traces Queen Mab’s association with Fawdon Hill to James Service’s *Metrical Legends of Northumberland* in 1835, where the hill becomes a fairy royal residence.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://faeryfolklorist.blogspot.com/2009/06/fawdon-hill-fairies.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: faeryfolklorist.blogspot.com">[faeryfolklorist.blogspot.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">faeryfolklorist.blogspot.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">fawdon hill fairies</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">fawdon hill fairies</span></span></span> This is folklore shaped through poetry as much as through oral testimony, but it still matters because nineteenth-century literary folklore helped fix many local supernatural geographies in public memory.

Northumberland’s [fairy places]({{ 'fairy-places-666dcc/' | relative_url }}) also belong to a wider northern pattern. The county sits between English, Scottish and Border traditions, so its “fairies” can look like elves, dwarfs, bogles, mine spirits or household goblins depending on the tale. That variety is more authentic than a single neat definition.

## Black dogs, brags and other shape-shifters

The spectral black dog is one of the best-known motifs in English folklore, and Northumberland belongs to the northern range of that tradition. The Barghest or Barguest is usually imagined as a monstrous black dog and a death omen in northern England, though in Northumberland and Durham the term can also blur into ghost or household-spirit meanings.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barghest" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wikipedia.org.</span></span></span>

The important point is that Northumberland black-dog lore should not be treated as a single fixed monster with one official appearance. The wider black-dog motif includes death omens, road apparitions, threshold visitations, glowing eyes, crossroads, execution sites and old paths. Some dogs are deadly, some are warning signs, and a few in British folklore are protective.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_dog_%28folklore%29" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Black dog (folklore</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Black dog (folklore</span></span></span>

The brag is another Northumbrian shape-shifter. Folklore dictionaries describe the brag as a mischievous goblin of Northumbria, often appearing as a horse or donkey and tricking people into riding before throwing them into water, mud or bushes. One noted example, the Picktree Brag, could take forms including a calf, a headless man and figures carrying a white sheet.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brag_%28folklore%29" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Brag (folklore</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Brag (folklore</span></span></span>

These beings reveal the comic as well as sinister side of Northumberland folklore. A black dog may warn of death; a brag humiliates the unwary. Both, however, belong to the same imaginative world of roads, thresholds, night journeys and uncertain shapes.

## Witches, witch-finders and the danger of accusation

Northumberland witchcraft material is less about broomstick fantasy and more about accusation, fear, law and local conflict. Northumberland Archives has published material on witchcraft in the county, including educational resources on a 1606 witchcraft indictment and later discussions of witchcraft belief.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://northumberlandarchives.com/2020/04/14/witchcraft-in-northumberland/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: northumberlandarchives.com">[Northumberland Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">northumberlandarchives.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Northumberland Archives Witchcraft in Northumberland</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Northumberland Archives Witchcraft in Northumberland</span></span></span>

The most dramatic nearby case is the Newcastle witch trials of 1649–50, which sit in the historic Northumberland orbit even though they are often discussed as Newcastle civic history. ITV’s account notes that suspected witches were held in Newgate prison, while another accused woman was held in the dungeons of Newcastle Castle, described as her native Northumberland county prison.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.itv.com/news/tyne-tees/2024-08-21/the-dark-history-behind-the-newcastle-witch-trials-and-hangings-of-1650" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: itv.com">[ITVX]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">itv.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on itv.com.</span></span></span>

This is a useful reminder that “witch folklore” has two different meanings. One is supernatural story: witches in ballads and fairy tales, such as the stepmother in the Laidly Worm. The other is historical belief with real consequences: accusations, examinations, imprisonment and execution. A good Northumberland folklore page should keep these apart. The witch in a dragon ballad belongs to narrative tradition; the accused women of early modern Northumberland and Newcastle belong to social and legal history.

Anne Armstrong, a Northumberland witch-finder connected with seventeenth-century accusations, shows how unstable and dangerous the category could become. A county history text preserved online refers to Birches-nook in 1673 as the abode of Anne Armstrong and links her to a major Northumbrian witchcraft case involving accusations against Anne Baites of Morpeth.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/stream/historyofnorthum06nort/historyofnorthum06nort_djvu.txt" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on archive.org.</span></span></span> Here folklore touches the archive: stories about supernatural harm became testimony, and testimony could become punishment.

## Saints, islands and holy routes

Northumberland’s sacred folklore is dominated by Lindisfarne, St Cuthbert and St Oswald. This is not “folklore” in the same sense as fairy lights or black dogs, but saints’ lives, relic stories, healing traditions, animal miracles, pilgrimage routes and holy landscapes are central to the county’s legendary identity.

Lindisfarne was one of the great early Christian centres of Northumbria. The historic-county account notes Holy Island as the first Christian missionary centre in Northumbria and the seat of a bishop for many centuries.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://wikishire.co.uk/wiki/Northumberland" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wikishire.co.uk">[Wikishire]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wikishire.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wikishire.co.uk.</span></span></span> Cuthbert, associated with Lindisfarne, became one of the North’s most powerful saintly figures; retellings of his life preserve stories of healing, solitude, animals and holiness in wild places.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://saintsofthenorth.wordpress.com/saint-cuthbert/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: saintsofthenorth.wordpress.com">[Saints of the North]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">saintsofthenorth.wordpress.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Saints of the North Saint Cuthbert</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Saints of the North Saint Cuthbert</span></span></span>

St Oswald adds the royal and battlefield dimension. Modern St Oswald’s Way runs for about 97 miles through Northumberland, connecting Heavenfield and Lindisfarne and presenting the saint’s story through landscape, walking and pilgrimage.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.britishpilgrimage.org/portfolio/st-oswalds-way" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: britishpilgrimage.org">[British Pilgrimage Trust]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">britishpilgrimage.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">British Pilgrimage Trust St Oswald&#x27;s Way</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">British Pilgrimage Trust St Oswald&#x27;s Way</span></span></span> Recent travel writing also shows how strongly Northumberland still markets and experiences these saintly landscapes, especially Lindisfarne, Bamburgh and Heavenfield.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.thetimes.co.uk/article/st-oswalds-way-walking-holiday-northumberland-9j983t7h2" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: thetimes.co.uk">[The Times]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">thetimes.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Times Empty beaches and Roman ruins on the UK&#x27;s prettiest pilgrimage trail</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Times Empty beaches and Roman ruins on the UK&#x27;s prettiest pilgrimage trail</span></span></span>

These traditions matter because they make Northumberland’s folklore more than “spooky stories”. The county’s supernatural imagination includes holiness, blessing, relics, healing, island retreat and the memory of early medieval Northumbria as well as ghosts and goblins.

## Stones, carvings and ancient places with later stories

Northumberland’s prehistoric monuments are not folklore in themselves, but they attract folklore because they are old, visible and mysterious. The Duddo Five Stones are a good example. Northumberland Archives describes the Duddo Stones as the remains of a Neolithic or Bronze Age monument over 4,000 years old, also known as “The Singing Stones” and “The Women”.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://northumberlandarchives.com/2020/07/13/the-duddo-stones/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: northumberlandarchives.com">[Northumberland Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">northumberlandarchives.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Northumberland Archives The Duddo Stones</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Northumberland Archives The Duddo Stones</span></span></span> Historic England’s scheduled monument entry identifies the site as a Neolithic/Bronze Age stone circle on a knoll overlooking the River Tweed, with five weathered sandstone stones forming a rough circle about 10.5 metres across.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://historicengland.org.uk/listing/the-list/list-entry/1006622" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: historicengland.org.uk">[Historic England]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">historicengland.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on historicengland.org.uk.</span></span></span>

The names “Singing Stones” and “The Women” show how later people humanised or enchanted the monument. This is common in British stone folklore: old stones become dancers, women, petrified people, memorials or musical objects. The stories are rarely as old as the stones themselves, but they reveal how communities made sense of prehistoric remains long before modern archaeology.

Northumberland is also rich in prehistoric rock art, especially cup-and-ring marked stones. The Bradshaw Foundation’s summary of Northumberland rock art notes that Stan Beckensall recorded more than 1,500 carved rocks across northern England and beyond, and that North East England contains a major concentration of rock-art panels. It also stresses that the meaning of cup-and-ring marks remains uncertain.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.bradshawfoundation.com/british_isles_prehistory_archive/northumbria_rock_art/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: bradshawfoundation.com">[Bradshaw Foundation]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">bradshawfoundation.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on bradshawfoundation.com.</span></span></span>

For folklore readers, that uncertainty is part of the attraction. It is tempting to call the carvings star maps, ritual diagrams or spirit marks, but the responsible answer is that archaeologists do not know exactly what they meant. Their folklore value lies in the way mystery, place and deep time invite stories without needing false certainty.

## Border ballads: history turned into song

Northumberland’s ballad tradition is one of the county’s strongest contributions to wider British folklore. Border ballads preserve memories of raiding, feuding, loyalty, betrayal and battle in the English-Scottish marches. They are not neutral history, but they are powerful evidence of how communities sang conflict into memory.

“The Ballad of Chevy Chase” is one of the most famous examples. It tells of a bloody encounter in the Cheviot Hills involving Percy, Earl of Northumberland, and the Scottish Earl Douglas. It is catalogued as Child Ballad 162 and appears in multiple printed and manuscript traditions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Ballad_of_Chevy_Chase" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Ballad of Chevy Chase</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Ballad of Chevy Chase</span></span></span> “The Battle of Otterburn”, linked to the 1388 battle, is another major Border ballad and is closely related in subject matter, although the two traditions differ in perspective and historical detail.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Battle_of_Otterburn_%28ballad%29" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Battle of Otterburn (ballad</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Battle of Otterburn (ballad</span></span></span>

Thomas Percy’s *Reliques of Ancient English Poetry*, first published in 1765, helped bring such ballads into wider literary culture. The collection included “Chevy Chase” and “The Battle of Otterburn” and gave prominence to Border material connected with Northumberland and the Percy family.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reliques_of_Ancient_English_Poetry" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Reliques of Ancient English Poetry</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Reliques of Ancient English Poetry</span></span></span> Walter Scott’s later *Minstrelsy of the Scottish Border*, first published in 1802, continued the process from the Scottish side of the border, mixing field collection, manuscript work and editorial shaping.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://blog.nls.uk/walter-scott-and-the-minstrelsy-of-the-scottish-border/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: blog.nls.uk">[blog.nls.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">blog.nls.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">walter scott and the minstrelsy of the scottish border</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">walter scott and the minstrelsy of the scottish border</span></span></span>

For modern readers, the key is to read Border ballads as both folklore and crafted texts. They may preserve older oral traditions, but collectors edited, arranged and sometimes improved them. That makes them no less central to Northumberland identity; it simply means they belong to the history of performance, memory and print as well as to battlefield history.

## Customs that are still performed

Some Northumberland traditions are not only remembered; they are still performed. The Allendale Tar Bar’l is the county’s most spectacular winter custom. Visit Northumberland describes it as a New Year’s Eve ceremony in which 45 local men carry burning whisky barrels through the town, a tradition said to have been celebrated for centuries.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.visitnorthumberland.com/explore/seasons/winter/tar-barl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: visitnorthumberland.com">[Visit Northumberland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">visitnorthumberland.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on visitnorthumberland.com.</span></span></span>

The origin story is debated. One modern account says a common explanation links the custom to Victorian village carol singers whose candles were blown out by Pennine winds, so barrels of fire were used to light hymn sheets; other theories see it as a more socially expressive working-class New Year custom.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://thisisnorthumberland.co.uk/journal/allendale-tar-barl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: thisisnorthumberland.co.uk">[This Is Northumberland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">thisisnorthumberland.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">This Is Northumberland The Night of the Burning Barrels</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">This Is Northumberland The Night of the Burning Barrels</span></span></span> The sensible conclusion is that the custom is old and locally meaningful, but its exact beginning is uncertain.

Rapper sword dance is another living Northumbrian tradition, though it belongs to the North East coalfield rather than to the whole county equally. The Sword Dance Union describes the Northumbrian rapper sword dance as a fast North East tradition originally performed by miners in the pit villages of Northumberland and County Durham, using flexible tools called rappers and a linked team of five dancers.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sworddanceunion.org.uk/Rapper" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sworddanceunion.org.uk">[sworddanceunion.org.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sworddanceunion.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on sworddanceunion.org.uk.</span></span></span> Northumberland Archives also preserves older evidence for sword dancing in the region, including descriptions of young men travelling with music, swords and comic costume to perform for households in return for money.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://northumberlandarchives.com/2016/12/16/northumberland-and-durham-sword-dancing/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: northumberlandarchives.com">[Northumberland Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">northumberlandarchives.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Northumberland Archives Northumberland and Durham Sword Dancing</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Northumberland Archives Northumberland and Durham Sword Dancing</span></span></span>

These customs are important because they show folklore as action, not just story. Fire carried through Allendale and swords woven at speed by linked dancers are public performances of belonging. They make local identity visible.

## Ghosts, redcaps and haunted castles

Northumberland’s haunted reputation is heavily shaped by castles and border violence. Chillingham Castle is the best-known example. The castle’s own site presents it as a thirteenth-century Grade I listed Northumberland stronghold with ghost tours, a torture chamber and a long martial history.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://chillingham-castle.com/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: chillingham-castle.com">[Chillingham Castle]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">chillingham-castle.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on chillingham-castle.com.</span></span></span> Visit Northumberland also promotes Chillingham as one of the county’s most haunted attractions, naming stories such as the white pantry ghost, courtyard ghosts and voices in the chapel.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.visitnorthumberland.com/inspire-me/most-haunted" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: visitnorthumberland.com">[Visit Northumberland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">visitnorthumberland.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on visitnorthumberland.com.</span></span></span>

A grounded account should add that castle ghost traditions are often much younger than the buildings themselves. History Hit notes that the first recorded accounts of Chillingham’s ghost stories are much more recent than the medieval castle, being associated with Lady Leonora Tankerville after her arrival in the late nineteenth century.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.historyhit.com/chillingham-most-haunted-castle/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: historyhit.com">[History Hit]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">historyhit.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">chillingham most haunted castle</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">chillingham most haunted castle</span></span></span> That distinction matters: Chillingham is genuinely historic, and its ghost tourism is genuinely part of modern Northumberland culture, but not every ghost story attached to it is medieval.

The Cheviot Redcaps bring a darker fairy-warrior element. Northumberland National Park describes the Cheviot Redcaps as troll-like old men wearing red caps said to be coloured with human blood, associated with hidden places, ruins and the violent atmosphere of the border hills.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.northumberlandnationalpark.org.uk/spooky-tales-from-northumberland-national-park/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: northumberlandnationalpark.org.uk">[Northumberland National Park]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">northumberlandnationalpark.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Northumberland National Park Spooky tales from Northumberland National Park</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Northumberland National Park Spooky tales from Northumberland National Park</span></span></span> This tradition overlaps with wider Border folklore, where redcaps often haunt ruined towers and battle sites.

Ghosts and redcaps both show how Northumberland’s violent past becomes supernatural landscape. The county’s ruins are not just architectural remains; in local storytelling they become places where old danger still has a shape.

## What is old tradition, and what is modern retelling?

Northumberland folklore is strongest when the different layers are kept visible. A visitor may meet the Duergars through a National Park Halloween page, the Laidly Worm through a children’s retelling, the Tar Bar’l through tourism promotion, and Border ballads through printed anthologies. All are part of the folklore ecosystem, but they are not the same kind of evidence.

A practical way to sort the material is to ask four questions:

**Is it attached to a specific place?** The Duergars belong to Simonside; the Laidly Worm belongs to Spindleston Heugh and Bamburgh; Duddo’s stone names belong to a real stone circle; Allendale’s Tar Bar’l belongs to one village.

**Is there an early or identifiable source?** The Laidly Worm’s print history can be traced to Hutchinson and Lambe in the late eighteenth century; Border ballads appear in major manuscript and print traditions; witchcraft cases appear in legal and archival records.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://northumberlandarchives.com/2020/06/01/the-laidley-worm-of-spindlestone-heugh/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: northumberlandarchives.com">[northumberlandarchives.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">northumberlandarchives.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Northumberland Archives The Laidley Worm of Spindlestone Heugh</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Northumberland Archives The Laidley Worm of Spindlestone Heugh</span></span></span>

**Has the story been reshaped by collectors or tourism?** Many have. Percy, Scott, Lambe, local poets, castle guidebooks, tourism pages and modern folklore writers all helped shape what readers now receive as “Northumberland folklore”.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reliques_of_Ancient_English_Poetry" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[wikipedia.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Reliques of Ancient English Poetry</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Reliques of Ancient English Poetry</span></span></span>

**Does it still have a living form?** The Allendale Tar Bar’l and rapper sword dance are living customs; saintly routes are walked; haunted castles are visited; ballads are still sung or studied; fairy sites are reimagined by walkers, writers and local-history enthusiasts.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.visitnorthumberland.com/explore/seasons/winter/tar-barl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: visitnorthumberland.com">[visitnorthumberland.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">visitnorthumberland.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on visitnorthumberland.com.</span></span></span>

This layered view makes the folklore richer, not weaker. Northumberland’s legends have always moved between oral tale, song, printed antiquarianism, local pride, performance and tourism.

## Why Northumberland matters in UK folklore

Northumberland matters because it brings together several major strands of UK folklore in one county: Border balladry, fairy hills, dangerous dwarf-like beings, dragon legends, saintly landscapes, witchcraft history, living fire customs, mining dance traditions, black dogs, prehistoric stones and haunted castles. Few counties show so clearly how folklore can grow from both natural landscape and political frontier.

The county is also a bridge. It is English, but deeply shaped by Scottish proximity. It is Christian in its saintly memory, but full of older stones and ambiguous hill beings. It has elite castles and working-class mining customs. It has literary ballads collected by bishops and poets, but also practical warnings about bogs, night roads and dangerous places.

For a reader exploring Northumberland folklore today, the most memorable pattern is this: the stories usually tell you how to look at the land. Simonside becomes a place where lights may deceive you. Bamburgh becomes a castle of enchantment as well as power. Lindisfarne becomes a holy island of saints, animals and tides. Duddo becomes a circle of “women” or “singing” stones. Allendale becomes a village where fire carries the year across midnight. That is why Northumberland’s folklore still feels local, even when its motifs are shared across Britain.

    params.push('mkevt=1');
    params.push('mkcid=1');
    params.push('toolid=' + encodeURIComponent(cfg.tool_id || '10001'));
    if (reference) params.push('customid=' + encodeURIComponent(reference).slice(0, 256));
    return url + joiner + params.join('&');
  }
  function domainLabel(marketId) {
    var cfg = markets[marketId] || markets[defaultMarket] || {};
    return cfg.domain_label || 'eBay';
  }
  function flagClass(marketId) {
    return 'fr-ebay-market-flag fr-ebay-market-flag--' + String(marketId || '').toLowerCase().replace(/_/g, '-').replace(/[^a-z0-9-]+/g, '');
  }
  function closeMenu(section) {
    var menu = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-menu]');
    var trigger = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-trigger]');
    var picker = section.querySelector('.fr-ebay-market-picker');
    if (menu) menu.hidden = true;
    if (trigger) trigger.setAttribute('aria-expanded', 'false');
    if (picker) picker.classList.remove('fr-ebay-market-picker--open');
  }
  function availableMarkets(section) {
    var select = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-select]');
    if (select) {
      return Array.prototype.slice.call(select.options)
        .map(function (option) { return option.value; })
        .filter(function (marketId) { return !!markets[marketId]; });
    }
    return Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]'))
      .map(function (button) { return button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button'); })
      .filter(function (marketId) { return !!markets[marketId]; });
  }
  function applyMarket(section, marketId, persist) {
    var available = availableMarkets(section);
    if (available.indexOf(marketId)< 0) marketId = available[0] || defaultMarket;
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-localized-link]')).forEach(function (link) {
      var query = link.getAttribute('data-ebay-query') || '';
      var reference = link.getAttribute('data-ebay-reference') || '';
      link.setAttribute('href', buildUrl(query, reference, marketId));
    });
    var select = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-select]');
    if (select && available.indexOf(marketId) >= 0) select.value = marketId;
    var selectedText = marketId;
    if (select && select.selectedIndex >= 0) selectedText = select.options[select.selectedIndex].text;
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-selected-market-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = selectedText;
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-selected-market-flag], [data-ebay-trigger-market-flag]')).forEach(function (flag) {
      flag.className = flagClass(marketId);
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-trigger-market-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = selectedText;
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-domain-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = domainLabel(marketId);
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-option]')).forEach(function (button) {
      button.setAttribute('aria-selected', button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-option') === marketId ? 'true' : 'false');
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      var active = button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button') === marketId;
      button.setAttribute('aria-pressed', active ? 'true' : 'false');
    });
    section.setAttribute('data-ebay-selected-market', marketId);
    if (persist) {
      try { if (window.localStorage) localStorage.setItem('phoenix-ebay-market', marketId); } catch (err) {}
    }
    closeMenu(section);
  }
  sections.forEach(function (section) {
    if (section.getAttribute('data-ebay-localized-init') === '1') return;
    section.setAttribute('data-ebay-localized-init', '1');
    var available = availableMarkets(section);
    var marketId = defaultMarket;
    if (window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation && window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation.inferMarket) {
      marketId = window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation.inferMarket(available, {
        storageKey: 'phoenix-ebay-market',
        defaultMarket: defaultMarket
      });
    } else if (available.indexOf(defaultMarket)< 0) {
      marketId = available[0] || defaultMarket;
    }
    var select = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-select]');
    if (select) {
      select.addEventListener('change', function () {
        applyMarket(section, select.value, true);
      });
    }
    var trigger = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-trigger]');
    var menu = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-menu]');
    if (trigger && menu) {
      trigger.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
        event.stopPropagation();
        var expanded = trigger.getAttribute('aria-expanded') === 'true';
        menu.hidden = expanded;
        trigger.setAttribute('aria-expanded', expanded ? 'false' : 'true');
        var picker = trigger.closest('.fr-ebay-market-picker');
        if (picker) picker.classList.toggle('fr-ebay-market-picker--open', !expanded);
      });
      Array.prototype.slice.call(menu.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-option]')).forEach(function (button) {
        button.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
          event.stopPropagation();
          applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-option'), true);
        });
      });
      document.addEventListener('click', function () { closeMenu(section); });
    }
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      button.addEventListener('click', function () {
        applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button'), true);
      });
    });
    applyMarket(section, marketId, false);
  });
})();
</script>
