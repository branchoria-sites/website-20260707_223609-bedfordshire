## Introduction

[Sutherland]({{ 'where-sutherlands-old-stories-still/' | relative_url }})’s water-spirit stories are not vague Highland mood music. They are pinned to particular waters: the salmon pools of the Shin, a loch below Ben Stack near Laxford, and the burn running out of Loch Migdale past the place Charlotte Louisa [Dempster]({{ 'dempster/' | relative_url }}) recorded as Moulinna Vaugha, “the kelpie’s mill”. In these tales the dangerous figure is often called a Vaugh, Baugh or kelpie: usually female, usually attached to water, sometimes beautiful, sometimes monstrous, and never safely explained away as only a mermaid, ghost or fairy.



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/Sutherland_6dbe51_water_women_kelpies_394e09-overview.webp" | relative_url }}" alt="Overview image for Water Spirits" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

The strongest evidence comes from Dempster’s nineteenth-century Sutherland collecting. Her manuscript, *Tales from the people of Sutherland*, is dated 1859 and held by the National Library of Scotland; most of its tales were later published in *The Folk-Lore Journal* in 1888.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://manuscripts.nls.uk/repositories/2/archival_objects/25033" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: manuscripts.nls.uk">[National Library of Scotland Manuscripts]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">manuscripts.nls.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">te Louisa Dempster., 1859. &#124; Archives and Manuscripts Catalogue...</span></span></span> That makes these stories valuable not because they “prove” water spirits, but because they preserve local oral tradition at the point where Gaelic storytelling, estate memory, salmon fishing, mills and dangerous crossings met.

## Why Sutherland’s Water Spirits Feel So Local

The water-women of Sutherland belong to a landscape where lochs, burns and river pools were practical places as well as uncanny ones. A pool could hold salmon, a ford could be a necessary crossing, and a mill burn could be the working edge of a community. The supernatural danger in these tales is therefore not remote. It appears exactly where people worked, poached, travelled, milled grain, carried meal, or walked home after dark.

Dempster herself framed her 1888 publication as material gathered “from the mouths of the folk” in 1859, at a time when she believed such unwritten lore was already becoming difficult to collect in Sutherland.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Folk-Lore_Journal/Volume_6/Folk-Lore_of_Sutherlandshire_%28September%29" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: en.wikisource.org">[Wikisource]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">en.wikisource.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Folk-Lore Journal/Volume 6/Folk-Lore of Sutherlandshire (September</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Folk-Lore Journal/Volume 6/Folk-Lore of Sutherlandshire (September) - Wikisource, the free online library...</span></span></span> Her water-spirit material is especially useful because it often names informants or local transmitters: D. M. of Stack for the Shin tale, J. Macleod of Laxford for the Ben Stack episode, Mary Calder for the mill story, the miller’s wife for the Loch Migdale apparition, and Peggy Munroe for a nearby disappearance story in Creich.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Folk-Lore_Journal/Volume_6/Folk-Lore_of_Sutherlandshire_%28December%29" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: en.wikisource.org">[Wikisource]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">en.wikisource.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Folk-Lore Journal/Volume 6/Folk-Lore of Sutherlandshire (December</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Folk-Lore Journal/Volume 6/Folk-Lore of Sutherlandshire (December) - Wikisource, the free online library...</span></span></span>

There is also a useful warning here. The names are fluid. Dempster’s text moves between “Vaugh”, “Baugh”, “banshee”, “weird woman of the water” and “kelpie”. That does not mean all these beings were identical in every Highland district. It means Sutherland tradition, as written down in English from Gaelic-speaking informants, used overlapping labels for dangerous water-haunting figures. The page is best read as a family of local cases rather than a tidy monster catalogue.

## The Vaugh of the Shin

The River Shin story is one of Sutherland’s sharpest examples of folklore enforcing the danger of water after dark. Dempster’s “The Vaugh, the Poacher, and the Dog” begins with two men from Inveran who poach salmon in the Shin by dragging pools with a small net. The setting matters: this is not a romantic lochside encounter but a night-time trespass into valuable fishing water. When one man calls to his companion across the pool, the answer comes from something else.

Dempster’s version gives the scene a sudden, practical terror. A voice answers the poacher, and the Vaugh appears beside him, “revolting in face” and dressed in green. The man resists losing what the tale calls the wages of iniquity, and a dog fights the water-woman. The dog survives only after losing all its hair, while the poacher is said to turn grey from terror overnight and never again visit the Shin pools after dusk for illicit fishing.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Folk-Lore_Journal/Volume_6/Folk-Lore_of_Sutherlandshire_%28December%29" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: en.wikisource.org">[Wikisource]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">en.wikisource.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Folk-Lore Journal/Volume 6/Folk-Lore of Sutherlandshire (December</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Folk-Lore Journal/Volume 6/Folk-Lore of Sutherlandshire (December) - Wikisource, the free online library...</span></span></span>

The story works on several levels. At the simplest, it is a frightening encounter at a salmon pool. As local social memory, it is also a warning against poaching. As folklore, it places a female water-being at the boundary between human appetite and river danger. The Shin is not merely scenery: it is the place where greed, darkness, dogs, salmon and supernatural reprisal converge.

Dempster’s “Web-footed Kelpie” section adds another Shin-side sighting. A richly dressed weird woman, wearing gold and silk, is said to have been seen by an old keeper running down a steep slope on the east side of the River Shin before disappearing into one of its deepest pools. Dempster says she was seen by several “trustworthy witnesses”, a phrase that tells us how the story was presented locally: not just as a fireside tale, but as something supported by named or socially credible observers.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Folk-Lore_Journal/Volume_6/Folk-Lore_of_Sutherlandshire_%28December%29" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: en.wikisource.org">[Wikisource]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">en.wikisource.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Folk-Lore Journal/Volume 6/Folk-Lore of Sutherlandshire (December</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Folk-Lore Journal/Volume 6/Folk-Lore of Sutherlandshire (December) - Wikisource, the free online library...</span></span></span>

That does not make the apparition factual in a modern evidential sense. It does show how belief gained weight in a rural community: the tale was attached to known work, known water, known witnesses and known moral boundaries. The Vaugh haunts the Shin because the Shin was already a charged place.

## The Laxford Water-Fairy and the Dog

The Laxford example is shorter, but it widens the geography of the Vaugh beyond the Shin. Dempster records “The Vaugh of the Laxford” immediately after the Shin tale. She says the figure seems to have been migratory, because a man met her by a loch on the south side of Ben Stack. He had a large white and yellow dog which neighbours had advised him to kill because it had a “bad name”, but in the encounter the dog attacks the Vaugh and prevails. Both the woman and the dog then fall over the steep terraces on the north side of the hill and disappear.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Folk-Lore_Journal/Volume_6/Folk-Lore_of_Sutherlandshire_%28December%29" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: en.wikisource.org">[Wikisource]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">en.wikisource.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Folk-Lore Journal/Volume 6/Folk-Lore of Sutherlandshire (December</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Folk-Lore Journal/Volume 6/Folk-Lore of Sutherlandshire (December) - Wikisource, the free online library...</span></span></span>

This is a compact story, but it is rich in local texture. Ben Stack is a real Sutherland landmark; Laxford is not being used as a generic Highland label. The tale also gives the dog an ambiguous status. It may be dangerous or uncanny itself, yet it becomes the man’s protector. In other words, the water-woman is not defeated by simple human bravery. She is met by another creature with a reputation close enough to the supernatural to survive the encounter, at least until both vanish.

The Laxford tale also prevents a too-narrow reading of the Vaugh as only a mill-haunting spirit. Here she is met by a loch near a mountain, not at a mill wheel or ford. Dempster’s note that she was “migratory” is important: it suggests that the tradition was not about one fixed ghost, but about a type of dangerous female water-being that could attach to several places in Sutherland’s landscape.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Folk-Lore_Journal/Volume_6/Folk-Lore_of_Sutherlandshire_%28December%29" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: en.wikisource.org">[Wikisource]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">en.wikisource.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Folk-Lore Journal/Volume 6/Folk-Lore of Sutherlandshire (December</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Folk-Lore Journal/Volume 6/Folk-Lore of Sutherlandshire (December) - Wikisource, the free online library...</span></span></span>

A small uncertainty remains. Dempster adds that some ascribed the deed to Donald Duival Mackay, which hints at variant local attribution.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Folk-Lore_Journal/Volume_6/Folk-Lore_of_Sutherlandshire_%28December%29" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: en.wikisource.org">[Wikisource]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">en.wikisource.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Folk-Lore Journal/Volume 6/Folk-Lore of Sutherlandshire (December</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Folk-Lore Journal/Volume 6/Folk-Lore of Sutherlandshire (December) - Wikisource, the free online library...</span></span></span> That is exactly how oral tradition often behaves: the core motif survives, while the human name, heroic credit or local family connection shifts from teller to teller.

## Loch Migdale and the Kelpie’s Mill

The richest cluster of Sutherland water-spirit material gathers around Loch Migdale and the mill called Moulinna Vaugha or Moulinna Gleannan. Dempster explicitly defines “Vaugh, or Baugh” as a water-fairy attached to this mill, and notes that the word appeared as “fouah” in estate maps and surveys made when Captain J. Hamilton Dempster bought the estate. She also identifies the source chain: the story was told in Gaelic by widow Mary Calder to D. M., the gamekeeper, and transcribed for C. H. D.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Folk-Lore_Journal/Volume_6/Folk-Lore_of_Sutherlandshire_%28December%29" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: en.wikisource.org">[Wikisource]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">en.wikisource.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Folk-Lore Journal/Volume 6/Folk-Lore of Sutherlandshire (December</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Folk-Lore Journal/Volume 6/Folk-Lore of Sutherlandshire (December) - Wikisource, the free online library...</span></span></span>

The central capture story is almost comic until it turns uncanny. A man connected with Inveran bets that he can seize the kelpie of Moulinna Vaugha and bring her bound to the inn at Inveran. He uses a brown horse and a black-muzzled dog, secures the Vaugh, ties her behind him, and keeps her subdued with an awl and a needle. When they cross the burn at the far side of Loch Migdale she becomes violently restless, and Dempster gives a translated cry in which the creature can endure the crooked awl better than the small sharp needle.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Folk-Lore_Journal/Volume_6/Folk-Lore_of_Sutherlandshire_%28December%29" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: en.wikisource.org">[Wikisource]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">en.wikisource.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Folk-Lore Journal/Volume 6/Folk-Lore of Sutherlandshire (December</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Folk-Lore Journal/Volume 6/Folk-Lore of Sutherlandshire (December) - Wikisource, the free online library...</span></span></span>

The ending is one of the strangest details in the Sutherland corpus. When the man reaches Inveran and calls his companions to bring lights, the Vaugh falls to the ground like the remains of a fallen star, a small lump of jelly. Dempster adds that such jellies were seen on the moors and called “dropped stars”.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Folk-Lore_Journal/Volume_6/Folk-Lore_of_Sutherlandshire_%28December%29" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: en.wikisource.org">[Wikisource]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">en.wikisource.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Folk-Lore Journal/Volume 6/Folk-Lore of Sutherlandshire (December</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Folk-Lore Journal/Volume 6/Folk-Lore of Sutherlandshire (December) - Wikisource, the free online library...</span></span></span> The supernatural moment is therefore tied to a real natural curiosity: mysterious jelly-like matter found outdoors, interpreted through local belief.

The mill is also the home of the Brolachan Mac Vaugh, the shapeless “son of the Vaugh”. Dempster describes him as having eyes and a mouth, no proper shape, and only two words: “myself” and “yourself”. In the tale, a disabled man called Murray, known as Allay na Moulin, burns the Brolachan by throwing peat on the fire; when the enraged Vaugh demands to know who did it, the creature cannot explain. Murray hides among the machinery and escapes, but a woman passing the place that night is chased so fiercely that the Vaugh tears off her heel as she reaches her own door.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Folk-Lore_Journal/Volume_6/Folk-Lore_of_Sutherlandshire_%28December%29" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: en.wikisource.org">[Wikisource]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">en.wikisource.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Folk-Lore Journal/Volume 6/Folk-Lore of Sutherlandshire (December</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Folk-Lore Journal/Volume 6/Folk-Lore of Sutherlandshire (December) - Wikisource, the free online library...</span></span></span>

These mill stories are more than monster anecdotes. They turn a working building into a threshold. Grain, fire, machinery, charity, night shelter and running water all gather in one place. The mill is socially central but also dangerous after dark. That is why the Loch Migdale tradition feels so memorable: the supernatural is embedded in the everyday technology of the district.

## The Woman in Green at Loch Migdale

Dempster’s “Banshee, or Vaugh, or Weird Woman of the Water” gives the Migdale spirit a more visual form. She places Loch Migdale four or five miles from Skibo, with a granite rock to the north, and says that at one end a burn runs out past Moulinna Vaugha, the kelpie’s mill. The miller’s wife reportedly saw the being sitting quietly on a stone, beautifully dressed in green silk with puffed sleeves from wrist to shoulder. When seen more closely, however, her long yellow hair and fine clothing were undercut by one grotesque detail: she had no nose.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Folk-Lore_Journal/Volume_6/Folk-Lore_of_Sutherlandshire_%28December%29" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: en.wikisource.org">[Wikisource]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">en.wikisource.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Folk-Lore Journal/Volume 6/Folk-Lore of Sutherlandshire (December</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Folk-Lore Journal/Volume 6/Folk-Lore of Sutherlandshire (December) - Wikisource, the free online library...</span></span></span>

That contrast is typical of dangerous fairy and water-woman traditions across the Highlands: beauty invites attention, but a bodily flaw reveals the non-human nature of the being. In Sutherland’s version, the detail is not a broad moral lesson about vanity. It is a precise remembered image: green silk, yellow hair, a stone by the burn, and the miller’s wife as witness.

A neighbouring “Web-footed Kelpie” tale repeats the same pattern in rougher form. A shepherd finds an apparently crippled, dirty old banshee at the edge of a moss and offers to carry her. When he sees her webbed feet dangling down his back, he throws her off, discards the plaid on which she had lain, and runs.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Folk-Lore_Journal/Volume_6/Folk-Lore_of_Sutherlandshire_%28December%29" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: en.wikisource.org">[Wikisource]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">en.wikisource.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Folk-Lore Journal/Volume 6/Folk-Lore of Sutherlandshire (December</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Folk-Lore Journal/Volume 6/Folk-Lore of Sutherlandshire (December) - Wikisource, the free online library...</span></span></span> In both stories, the human observer recognises the danger through an anatomical sign: no nose, webbed feet, something that breaks the disguise.

The wider Loch Migdale landscape deepens the atmosphere without explaining the story away. Historic Environment Scotland records a small henge near the west end of Loch Migdale: a Neolithic ritual enclosure, probably dating between 3500 and 2500 BC, about 12 metres across, with ditch, bank and a single entrance.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://portal.historicenvironment.scot/apex/f?p=1505%3A300%3A%3A%3A%3A%3AVIEWTYPE%2CVIEWREF%3Adesignation%2CSM10916" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: portal.historicenvironment.scot">[Historic Environment Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">portal.historicenvironment.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Historic Environment ScotlandLoch Migdale, henge 160m ESE of Lochend (SM10916)...</span></span></span> Wessex Archaeology’s later Loch Migdale evaluation examined a crannog, henge and hut circle and described the work as producing important information on the area’s prehistory.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://wessexarchaeologylibrary.org/library/repository/view/236/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wessexarchaeologylibrary.org">[wessexarchaeologylibrary.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wessexarchaeologylibrary.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Time Team</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Time Team</span></span></span>

The folklore should not be treated as a direct survival from the Neolithic. That would be a leap beyond the evidence. What can be said is more modest and more useful: Loch Migdale is a landscape where water, old settlement traces, ritual archaeology, mills and later oral tradition overlap. The kelpie’s mill belongs to a place already layered with human memory.

## What Kind of Being Is a Vaugh?

For a modern reader, the hardest question is classification. Is the Sutherland Vaugh a kelpie, fairy, banshee, ghost, water-woman or something else? Dempster’s answer, intentionally or not, is: more than one of these at once. Her headings and notes use “Vaugh”, “Baugh”, “water-fairy”, “kelpie”, “banshee” and “weird woman of the water” across closely related cases.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Folk-Lore_Journal/Volume_6/Folk-Lore_of_Sutherlandshire_%28December%29" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: en.wikisource.org">[Wikisource]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">en.wikisource.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Folk-Lore Journal/Volume 6/Folk-Lore of Sutherlandshire (December</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Folk-Lore Journal/Volume 6/Folk-Lore of Sutherlandshire (December) - Wikisource, the free online library...</span></span></span>

Several features recur strongly enough to define the local pattern:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **Water attachment:** the Shin pools, the Laxford-side loch, Loch Migdale’s outflow burn, a ford on the Oykel, and deep river pools all matter as settings.
* **Female presentation:** the being often appears as a woman, whether revolting, richly dressed, crippled, beautiful or old.
* **Disguise and revelation:** the danger becomes visible through a flaw, such as webbed feet or the missing nose.
* **Animal conflict:** dogs play a striking role, either fighting the Vaugh or helping capture her.
* **Iron or sharp tools:** the Loch Migdale capture tale gives special force to the awl and needle.
* **Mills and crossings:** the creature’s territory is not just open water, but the human edges of water: mills, burns, fords and fishing pools.

</div>

Dempster also distinguishes dressed fairies from water-kelpies, saying that kelpies are more “mischievous” and preside over mills and fords, where they do harm.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Folk-Lore_Journal/Volume_6/Folk-Lore_of_Sutherlandshire_%28December%29" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: en.wikisource.org">[Wikisource]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">en.wikisource.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Folk-Lore Journal/Volume 6/Folk-Lore of Sutherlandshire (December</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Folk-Lore Journal/Volume 6/Folk-Lore of Sutherlandshire (December) - Wikisource, the free online library...</span></span></span> This is one of the most useful interpretive comments in the source. It places the Vaugh close to the kelpie family, but not neatly inside the later popular image of the kelpie as a horse that drowns riders. In Sutherland, the water-being is often woman-shaped.

That is why a plain English description works best: these are Sutherland water-women and kelpie-like beings, preserved under local names. Calling them only “kelpies” loses the female water-fairy texture. Calling them only “fairies” loses the danger of mills, fords and pools.

## How Strong Is the Evidence?

The evidence is strong for nineteenth-century Sutherland oral tradition, but not for a single fixed ancient myth. The core source is Dempster’s collecting: a manuscript dated 1859, later published in 1888, with named local informants and place-specific detail. The National Library of Scotland catalogue confirms the manuscript as *Tales from the people of Sutherland*, containing stories, beliefs and riddles, with most tales published as *The Folk-lore of Sutherlandshire*.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://manuscripts.nls.uk/repositories/2/archival_objects/25033" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: manuscripts.nls.uk">[National Library of Scotland Manuscripts]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">manuscripts.nls.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">te Louisa Dempster., 1859. &#124; Archives and Manuscripts Catalogue...</span></span></span>

Within that source, the water-spirit material has unusually good local anchoring. The Shin tale is attached to Inveran and salmon pools; the Laxford tale names J. Macleod; the Moulinna Vaugha story gives Mary Calder as Gaelic teller and D. M. the gamekeeper as intermediary; the Migdale apparition names the miller’s wife as witness; the web-footed kelpie tale is attached to a Dempster sheep-farm.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Folk-Lore_Journal/Volume_6/Folk-Lore_of_Sutherlandshire_%28December%29" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: en.wikisource.org">[Wikisource]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">en.wikisource.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Folk-Lore Journal/Volume 6/Folk-Lore of Sutherlandshire (December</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Folk-Lore Journal/Volume 6/Folk-Lore of Sutherlandshire (December) - Wikisource, the free online library...</span></span></span>

The weaknesses are also clear. The stories reach us through Dempster’s English publication, not through full Gaelic transcripts on the page. Some names and spellings vary: Moulinna Vaugha, Moulinna Gleannan, Migdall, Vaugh, Baugh and “fouah”. The Laxford story has variant attribution. The categories are blended. These are not problems to hide; they are part of what makes the material recognisably oral.

The safest conclusion is that Sutherland had a well-attested cluster of water-spirit traditions by the mid-nineteenth century, especially around the Shin, Laxford and Loch Migdale. The traditions were remembered through particular people and places, but their deeper age cannot be pinned down with confidence.

## Why These Stories Still Matter in Sutherland

The water-women and kelpies matter because they show Sutherland folklore at its most local. A reader looking for one famous monster may miss the real pattern: Sutherland’s supernatural map is made from named pools, burns, mills, lochs, hills and witnesses. The stories make ordinary places feel charged. A salmon pool becomes morally dangerous. A lochside dog becomes a defender against a water-woman. A mill becomes the home of a shapeless child of the Vaugh. A woman in green silk becomes terrifying because of one missing feature.

They also show how folklore preserves social memory. The Shin story remembers anxiety about poaching and night fishing. The mill stories remember working buildings as places of both community and risk. The Migdale apparition turns clothing, bodily detail and local testimony into a haunting image. The Laxford tale keeps alive the idea that certain animals, like certain waters, could have uncanny reputations of their own.

For modern retellings, these stories are easy to oversimplify into “Scottish kelpie legends”. The better version keeps the Sutherland grain intact. The Vaugh of the Shin is not the same scene as the green-clad woman of Loch Migdale. The Laxford dog is not a decorative sidekick. The mill is not just a spooky ruin. Each case shows a different way water could become dangerous in the local imagination.

Within the wider Sutherland branch, these tales sit naturally beside mermaid stories, [fairy hills]({{ 'fairy-hills-e133aa/' | relative_url }}), witch traditions, Loch Shin legends and haunted routes through Assynt and Strathnaver. Their special contribution is that they make water itself the county’s threshold: useful, beautiful, economically vital, and never quite safe after dark.

    return 'fr-ebay-market-flag fr-ebay-market-flag--' + String(marketId || '').toLowerCase().replace(/_/g, '-').replace(/[^a-z0-9-]+/g, '');
  }
  function closeMenu(section) {
    var menu = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-menu]');
    var trigger = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-trigger]');
    var picker = section.querySelector('.fr-ebay-market-picker');
    if (menu) menu.hidden = true;
    if (trigger) trigger.setAttribute('aria-expanded', 'false');
    if (picker) picker.classList.remove('fr-ebay-market-picker--open');
  }
  function availableMarkets(section) {
    var select = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-select]');
    if (select) {
      return Array.prototype.slice.call(select.options)
        .map(function (option) { return option.value; })
        .filter(function (marketId) { return !!markets[marketId]; });
    }
    return Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]'))
      .map(function (button) { return button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button'); })
      .filter(function (marketId) { return !!markets[marketId]; });
  }
  function applyMarket(section, marketId, persist) {
    var available = availableMarkets(section);
    if (available.indexOf(marketId)< 0) marketId = available[0] || defaultMarket;
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-localized-link]')).forEach(function (link) {
      var query = link.getAttribute('data-ebay-query') || '';
      var reference = link.getAttribute('data-ebay-reference') || '';
      link.setAttribute('href', buildUrl(query, reference, marketId));
    });
    var select = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-select]');
    if (select && available.indexOf(marketId) >= 0) select.value = marketId;
    var selectedText = marketId;
    if (select && select.selectedIndex >= 0) selectedText = select.options[select.selectedIndex].text;
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-selected-market-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = selectedText;
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-selected-market-flag], [data-ebay-trigger-market-flag]')).forEach(function (flag) {
      flag.className = flagClass(marketId);
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-trigger-market-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = selectedText;
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-domain-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = domainLabel(marketId);
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-option]')).forEach(function (button) {
      button.setAttribute('aria-selected', button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-option') === marketId ? 'true' : 'false');
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      var active = button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button') === marketId;
      button.setAttribute('aria-pressed', active ? 'true' : 'false');
    });
    section.setAttribute('data-ebay-selected-market', marketId);
    if (persist) {
      try { if (window.localStorage) localStorage.setItem('phoenix-ebay-market', marketId); } catch (err) {}
    }
    closeMenu(section);
  }
  sections.forEach(function (section) {
    if (section.getAttribute('data-ebay-localized-init') === '1') return;
    section.setAttribute('data-ebay-localized-init', '1');
    var available = availableMarkets(section);
    var marketId = defaultMarket;
    if (window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation && window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation.inferMarket) {
      marketId = window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation.inferMarket(available, {
        storageKey: 'phoenix-ebay-market',
        defaultMarket: defaultMarket
      });
    } else if (available.indexOf(defaultMarket)< 0) {
      marketId = available[0] || defaultMarket;
    }
    var select = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-select]');
    if (select) {
      select.addEventListener('change', function () {
        applyMarket(section, select.value, true);
      });
    }
    var trigger = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-trigger]');
    var menu = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-menu]');
    if (trigger && menu) {
      trigger.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
        event.stopPropagation();
        var expanded = trigger.getAttribute('aria-expanded') === 'true';
        menu.hidden = expanded;
        trigger.setAttribute('aria-expanded', expanded ? 'false' : 'true');
        var picker = trigger.closest('.fr-ebay-market-picker');
        if (picker) picker.classList.toggle('fr-ebay-market-picker--open', !expanded);
      });
      Array.prototype.slice.call(menu.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-option]')).forEach(function (button) {
        button.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
          event.stopPropagation();
          applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-option'), true);
        });
      });
      document.addEventListener('click', function () { closeMenu(section); });
    }
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      button.addEventListener('click', function () {
        applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button'), true);
      });
    });
    applyMarket(section, marketId, false);
  });
})();
</script>
