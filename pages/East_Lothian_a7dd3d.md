## Introduction

East Lothian’s folklore is unusually vivid because it is tied to places people can still recognise: the ruined [Goblin Ha]({{ 'goblin-ha/' | relative_url }})’ below Yester Castle, the old kirk at North Berwick, the Witches’ Stone at Spott, Traprain Law rising out of farmland, Bass Rock offshore, and the riding of old burgh boundaries. The county’s legendary identity is not just a set of spooky tales. It is a layered local memory of medieval castles, early Christian saints, witch panics, ancient hillforts, coastal pilgrimage, farming landscapes and modern heritage retelling.



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/East_Lothian_a7dd3d-overview.webp" | relative_url }}" alt="Overview image for Where East Lothian&#x27;s Legends Still Have..." loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

For this project, East Lothian means the historic county also known as Haddingtonshire. The wider historic-counties map used by the project treats these old county areas as the organising frame, and East Lothian sits on the south side of the Firth of Forth, with the Lammermuir Hills forming the southern divide from Berwickshire. Modern local government has shifted at points, but the folklore centre of gravity remains the historic county: Haddington, North Berwick, Dunbar, Dirleton, Gifford, Spott, Traprain Law, Bass Rock and the coastal plain between them.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File%3ABritish_Isles_map_showing_UK%2C_Republic_of_Ireland%2C_and_historic_counties.svg" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: commons.wikimedia.org">[Wikimedia Commons]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">commons.wikimedia.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wikimedia.org.</span></span></span>

## What makes East Lothian folklore distinctive?

East Lothian’s strongest traditions are not chiefly about remote Highland otherworlds or romanticised Celtic mist. They are Lowland, coastal and documentary. Many of the best-known stories are attached to named people, court records, church courts, antiquarian inventories or surviving ruins. That gives the county’s folklore a particular character: dramatic, but often uncomfortably close to real social history.

Three strands stand out. First, East Lothian has one of Scotland’s most famous witchcraft landscapes, centred on the North Berwick [witch trials]({{ 'witch-trials-4ca92d/' | relative_url }}) of 1590–92 and later local cases at Dirleton and Spott. Secondly, it has a deep medieval legend of magic and hobgoblins at Yester, where the subterranean Goblin Ha’ made a local nobleman into the “Wizard of Yester”. Thirdly, it has a sacred coastal geography around St Baldred, Bass Rock, East Linton, Tyninghame, Auldhame and Whitekirk, where saintly memory, holy wells and pilgrimage traditions sit alongside seabirds, ruined chapels and old parish claims.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://libraryblogs.is.ed.ac.uk/edinburghuniversityarchives/2020/10/30/the-scotichronicon-and-the-goblin-ha-witchcraft-and-hobgoblins-in-our-manuscripts/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: libraryblogs.is.ed.ac.uk">[ed.ac.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">libraryblogs.is.ed.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on ed.ac.uk.</span></span></span>

The county also has prehistoric and civic layers. Traprain Law, one of Scotland’s great hillforts, dominates the plain and gathers later imagination because it looks like a natural stage for old kings, armies and lost peoples. Haddington’s Riding of the Marches belongs to a different type of tradition: not a ghost story, but a public custom about boundaries, common land, civic memory and local belonging.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://hillforts.arch.ox.ac.uk/records/SC3932.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: hillforts.arch.ox.ac.uk">[Atlas of Hillforts]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">hillforts.arch.ox.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on ox.ac.uk.</span></span></span>

## Yester Castle and the Goblin Ha’: East Lothian’s wizard legend

The most folkloric ruin in East Lothian is Yester Castle, near Gifford. Little remains above ground compared with the legend attached to it, but the underground chamber known as the Goblin Ha’ or Hobgoblin Ha’ has given the site a reputation far beyond ordinary castle history. East Lothian Council’s Historic Environment Record identifies Yester Castle and the Goblin Ha’ as a medieval castle site, with the Goblin Ha’ possibly dating from the 13th century.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.johngraycentre.org/collections/getrecord/ELHER_MEL663/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: johngraycentre.org">[John Gray Centre]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">johngraycentre.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on johngraycentre.org.</span></span></span>

The story centres on Hugo de Giffard, a 13th-century lord of Yester. In sober history he was a powerful local figure, associated with the guardianship and regency arrangements around the young Alexander III. In legend he became a magician or necromancer who summoned hobgoblins to build his underground hall. A University of Edinburgh Archives discussion traces the famous medieval reference not to simple modern invention, but to the *Scotichronicon*, where Walter Bower’s continuation preserves a story that Hugo’s cave and dungeon were said to have been made by demonic art.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://libraryblogs.is.ed.ac.uk/edinburghuniversityarchives/2020/10/30/the-scotichronicon-and-the-goblin-ha-witchcraft-and-hobgoblins-in-our-manuscripts/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: libraryblogs.is.ed.ac.uk">[Library &amp; University Collections]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">libraryblogs.is.ed.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on ed.ac.uk.</span></span></span>

That does not mean goblins built the chamber. It means the legend is old enough to matter. The Goblin Ha’ is a strong example of how a striking architectural feature can attract supernatural explanation. A dark vaulted space beneath a ruined castle naturally invites questions: who made it, why is it there, and why does it feel so unlike the open countryside around it? In local tradition, the answer became magical labour, a pact with spirits and a nobleman whose power crossed the line between lordship and sorcery.

Later writers helped preserve and popularise the tale. Sir Walter Scott referred to Hugo and the Goblin Hall in *Marmion*, and modern walkers, local-history pages and heritage guides still retell the legend as one of East Lothian’s most atmospheric stories. The important distinction is that Yester is both an archaeological site and a literary-folkloric site: the chamber is real, the medieval reputation is documented, and the full hobgoblin-building narrative has grown through retelling.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://libraryblogs.is.ed.ac.uk/edinburghuniversityarchives/2020/10/30/the-scotichronicon-and-the-goblin-ha-witchcraft-and-hobgoblins-in-our-manuscripts/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: libraryblogs.is.ed.ac.uk">[Library &amp; University Collections]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">libraryblogs.is.ed.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on ed.ac.uk.</span></span></span>

## North Berwick and the witch panic that became national folklore

The North Berwick witch trials are the county’s best-known supernatural history, but they should be handled carefully. They are folklore in the sense that they generated stories of devil meetings, sea magic, storms, night gatherings and charms. They are also a record of persecution, torture and execution. The people accused were not fantasy witches; they were real men and women caught in a deadly early modern panic.

The trials began in 1590 and implicated around 70 people, according to contemporary and later accounts. A University of Glasgow account of the 1591 pamphlet *Newes from Scotland* describes Geillis Duncan being tortured and then naming others, including Agnes Sampson, Doctor Fian, Barbara Napier and Effie MacCalyan. The same pamphlet claimed that a large group met the Devil in the kirk at North Berwick and used ritual magic, including a dead cat cast into the sea, to raise storms against King James VI’s return from Denmark with Anne of Denmark.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gla.ac.uk/myglasgow/library/files/special/exhibns/month/aug2000.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gla.ac.uk">[University of Glasgow]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gla.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">University of Glasgow Newes from Scotland</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">University of Glasgow Newes from Scotland</span></span></span>

Agnes Sampson, often called the Wise Wife of Keith, is central to the East Lothian memory of the trials. Modern scholarship and historical explainers describe her as a local healer and midwife figure before she was transformed, under accusation and torture, into one of the most famous supposed witches in Scotland. Her story shows why East Lothian witchcraft tradition is not just about eerie scenes at North Berwick kirk. It is about the dangerous overlap between folk healing, neighbourly suspicion, religious fear, state power and royal anxiety.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://origins.osu.edu/read/agnes-sampson-witchcraft-edinburgh" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: origins.osu.edu">[Origins]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">origins.osu.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">agnes sampson witchcraft edinburgh</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">agnes sampson witchcraft edinburgh</span></span></span>

The North Berwick material also shows how old “evidence” becomes folklore. *Newes from Scotland* was not a neutral modern report. It was a sensational printed account, shaped by the politics and beliefs of its time. Yet because it contained vivid scenes — the Devil in the kirk, night dances, storm-raising, confessions before the king — it fixed the imagery through which later generations imagined Scottish witchcraft. The county therefore sits at a crossroads between court record, propaganda, oral retelling and modern popular culture.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gla.ac.uk/myglasgow/library/files/special/exhibns/month/aug2000.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gla.ac.uk">[University of Glasgow]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gla.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">University of Glasgow Newes from Scotland</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">University of Glasgow Newes from Scotland</span></span></span>

## Dirleton, Spott and the local memory of accused witches

East Lothian’s witchcraft traditions did not end with North Berwick. Dirleton Castle became a prison for accused witches during the panic of 1649–50. Historic Environment Scotland records that more than 600 people were accused across southern and eastern Scotland during that wave, with six from the parish of Dirleton. The castle’s pit prison, usually a feature of medieval power, became part of a later landscape of fear and interrogation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://blog.historicenvironment.scot/2020/03/dirleton-witch-hunts/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: blog.historicenvironment.scot">[Historic Environment Scotland Blog]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">blog.historicenvironment.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Historic Environment Scotland Blog The Dirleton Witch Hunts</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Historic Environment Scotland Blog The Dirleton Witch Hunts</span></span></span>

One Dirleton case, Agnes Clarkson, is especially striking for folklore readers because her confession included the Devil appearing first in the likeness of a black dun dog before taking human form. That detail matters beyond Dirleton itself. Britain has many black-dog traditions, from spectral hounds on roads to churchyard apparitions, but here the “black dog” appears inside a witchcraft confession shaped by demonological questioning. It is not quite the same as a free-standing black-dog legend; it is a legal-religious narrative in which a familiar folklore image was folded into a confession.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://blog.historicenvironment.scot/2020/03/dirleton-witch-hunts/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: blog.historicenvironment.scot">[Historic Environment Scotland Blog]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">blog.historicenvironment.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Historic Environment Scotland Blog The Dirleton Witch Hunts</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Historic Environment Scotland Blog The Dirleton Witch Hunts</span></span></span>

Spott, near Dunbar, preserves a different kind of memory in the Witches’ Stone. Older antiquarian notes recorded a rough stone said to mark the place where Marion Lillie, the “Ringwoodie” or “Rigwoody” Witch, was burnt around 1698, and the New Statistical Account linked the stone with the supposed “last witch executed in Scotland”. Trove’s record preserves this older tradition while also showing how it depended on antiquarian and local report rather than a simple, settled fact.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.trove.scot/place/57667" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: trove.scot">[Trove Scot]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">trove.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Scot Witches&#x27; Stone, Spott &#124; Place &#124; trove.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Scot Witches&#x27; Stone, Spott &#124; Place &#124; trove.scot</span></span></span>

Modern local research has complicated the older story. Spott Community Association’s account, drawing on local historian Ron Pugh, says Marion Lillie was charged in 1698 and apparently released; accused again in 1704, she died in February 1705 and was buried in Spott Churchyard. Since those found guilty of witchcraft would not normally be buried in holy ground, the local interpretation now stresses that she was not actually convicted and burnt, even though the stone and later song tradition kept the older burning story alive.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://spottvillage.org.uk/the-witches-stone/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: spottvillage.org.uk">[spottvillage.org.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">spottvillage.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Witches Stone – Spott Community Association</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Witches Stone – Spott Community Association</span></span></span>

That correction does not make the Witches’ Stone less folkloric. It makes it more interesting. The site shows how communities inherit powerful stories, then rework them as new evidence and new ethics emerge. The stone has shifted from a marker of a possibly mistaken execution legend into a broader memorial for women and others persecuted as witches.

## St Baldred, Bass Rock and the sacred coast

East Lothian’s supernatural landscape is not all witches and goblins. Around East Linton, Tyninghame, Auldhame, Whitekirk and Bass Rock, the county preserves a Christian legendary geography associated with St Baldred, sometimes called the Apostle of the Lothians. The British Pilgrimage Trust describes St Baldred’s Well at East Linton as one of the best surviving local relics of his veneration, set beside a wider cluster of churches, chapels and hermit traditions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.britishpilgrimage.org/places/st-baldreds-well-east-linton" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: britishpilgrimage.org">[British Pilgrimage Trust]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">britishpilgrimage.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">British Pilgrimage Trust St Baldred’s Well, East Linton</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">British Pilgrimage Trust St Baldred’s Well, East Linton</span></span></span>

The saint’s folklore has a distinctive local motif: multiple holy bodies. An information panel at Prestonkirk, as reported by the British Pilgrimage Trust, preserves the story that when Baldred died, his body miraculously became three identical corpses so that Prestonkirk, Tyninghame and Auldhame could each possess his relics. A more conventional tradition says his remains were found at Tyninghame in the 11th century and translated to Durham. The contrast is useful: one version is miracle legend, the other ecclesiastical history, and both show how fiercely local churches valued saintly presence.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.britishpilgrimage.org/places/st-baldreds-well-east-linton" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: britishpilgrimage.org">[British Pilgrimage Trust]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">britishpilgrimage.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">British Pilgrimage Trust St Baldred’s Well, East Linton</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">British Pilgrimage Trust St Baldred’s Well, East Linton</span></span></span>

Bass Rock gives the tradition its most dramatic setting. The island’s ruined chapel is associated with Baldred’s hermitage, and later structures marked the sacred memory of the site. The rock’s physical presence — sheer, white with seabirds, offshore but visible from the mainland — makes it easy to understand why it became more than a landmark. It is a natural hermit island, a prison island in later history, and a sacred silhouette in the East Lothian seascape.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.britishpilgrimage.org/places/st-baldreds-well-east-linton" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: britishpilgrimage.org">[British Pilgrimage Trust]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">britishpilgrimage.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">British Pilgrimage Trust St Baldred’s Well, East Linton</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">British Pilgrimage Trust St Baldred’s Well, East Linton</span></span></span>

Whitekirk adds another layer. It became an important late-medieval pilgrimage centre, linked especially with the Virgin Mary and a holy well. The future Pope Pius II is said to have visited Whitekirk on pilgrimage in 1435 after surviving a storm at sea. Here again, East Lothian folklore is tied to movement: pilgrims, sailors, hermits, relics and coastal routes rather than isolated fireside tales.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.britishpilgrimage.org/places/st-baldreds-well-east-linton" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: britishpilgrimage.org">[British Pilgrimage Trust]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">britishpilgrimage.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">British Pilgrimage Trust St Baldred’s Well, East Linton</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">British Pilgrimage Trust St Baldred’s Well, East Linton</span></span></span>

## Traprain Law and the mythic pull of ancient places

Traprain Law is not a fairy hill in the simple storybook sense, but it is one of East Lothian’s most powerful mythic landscapes. The hill rises sharply out of the agricultural plain east of Haddington, and its shape makes it visible and memorable from far around. The Atlas of Hillforts describes it as a distinctive landmark whose fortifications rank among the largest in Scotland, enclosing about 16 hectares at their greatest extent.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://hillforts.arch.ox.ac.uk/records/SC3932.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: hillforts.arch.ox.ac.uk">[Atlas of Hillforts]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">hillforts.arch.ox.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on ox.ac.uk.</span></span></span>

Archaeologically, Traprain Law is complex rather than merely picturesque. The Traprain Law Environs Project investigated settlement across the surrounding East Lothian coastal plain, with evidence spanning from the fourth millennium BC to the early historic period. Around the hillfort, excavated enclosures showed repeated use, abandonment, re-use, burial and settlement over long periods.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://books.socantscot.org/digital-books/catalog/book/14" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: books.socantscot.org">[books.socantscot.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">books.socantscot.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Traprain Law Environs Project: Fieldwork and Excavations 2000–2004 &#124; Open Access E-Books...</span></span></span>

For folklore readers, the key point is not to invent unsupported tales, but to see why such a hill gathers legend. A place used and reused for thousands of years becomes a focus for memory even when precise stories are lost. Its association with ancient peoples, Roman-era contact, hillfort ramparts and the famous Traprain Law Treasure gives the site a “deep time” quality. In a county where many legends are well documented, Traprain represents the opposite: a powerful place where archaeology is strong but named oral tales are less secure.

This distinction matters. Not every ancient site needs a neat fairy or giant story to be folklorically important. Sometimes the folklore value lies in how later communities look at a conspicuous hill and sense that it belongs to an older world.

## Customs, boundaries and living tradition

Folklore is not only supernatural. East Lothian’s civic customs show how local identity is performed in public. Haddington’s Riding of the Marches belongs to the wider Scottish tradition of riding or walking boundaries, a custom linked to checking burgh lands and remembering common rights. A University of Edinburgh thesis summary describes Riding of the Marches and Common Riding festivals as ceremonial processions, often on horseback, around burgh boundaries to mark the area and check encroachment.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://era.ed.ac.uk/handle/1842/21633" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: era.ed.ac.uk">[ERA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">era.ed.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on ed.ac.uk.</span></span></span>

The John Gray Centre’s discussion of Haddington’s Riding of the Marches notes that such marches are believed to have begun partly as a religious pilgrimage following a route of crosses, later reshaped after the Reformation and through civic practice. That makes the custom a bridge between sacred landscape and local government, between old parish routes and modern community celebration.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.johngraycentre.org/oor-ain-ridingofthemarches/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: johngraycentre.org">[John Gray Centre]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">johngraycentre.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">oor ain ridingofthemarches</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">oor ain ridingofthemarches</span></span></span>

This type of tradition is quieter than a witch trial or goblin hall, but it may be more “living” in the everyday sense. It says that folklore can be a route, a procession, a set of roles, a remembered boundary and a yearly assertion that a town still knows where it belongs.

## Old tradition, literary invention and modern retelling

East Lothian’s folklore is best read in layers. Some traditions have medieval roots, such as the Goblin Ha’ story preserved through the *Scotichronicon*. Some are early modern records of persecution, like North Berwick, Dirleton and Spott. Some are saintly legends preserved through church sites, wells and pilgrimage memory. Others are modern heritage retellings, songs, walking guides, museum displays and tourism pages that keep older material visible for new audiences.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://libraryblogs.is.ed.ac.uk/edinburghuniversityarchives/2020/10/30/the-scotichronicon-and-the-goblin-ha-witchcraft-and-hobgoblins-in-our-manuscripts/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: libraryblogs.is.ed.ac.uk">[ed.ac.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">libraryblogs.is.ed.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on ed.ac.uk.</span></span></span>

The John Gray Centre in Haddington is important here because it functions as East Lothian’s archive, local history and museum hub. The Scottish Council on Archives describes its collections as including manuscripts, minute books, images, criminal registers, poor law records, newspaper indexes and other material relating to many aspects of East Lothian’s history. For folklore, such institutions matter because they help separate a traceable record from a repeated rumour while still preserving the stories people care about.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.scottisharchives.org.uk/archives-map/east-lothian-council-archives-local-history-centre/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: scottisharchives.org.uk">[Scottish Council on Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">scottisharchives.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on scottisharchives.org.uk.</span></span></span>

Tourism also shapes modern folklore. Visit East Lothian presents the area as rich in stories of battles, ghosts, witches and folklore, and walking pages now send visitors to places like Yester Castle and the Goblin Ha’. That does not invalidate the traditions, but it changes their use. A warning tale, court record or saintly legend becomes part of a visitor route, a local identity marker and a shareable story.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://visiteastlothian.org/east-lothian-stories/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: visiteastlothian.org">[Visit East Lothian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">visiteastlothian.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on visiteastlothian.org.</span></span></span>

The most responsible way to enjoy East Lothian folklore is therefore not to flatten it into either “true history” or “just myth”. The county’s stories work because they sit between the two. Goblins explain a chamber that really exists. Witch legends grow out of real injustice. Saints attach holiness to wells, rocks and ruined chapels. Boundary customs turn old rights into public ritual. East Lothian’s folklore is a map of how people have made meaning from a compact but story-rich landscape.

## Why East Lothian matters in the wider UK folklore map

East Lothian is a strong county page because it connects several major UK folklore themes within a small area. Yester belongs with magical builders, wizard-lords and haunted castle undercrofts. North Berwick belongs with witch-trial folklore, sea magic and printed demonology. Dirleton and Spott belong with the memorialisation of accused witches and the modern correction of older local legends. St Baldred belongs with holy wells, saintly relics, coastal pilgrimage and hermit islands. Traprain Law belongs with ancient hillfort landscapes whose physical presence outlasts clear oral tradition.

It also shows why county-level folklore is useful. National summaries of Scottish folklore often jump from kelpies and selkies to Highland fairies and famous monsters. East Lothian asks different questions: how did a Lowland farming and coastal county remember witches? How did a medieval noble become a wizard? How did an island rock become a saint’s retreat? How did civic boundary riding become a living custom? Those questions make the county feel specific rather than generic.

The result is a folklore landscape that is lively without needing exaggeration. East Lothian’s strongest stories are already strange enough: a goblin-built hall below a ruined castle, a royal witch panic at North Berwick, a black dog in a Dirleton confession, a contested witch stone at Spott, a saint whose body multiplied for rival churches, and an ancient hillfort that still dominates the plain. Each belongs to the county not because it is merely spooky, but because it attaches story to place with unusual force.
