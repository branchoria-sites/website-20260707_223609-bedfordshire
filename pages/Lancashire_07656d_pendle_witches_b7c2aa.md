## Introduction

Pendle became [Lancashire]({{ 'where-lancashire-folklore-still-haunts/' | relative_url }})’s witch country because a real prosecution in 1612 left behind unusually vivid names, places and accusations, then later writers, museums, walking routes and local memory turned that legal tragedy into the county’s most famous supernatural landscape. The story begins around Pendle Hill, with poor families, village healers, local quarrels and a magistrate’s investigation; it reaches its deadly centre at Lancaster Castle, where most of the accused were tried in August 1612; and it survives because Thomas Potts, clerk of the court, published a detailed but deeply one-sided account in 1613.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.lancastercastle.com/history-heritage/further-articles/the-pendle-witches/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: lancastercastle.com">[Lancaster Castle]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">lancastercastle.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Lancaster Castle The Pendle Witches &#124; Lancaster Castle: Lancaster Castle</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Lancaster Castle The Pendle Witches &#124; Lancaster Castle: Lancaster Castle</span></span></span>



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/Lancashire_07656d_pendle_witches_b7c2aa-overview.webp" | relative_url }}" alt="Overview image for Pendle Witches" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

For folklore readers, the Pendle witches are not simply “a spooky legend”. They sit at the crossing point between early modern law, local belief, hostile testimony, family fear, printed propaganda, Gothic fiction and modern heritage tourism. That is why Pendle matters so strongly within Lancashire identity: the hill, the villages below it, the road to Lancaster and the castle court all form a map where history and legend keep being retold together.

## The 1612 trials at Lancaster Castle

The Pendle case began not with a coven in the modern fantasy sense, but with a small roadside encounter. In March 1612, Alizon Device met John Law, a pedlar from Halifax, on the road near Colne and asked him for pins. After a quarrel, Law became seriously ill; in Potts’s account, Alizon connected this illness with a familiar spirit in the shape of a dog. Modern summaries often read Law’s sudden collapse as medically plausible without any magic at all, but in 1612 the incident was interpreted through a world where harmful witchcraft was a criminal offence and where neighbours might already suspect certain poor, marginal or “cunning” people of dangerous powers.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.lancastercastle.com/history-heritage/further-articles/the-pendle-witches/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: lancastercastle.com">[Lancaster Castle]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">lancastercastle.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Lancaster Castle The Pendle Witches &#124; Lancaster Castle: Lancaster Castle</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Lancaster Castle The Pendle Witches &#124; Lancaster Castle: Lancaster Castle</span></span></span>

The local justice of the peace, Roger Nowell of Read Hall near Burnley, investigated. His questioning drew in two interlinked and rival family groups around Pendle: the Devices, connected with Elizabeth Southerns, known as Old Demdike, and the Whittles, connected with Anne Whittle, known as Chattox. Visit Lancashire’s chronology places the first interrogations between 30 March and 2 April 1612, when Demdike, Alizon Device, Chattox and Anne Redfearn were sent to Lancaster Castle to await trial.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.visitlancashire.com/the-lancashire-witches" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: visitlancashire.com">[Visit Lancashire]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">visitlancashire.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Visit Lancashire The Lancashire Witches &#124; Visit Lancashire</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Visit Lancashire The Lancashire Witches &#124; Visit Lancashire</span></span></span>

The case widened sharply after Good Friday, 10 April 1612. A gathering at Malkin Tower, the Device family home, was described by accusers as a witches’ meeting. Visit Lancashire calls it the first British allegation of a witches’ sabbat, and notes that on 27 April James Device and the young Jennet Device gave information about the gathering. Jennet’s role is one of the most disturbing parts of the story: a child’s evidence helped condemn members of her own family and neighbours.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.visitlancashire.com/the-lancashire-witches" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: visitlancashire.com">[Visit Lancashire]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">visitlancashire.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Visit Lancashire The Lancashire Witches &#124; Visit Lancashire</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Visit Lancashire The Lancashire Witches &#124; Visit Lancashire</span></span></span>

Most of the Pendle accused were tried at Lancaster Castle on 18 and 19 August 1612, alongside the Samlesbury accused and others from Lancashire. Lancaster Castle identifies Judge Bromley and Judge Altham as presiding judges, Roger Nowell as prosecutor, and Thomas Potts as clerk of the court. The same castle account makes clear how far the trial was from a modern fair hearing: the prisoners had no defence counsel to plead for them and could not call witnesses on their own behalf.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.lancastercastle.com/history-heritage/a-dark-history/lancashire-witch-trials/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: lancastercastle.com">[Lancaster Castle]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">lancastercastle.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Lancaster Castle Lancashire Witch Trials &#124; Lancaster Castle: Lancaster Castle</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Lancaster Castle Lancashire Witch Trials &#124; Lancaster Castle: Lancaster Castle</span></span></span>

The outcomes were brutal. Old Demdike died in the dungeon before trial. Jennet Preston had already been tried at York Castle on 27 July and found guilty. At Lancaster, Anne Whittle, Elizabeth Device, James Device, Anne Redferne, Alice Nutter, Katherine Hewitt, Jane Bulcock, John Bulcock, Alizon Device and Isobel Robey were found guilty and sentenced to death; Margaret Pearson escaped execution but was sentenced to public punishment and imprisonment. On 20 August 1612, ten condemned people were hanged on the moors above Lancaster.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.lancastercastle.com/history-heritage/further-articles/the-pendle-witches/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: lancastercastle.com">[Lancaster Castle]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">lancastercastle.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Lancaster Castle The Pendle Witches &#124; Lancaster Castle: Lancaster Castle</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Lancaster Castle The Pendle Witches &#124; Lancaster Castle: Lancaster Castle</span></span></span>

What makes the Pendle trials so memorable is not that they were typical. They were unusually well documented, unusually large for England, and unusually easy to attach to a landscape. Pendle Hill gives the story a looming landmark; Lancaster Castle gives it a courtroom and prison; Malkin Tower gives it a legendary meeting place; and the route between Pendle and Lancaster turns prosecution into pilgrimage.

## Thomas Potts and the printed witch story

Almost everything most readers know about the Pendle witches comes through Thomas Potts’s book, *The Wonderfull Discoverie of Witches in the Countie of Lancaster*, published in 1613. Project Gutenberg describes it as an account of the Lancashire witch trials of 1612, ordered by the trial judges, and notes that twenty men and women faced accusations, with eleven condemned to hang.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/18253" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Project GutenbergDiscovery of Witches by active 1612-1618 Thomas Potts &#124; Project Gutenberg...</span></span></span>

That makes Potts indispensable, but it also makes him dangerous as a source. He was not a neutral folklorist collecting village tradition, and he was not writing a balanced modern court transcript. Lancaster Castle’s own account calls his version “very full” but “biased”, and stresses that Potts was writing for an audience ready to believe in witchcraft, including King James I.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.lancastercastle.com/history-heritage/further-articles/the-pendle-witches/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: lancastercastle.com">[Lancaster Castle]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">lancastercastle.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Lancaster Castle The Pendle Witches &#124; Lancaster Castle: Lancaster Castle</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Lancaster Castle The Pendle Witches &#124; Lancaster Castle: Lancaster Castle</span></span></span>

The politics of the book matter. The Gunpowder Plot of 1605 was still a recent memory, anti-Catholic suspicion was strong, and Potts dedicated his work to Lord Knyvett, the man associated with the arrest of Guy Fawkes. In that climate, a Lancashire witch case could be presented not merely as village malice or courtroom tragedy, but as proof that vigilant Protestant authority was uncovering hidden evil.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.lancastercastle.com/history-heritage/further-articles/the-pendle-witches/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: lancastercastle.com">[Lancaster Castle]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">lancastercastle.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Lancaster Castle The Pendle Witches &#124; Lancaster Castle: Lancaster Castle</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Lancaster Castle The Pendle Witches &#124; Lancaster Castle: Lancaster Castle</span></span></span>

For folklore, Potts’s account has a double afterlife. On one hand, it preserved details that might otherwise have vanished: names, accusations, familiars, supposed meetings, quarrels over food or goods, and the frightening logic by which everyday misfortunes became evidence of witchcraft. On the other hand, it froze those details in the language of the prosecution. The “witch story” that later readers inherited was already shaped by power: magistrates, judges, clerks and hostile witnesses had more control over the page than the accused.

This is why Pendle should be read as both history and legend, but not as simple oral tradition. The story contains elements familiar from wider witch belief: animal familiars, harmful magic, clay images, beggars’ curses, old women feared by neighbours, and a gathering reimagined as a sabbat. Yet the version that survived most strongly is a court-produced narrative, not a fireside tale passed down untouched from the villages under the hill.

## Why Pendle attached itself to Lancashire memory

Pendle’s power as a Lancashire legend comes from the way the case joins named people to a recognisable local geography. The accused were not distant fairytale figures. They were associated with farms, hamlets, tracks and parishes around Pendle Hill, then carried into the county town’s legal machinery at Lancaster. Visit Lancashire’s present-day trail still follows that broad movement, from Pendle towards Lancaster Castle, explicitly inviting visitors to piece together a mystery more than 400 years old.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.visitlancashire.com/business-directory/pendle-witch-trail-lancaster-to-pendle" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: visitlancashire.com">[Visit Lancashire]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">visitlancashire.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Visit Lancashire Pendle Witch Trail, Lancaster to Pendle &#124; Visit Lancashire</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Visit Lancashire Pendle Witch Trail, Lancaster to Pendle &#124; Visit Lancashire</span></span></span>

The landscape also helps explain why the story feels older than the documents. Pendle Forest was not a dense modern woodland but an old hunting and upland district of scattered settlements, difficult roads and strong local ties. Lancaster Castle’s account describes early seventeenth-century Pendle as remote, with poor roads, infertile valleys and scattered hamlets. That does not prove the accusations were true; it helps explain why suspicion, reputation and local grievance could travel in particular ways.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.lancastercastle.com/history-heritage/further-articles/the-pendle-witches/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: lancastercastle.com">[Lancaster Castle]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">lancastercastle.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Lancaster Castle The Pendle Witches &#124; Lancaster Castle: Lancaster Castle</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Lancaster Castle The Pendle Witches &#124; Lancaster Castle: Lancaster Castle</span></span></span>

The accused were not all socially alike. Alice Nutter, for example, has become one of the best-known Pendle figures partly because she seems to sit awkwardly within the stereotype of the poor old witch. Roughlee Parish Council describes her as comparatively wealthy, the widow of a tenant yeoman farmer, and notes that she made no statement except to plead not guilty. Her silence has left later writers and artists room to imagine dignity, resistance, fear or political entanglement.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.roughlee.org.uk/roughlee-commemorates-alice-nutter/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: roughlee.org.uk">[Roughlee Village]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">roughlee.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Roughlee Village Alice Nutter</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Roughlee Village Alice Nutter</span></span></span>

That is one reason Pendle has become such a strong local memory site. Different audiences find different meanings in the same story. For some, it is a warning about misogyny, poverty, disability, age and legal cruelty. For others, it is part of Lancashire’s dark tourism economy. For modern witches and Pagans, it can be a story of persecution and memorialisation, though the historical accused should not simply be recast as members of modern religious witchcraft. For local heritage, it is a way to talk about landscape, injustice and identity together.

The story also links naturally to other Lancashire folklore. The familiar in dog form sits beside northern black-dog traditions, though it belongs to a courtroom demonology rather than a roadside ghost tale. The cunning-woman and healer background connects Pendle to charms, folk medicine and household magic. The route to Lancaster links upland villages to the county’s legal and prison history. Pendle is therefore not an isolated “witch attraction”; it is a hub where several Lancashire story-worlds meet.

## From Gothic fiction to modern memory

The Pendle witches did not remain fixed in Potts’s seventeenth-century prose. In the nineteenth century, William Harrison Ainsworth’s *The Lancashire Witches: A Romance of Pendle Forest* gave the trials a powerful Gothic afterlife. Project Gutenberg identifies the novel as published in 1849 and based on the true story of the Pendle witches executed in 1612; it folds the witch accusations into a dramatic historical romance of religion, curse, landscape and family fate.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/15493" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on gutenberg.org.</span></span></span>

Ainsworth’s version matters because many later readers met Pendle through atmosphere before archive. Gothic fiction made the hill, forest, old houses and accused women feel dramatically charged. It helped turn a legal record into a legendary county landscape. But it also blurred lines: invented scenes, heightened villainy, supernatural colouring and romantic plotting could easily be mistaken for inherited local tradition.

Pendle’s afterlife also includes stage and popular entertainment. The later Lancashire witch scare of 1633–34 inspired Thomas Heywood and Richard Brome’s play *The Late Lancashire Witches*. The Richard Brome Online edition notes how the play treats witchcraft through comedy, spectacle and scepticism, placing supposed Lancashire witchcraft at the border between gullible belief and theatrical entertainment.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.dhi.ac.uk/brome/viewInFrame.jsp?act=1&amp;play=LW&amp;type=CRIT&amp;view=print" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: dhi.ac.uk">[DHI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">dhi.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">DHIRichard Brome Online</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">DHIRichard Brome Online</span></span></span>

In the twenty-first century, the afterlife is more visible in heritage trails, sculpture, museums, guided walks and souvenirs. Pendle Heritage Centre in Barrowford presents the Pendle witches as part of a wider local history offer, alongside vernacular buildings, Park Hill farmstead, George Fox and the Quakers, and the landscape of Pendle Forest.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.pendleheritage.co.uk/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pendleheritage.co.uk">[pendleheritage.co.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pendleheritage.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Pendle Heritage Centre – Museum, Tea Room, 18th Century Walled Garden</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Pendle Heritage Centre – Museum, Tea Room, 18th Century Walled Garden</span></span></span>

The Pendle Witch Trail is a self-guided 45-mile car, minibus or bike route between Lancaster and Pendle. Its visitor description emphasises villages below Pendle Hill, the road through the Ribble Valley, and Lancaster Castle, showing how modern retelling turns a chain of legal events into a mapped experience.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.visitlancashire.com/business-directory/pendle-witch-trail-lancaster-to-pendle" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: visitlancashire.com">[Visit Lancashire]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">visitlancashire.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Visit Lancashire Pendle Witch Trail, Lancaster to Pendle &#124; Visit Lancashire</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Visit Lancashire Pendle Witch Trail, Lancaster to Pendle &#124; Visit Lancashire</span></span></span>

Public art has also shifted the tone from spectacle to commemoration. In Roughlee, the Alice Nutter statue was erected in 2012 for the 400th anniversary of the trials. Roughlee Parish Council says the aim was to commemorate Alice’s leaving for Lancaster and raise awareness of the “true story” of the witches; the Public Statues and Sculpture Association describes the statue as showing her in chains, stepping as if towards a scaffold.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.roughlee.org.uk/roughlee-commemorates-alice-nutter/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: roughlee.org.uk">[Roughlee Village]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">roughlee.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Roughlee Village Alice Nutter</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Roughlee Village Alice Nutter</span></span></span>

This modern memory is not free of tension. ITV Granada reported in 2024 that the Pendle witches remain a source of “dark tourism”, with Lancaster Castle, ghost walks, museums, independent shops and witch-themed goods all forming part of a local visitor economy. That tourism keeps the story alive, but it also risks making executed people into branding. The strongest modern interpretation tries to hold both facts together: Pendle is a compelling visitor landscape, and it is also the site-memory of people condemned through fear, gossip, pressure and law.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.itv.com/news/granada/2024-04-23/the-infamous-pendle-witches-are-still-a-rich-source-of-dark-tourism" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: itv.com">[ITVX]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">itv.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on itv.com.</span></span></span>

## How to read the Pendle witches today

The most useful way to read the Pendle story is in layers. The first layer is the historical event: accusations, examinations, imprisonment, trial and execution in 1612. The second is the printed prosecution narrative created by Potts. The third is the literary and theatrical afterlife, especially Gothic and romantic retellings. The fourth is the modern heritage landscape of trails, statues, museums and tourism.

Those layers should not be flattened into one simple legend. A familiar spirit in the shape of a dog belongs to early modern witch belief and courtroom testimony. The Pendle “witch country” of gift shops and guided trails belongs to modern heritage culture. Ainsworth’s dramatic scenes belong to historical fiction. Alice Nutter’s statue belongs to memorial art. All are part of Pendle’s afterlife, but they do not carry the same kind of evidence.

The Pendle witches matter within Lancashire because they show how folklore can grow from real injustice without ceasing to be folklore. The story has named victims, a court record, a castle, a hill and a route across the county. It also has demons, dogs, curses, sabbats, Gothic romance, ghost walks and souvenirs. Its power lies in that uneasy mixture: Pendle is remembered as witch country not because the supernatural claims are proven, but because Lancashire has spent four centuries retelling a terrifying human event in supernatural language.
