## Introduction

[Denbighshire]({{ 'where-denbighshires-legends-still/' | relative_url }})’s ghost stories are often found at crossings because these are the places where ordinary life feels most exposed: bridges over rivers, churchyard edges, rectory rooms, road bends, wells after sunset and the dark approach to a parish church. The best-attested examples are not random “hauntings” in the modern paranormal sense, but local traditions about boundaries between safety and danger, the living and the dead, the parish and the wider night. Elias Owen’s *Welsh Folk-Lore*, published in 1896 after winning the 1887 National Eisteddfod prize, is the key source for several of them, including the Llandegla spirit and the Angelystor of Llangernyw. The stories matter because they show how historic Denbighshire imagined haunted places as practical landmarks: the bridge children must watch, the church window where names are heard, the road where an unburied or wrongly buried dead person might still trouble travellers.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.library.wales/discover-learn/digital-exhibitions/books/history-books/welsh-folk-lore-a-collection-of-the-folk-tales-and-legends-of-north-wales" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: library.wales">[library.wales]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">library.wales</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on library.wales.</span></span></span>

The geography needs one caution. This page follows historic Denbighshire, so it includes places such as Llangernyw, now in Conwy County Borough but historically treated as Denbighshire in county folklore. Wikishire describes Llangernyw as a rural village and parish in Denbighshire, while also showing its present local government under Conwy; that split is exactly why older folklore has to be read through historic county geography rather than only modern council boundaries.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://wikishire.co.uk/wiki/Llangernyw" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wikishire.co.uk">[Wikishire]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wikishire.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Wikishire Llangernyw</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wikishire Llangernyw</span></span></span>

## The trapped Llandegla spirit beneath the bridge

The strongest Denbighshire bridge-ghost story is the Llandegla spirit, preserved by Elias Owen from Mr Roberts, the former schoolmaster of Llandegla, who had received it from the old parish clerk, John Jones the weaver. Owen places the haunting at the old rectory, where a troublesome spirit made peace impossible until Griffiths of Graianrhyd was called in to “lay” it. The spirit evaded him by appearing in forms he could not approach, until it finally took the shape of a fly. Griffiths captured it, shut it in a small box and buried the box under a large stone in the river below the bridge near Llandegla Mills.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/files/20096/20096-h/20096-h.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Project Gutenberg Welsh Folk-Lore</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Project Gutenberg Welsh Folk-Lore</span></span></span>

What makes the story memorable is the condition attached to the ghost’s imprisonment. The spirit was to remain trapped only until a tree growing by the bridge reached the height of the parapet. To prevent that release, Owen says schoolchildren still nipped the upper branches and slowed its growth. This turns the legend into a tiny piece of community maintenance: the children are not just hearing a frightening tale, but acting inside it, keeping the danger contained by controlling a living marker beside the bridge.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/files/20096/20096-h/20096-h.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Project Gutenberg Welsh Folk-Lore</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Project Gutenberg Welsh Folk-Lore</span></span></span>

The physical setting helps explain why the story works. Cadw’s listing for the Bridge at Llandegla identifies it as a Grade II bridge over the River Alyn on the minor road leading west past the church, and describes a small mid-nineteenth-century road bridge forming part of a Victorian group with the church and school. That does not prove the ghost story’s events, but it does confirm the kind of village crossing Owen’s tale depends on: church, school, road, river and bridge close enough together for the story to become a shared local map.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cadwpublic-api.azurewebsites.net/reports/listedbuilding/FullReport?id=19307" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cadwpublic-api.azurewebsites.net">[Cadw Public API]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cadwpublic-api.azurewebsites.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Cadw Public APIListed Buildings</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cadw Public APIListed Buildings</span></span></span>

Modern retellings have kept the same structure while sharpening the “poltergeist” flavour. Curious Clwyd, drawing on local Llandegla material and Owen, retells the old rectory haunting as a noisy disturbance of smashing crockery and thrown cutlery, with Reverend Griffiths finally catching the spirit as a bluebottle and taking it to the bridge by the River Alyn. It also notes that the child-pruning tradition continued through the nineteenth century, as Owen had testified, while cautiously suggesting that today’s taller bridge-side trees make the old condition feel unresolved rather than settled.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.mythslegendsodditiesnorth-east-wales.co.uk/reverend-griffiths" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: mythslegendsodditiesnorth-east-wales.co.uk">[Curious Clwyd]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">mythslegendsodditiesnorth-east-wales.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Curious Clwyd Reverend Griffiths &#124; Curious Clwyd</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Curious Clwyd Reverend Griffiths &#124; Curious Clwyd</span></span></span>

## Why the bridge is more than scenery

The Llandegla legend belongs to a wider Welsh pattern in which a dangerous spirit is captured in a tiny form and confined in water. Owen himself compares such stories across North Wales: evil spirits trouble churches or houses, are overcome by force or trickery, and are deposited in a river, pool or lake so that quiet returns. In the Llandegla case, the bridge is not a decorative backdrop. It is the place where a domestic haunting is removed from the rectory and locked into the landscape.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/files/20096/20096-h/20096-h.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Project Gutenberg Welsh Folk-Lore</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Project Gutenberg Welsh Folk-Lore</span></span></span>

That matters because a bridge is a daily threshold. People cross it for church, school, work, market and travel; children know it intimately; the river below it is close enough to be ordinary and strange enough to hide things. The story makes the crossing feel watched. Anyone passing the tree is reminded that the village’s peace depends on a boundary being kept: box under stone, stone under water, tree below parapet.

Llandegla itself was already a route-centred place. Thomas Pennant’s eighteenth-century account, revisited by the Curious Travellers project, describes Llandegla as known for large fairs for black cattle, while later commentary notes its drovers’ roads and traffic towards English markets. The ghost under the bridge therefore sits naturally in a parish shaped by movement: cattle, traders, clergy, schoolchildren, worshippers and travellers all passing through a landscape where a particular crossing could gather story after story.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://curioustravellers.ac.uk/pennant-and-llandegla/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: curioustravellers.ac.uk">[Curious Travellers]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">curioustravellers.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">pennant and llandegla</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">pennant and llandegla</span></span></span>

## Churchyards, rectories and spectral warning places

Denbighshire’s churchyard ghosts are less about “haunted ruins” than about warning. The clearest example is the Angelystor of Llangernyw. Owen records a tradition that on All-Hallows’ Eve a spirit announced from the altar the names of those doomed to die in the coming year. Those anxious to know their fate stood beneath the east window and listened. In the tale’s sharpest version, a sceptical tailor, Shon Robert, goes to test the story and hears his own name called. His protest that he is not ready makes no difference; he dies within the year.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/files/20096/20096-h/20096-h.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Project Gutenberg Welsh Folk-Lore</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Project Gutenberg Welsh Folk-Lore</span></span></span>

The setting is crucial. The voice comes from inside the church, but listeners stand outside, under the east window. The tradition therefore depends on a precise threshold: not fully in the sacred building, not safely away from it, but pressed against the boundary between church interior, churchyard and night. Owen places this Llangernyw story immediately after a wider discussion of Welsh beliefs about hearing death omens at church porches on All-Hallows’ Eve, which suggests that the Denbighshire version is local but not isolated.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/files/20096/20096-h/20096-h.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Project Gutenberg Welsh Folk-Lore</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Project Gutenberg Welsh Folk-Lore</span></span></span>

Owen also records beliefs about the north side of churchyards and the so-called Devil’s Door. He says spirits were thought to leave by the north door when the priest entered by the south, and that the north side of churchyards was formerly associated with demons and avoided for burial, though by Owen’s day practical burial needs had changed that pattern. Whether or not every detail was still believed in late Victorian Denbighshire, the passage helps explain why churchyard geography mattered in folklore: doors, windows, sides and paths were not neutral space, but moral and spiritual boundaries.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/files/20096/20096-h/20096-h.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Project Gutenberg Welsh Folk-Lore</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Project Gutenberg Welsh Folk-Lore</span></span></span>

Llangernyw also has one of the most powerful physical anchors for such a story: the ancient yew in St Digain’s churchyard. The Woodland Trust’s Ancient Tree Inventory describes the Llangernyw Yew as possibly over 4,000 years old and one of the oldest living things in Wales, while also placing it in the grounds of St Dygain’s Church. Even with the usual caution that yews are hard to date, the tree gives the Angelystor tradition a striking material focus: a churchyard already marked by age, death, continuity and public memory.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ati.woodlandtrust.org.uk/tree-search/tree?treeid=5126" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ati.woodlandtrust.org.uk">[Ancient Tree Inventory]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ati.woodlandtrust.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Ancient Tree Inventory Tree</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Ancient Tree Inventory Tree</span></span></span>

## Llandegla’s well shows another kind of threshold

Although this page is about ghosts and haunted thresholds, Llandegla’s St Tegla’s Well helps explain the same local grammar of boundary places. The well stands close to the River Alyn and church route rather than in a remote fantasy landscape. Archaeologist Howard Williams describes walking from Llandegla church down to the River Alyn, over the bridge, then along the river path to the footbridge and St Tegla’s Well on the eastern bank. That route links church, bridge, river, footpath and well in a single ritual landscape.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://howardwilliamsblog.wordpress.com/2015/04/12/holy-water-a-chicken-and-a-bible-pillow-the-folklore-of-st-teglas-well-llandegla/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: howardwilliamsblog.wordpress.com">[Archaeo]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">howardwilliamsblog.wordpress.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wordpress.com.</span></span></span>

The traditional cure associated with the well was not a ghost story, but it worked through night, repetition and crossing from one state to another. Williams summarises the heritage-board account: the sufferer visited after sunset, washed at the well, made an offering, walked around the well and church three times, carried a chicken, recited the Lord’s Prayer, then spent the night inside the church lying beneath the communion table with a Bible as a pillow. If the chicken died, the sufferer was believed to be cured of epilepsy, locally associated with St Tegla’s disease.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://howardwilliamsblog.wordpress.com/2015/04/12/holy-water-a-chicken-and-a-bible-pillow-the-folklore-of-st-teglas-well-llandegla/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: howardwilliamsblog.wordpress.com">[Archaeo]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">howardwilliamsblog.wordpress.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wordpress.com.</span></span></span>

This ritual matters for Denbighshire ghost lore because it shows that parish thresholds were not only feared. They were also used. The same kind of place that could contain a spirit beneath a bridge or speak the names of the soon-to-die from a church could also offer healing through a carefully staged passage: sunset to night, outside to inside, water to church, illness to hoped-for cure. The logic is not modern medicine or modern ghost-hunting. It is folk practice built around places where change feels possible.

## Roadside ghosts and uneasy burial

A later Llandegla road story shows the same threshold pattern in a more modern form. Curious Clwyd records a ghost seen near the B5104 close to Llandegla, where nineteenth-century travellers were said to find horses startled by an apparition or animals refusing to pass a particular spot. In the early twentieth century, a skeleton was reportedly found in a field by the road and interpreted as the body of a suicide or criminal who had not been buried in consecrated ground. Once the remains were reburied in a churchyard, the haunting was said to stop.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.mythslegendsodditiesnorth-east-wales.co.uk/llandegla-ghosts" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: mythslegendsodditiesnorth-east-wales.co.uk">[Curious Clwyd]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">mythslegendsodditiesnorth-east-wales.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Curious Clwyd Llandegla Ghosts &#124; Curious Clwyd</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Curious Clwyd Llandegla Ghosts &#124; Curious Clwyd</span></span></span>

This is not as strongly attested as Owen’s Llandegla spirit, and it appears in a modern local-history retelling rather than a named Victorian collection. Still, it is useful because it shows how the same moral map survives: the road is disturbed because the dead are out of place; the solution is not investigation for its own sake, but proper burial within the churchyard. The frightening roadside apparition becomes a story about restoring order between the living community and the excluded dead.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.mythslegendsodditiesnorth-east-wales.co.uk/llandegla-ghosts" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: mythslegendsodditiesnorth-east-wales.co.uk">[Curious Clwyd]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">mythslegendsodditiesnorth-east-wales.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Curious Clwyd Llandegla Ghosts &#124; Curious Clwyd</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Curious Clwyd Llandegla Ghosts &#124; Curious Clwyd</span></span></span>

The motif also explains why animals appear so often in such accounts. Horses refusing to pass a place are not treated simply as frightened livestock; they become witnesses to a hidden disorder in the landscape. In a county of roads, fairs, drovers and upland journeys, an animal stopping at a particular bend or crossing gives the story a practical test: the route itself seems to know where danger lies.

## How thresholds make these stories memorable

Denbighshire’s ghost places endure because they attach supernatural danger to exact, repeatable actions. A person crosses a bridge, waits at a window, walks round a well, sleeps in a church, passes a road bend, hears a bell or watches a tree grow. The stories are easy to remember because they are built into movement and routine rather than floating free as vague spooky atmosphere.

Three features recur across the strongest examples:

* **A named place with a visible trigger.** Llandegla has the bridge, river stone, tree and former rectory; Llangernyw has the church, east window and yew; the B5104 story has the road spot and later reburial.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/files/20096/20096-h/20096-h.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Project Gutenberg Welsh Folk-Lore</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Project Gutenberg Welsh Folk-Lore</span></span></span>
* **A rule that keeps danger contained.** The Llandegla tree must not reach the parapet; the Llangernyw listener must face what the voice names; the road ghost is quieted only when the bones are moved to consecrated ground.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/files/20096/20096-h/20096-h.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Project Gutenberg Welsh Folk-Lore</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Project Gutenberg Welsh Folk-Lore</span></span></span>
* **A community role.** Schoolchildren prune the tree, villagers listen for names, travellers and animals mark the haunted road, clergy or cunning figures intervene when a spirit must be laid. These are social stories, not private visions.

The evidence also varies in strength. Owen’s accounts are Victorian antiquarian folklore collected through named local intermediaries, valuable but still shaped by the assumptions and storytelling habits of his time. The National Library of Wales identifies him as a cleric and antiquary whose *Welsh folk-lore* was an 1887 National Eisteddfod prize essay and appeared as a single volume in 1896. Later websites, walking pages and tourism retellings keep the stories alive, but they often simplify or dramatise them. The safest reading is to treat the tales as traditions about how Denbighshire communities imagined dangerous boundaries, not as proof of supernatural events.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.library.wales/discover-learn/digital-exhibitions/books/history-books/welsh-folk-lore-a-collection-of-the-folk-tales-and-legends-of-north-wales" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: library.wales">[library.wales]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">library.wales</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on library.wales.</span></span></span>

## Why these ghost places belong to Denbighshire

The ghost traditions of Denbighshire are distinctive because they sit at the meeting point of parish Christianity, upland travel and older local storytelling. They do not need ruined castles, theatrical séances or elaborate monsters to work. A rectory room, a church window, a river bridge or a road outside the village is enough. The uncanny enters through familiar infrastructure.

That is why Llandegla is so central to this page. In one village, the evidence clusters around the rectory, River Alyn bridge, St Tegla’s Well, church and road. The Church in Wales still lists St Tecla, Llandegla, within the Valle Crucis Mission Area and the Diocese of St Asaph, while Cadw’s bridge listing places the bridge on the road past the church. The official records are not folklore sources in themselves, but they help show why the legends feel so spatially coherent: the sacred, domestic and transport landscapes are close together.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.churchinwales.org.uk/en/structure/church/1587/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: churchinwales.org.uk">[Church in Wales]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">churchinwales.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">St Tecla, Llandegla - The Church in Wales...</span></span></span>

Llangernyw adds a second pattern: the churchyard as a place where death is not merely buried but announced. Its Angelystor story turns the parish into an annual roll-call of mortality, heard at the edge of the church on a night already associated with the dead. The ancient yew then gives modern visitors a visible focus for the tradition, which is why the legend still circulates in travel writing and folklore retellings even when historic county boundaries are no longer obvious to visitors.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/files/20096/20096-h/20096-h.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[gutenberg.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Project Gutenberg Welsh Folk-Lore</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Project Gutenberg Welsh Folk-Lore</span></span></span>

Across these cases, Denbighshire’s ghosts are best understood as threshold stories. They ask what happens when a boundary fails: a spirit escapes the house, the dead are not properly buried, the living try to overhear their fate, illness crosses into cure, or a tree threatens to release what the village has trapped. Their power lies not in proving the impossible, but in making ordinary places feel charged with memory, warning and responsibility.

    } else if (available.indexOf(defaultMarket)< 0) {
      marketId = available[0] || defaultMarket;
    }
    var select = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-select]');
    if (select) {
      select.addEventListener('change', function () {
        applyMarket(section, select.value, true);
      });
    }
    var trigger = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-trigger]');
    var menu = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-menu]');
    if (trigger && menu) {
      trigger.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
        event.stopPropagation();
        var expanded = trigger.getAttribute('aria-expanded') === 'true';
        menu.hidden = expanded;
        trigger.setAttribute('aria-expanded', expanded ? 'false' : 'true');
        var picker = trigger.closest('.fr-ebay-market-picker');
        if (picker) picker.classList.toggle('fr-ebay-market-picker--open', !expanded);
      });
      Array.prototype.slice.call(menu.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-option]')).forEach(function (button) {
        button.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
          event.stopPropagation();
          applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-option'), true);
        });
      });
      document.addEventListener('click', function () { closeMenu(section); });
    }
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      button.addEventListener('click', function () {
        applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button'), true);
      });
    });
    applyMarket(section, marketId, false);
  });
})();
