## Introduction

For this project, Selkirkshire should be treated as the historic county shown in the historic-counties mapping tradition rather than as a modern council district. Wikishire’s map uses Historic Counties Standard border data, while Wikimedia Commons also hosts a specific Selkirkshire location map derived from the British Isles historic counties SVG. That matters because the county’s folklore does not stop at today’s administrative labels: Carterhaugh, [Yarrow]({{ 'yarrow/' | relative_url }}), Ettrick, St Mary’s Loch and the old Forest all belong to a Border story-world that is older than the current Scottish Borders council map.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://wikishire.co.uk/map/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wikishire.co.uk">[wikishire.co.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wikishire.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Wikishire Great Britain and Ireland</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wikishire Great Britain and Ireland</span></span></span>

## Why Selkirkshire folklore feels different

Selkirkshire’s best-known traditions are unusually literary, but they are not merely invented literature. The county sits inside the old Border ballad zone, where oral songs, family memory, reiving history, fairy belief and antiquarian collecting were repeatedly reshaped by writers such as Sir Walter Scott and James Hogg. Scott’s *Minstrelsy of the Scottish Border*, first published from 1802 and later expanded, gathered Border ballads including “The Young Tamlane” and “The Dowie Dens of Yarrow”; the collection was influential precisely because it carried oral and manuscript material into print, though Scott’s editing has long been recognised as literary as well as documentary.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minstrelsy_of_the_Scottish_Border" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Minstrelsy of the Scottish Border</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Minstrelsy of the Scottish Border</span></span></span>

This creates a useful rule of thumb for readers: Selkirkshire folklore is often strongest where a named place, a traditional song, and a later literary retelling overlap. Carterhaugh is not simply a vague fairy wood; it is a real tract of land at the meeting of the Ettrick and Yarrow waters near Selkirk, tied to one of the great fairy ballads. Yarrow is not only a picturesque valley; it is a ballad landscape where remembered violence, standing stones and romantic poetry have been made to echo one another.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.tam-lin.org/scotland/carterhaugh.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: tam-lin.org">[tam-lin.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">tam-lin.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on tam-lin.org.</span></span></span>

The county’s folklore also reflects its terrain. Selkirkshire is hill country: wooded valleys, sheep ground, river junctions, lonely kirkyards and old riding routes. Such places are well suited to stories about boundary-crossing: between mortal and fairy, parish and forest, living community and ancestral dead, legal history and local legend. This is why the county’s traditions often feel less like isolated “spooky tales” and more like ways of reading the landscape.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://wikishire.co.uk/wiki/Selkirkshire" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wikishire.co.uk">[Wikishire]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wikishire.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wikishire.co.uk.</span></span></span>

## Tam Lin and Carterhaugh: Selkirkshire’s great fairy story

The most internationally recognisable piece of Selkirkshire folklore is the ballad of Tam Lin. In many versions, young women are warned not to go to Carterhaugh because Tam Lin is there; Janet goes anyway, plucks roses, meets him, and later learns that he is a mortal man held by the Queen of the Fairies. To save him, she must hold on to him through frightening transformations as the fairy host rides by at Halloween. Child’s great nineteenth-century collection prints multiple versions of the ballad as Child Ballad 39, and the Vaughan Williams Memorial Library lists “Tam Lin” as Roud 35.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://sacred-texts.com/neu/eng/child/ch039.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sacred-texts.com">[Internet Sacred Text Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sacred-texts.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on sacred-texts.com.</span></span></span>

Carterhaugh’s importance is that it gives the story a local body. The site is identified as woodland or haugh-land near the confluence of the Ettrick and Yarrow waters by Selkirk. The National Library of Scotland’s public storytelling material places Tam Lin at Carterhaugh woods near Yarrow in the Scottish Borders, while specialist Tam Lin material identifies Carterhaugh as a genuine location in Selkirkshire.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://artsandculture.google.com/story/myths-and-tales-from-the-scottish-borders-national-library-of-scotland/6wWxxemLDPDSkQ?hl=en" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: artsandculture.google.com">[Google Arts &amp; Culture]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">artsandculture.google.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on google.com.</span></span></span>

For folklore readers, Tam Lin matters because it is not a simple “fairy abduction” tale. Janet is not merely rescued; she is the rescuer. The fairy world is dangerous, seductive and rule-bound, while the human heroine wins through courage, grip and timing. The story also preserves motifs familiar across European folklore: the captive human in fairyland, the fairy tithe or payment, the Halloween ride, and the test of holding a loved one through shape-changing. Yet in Selkirkshire those broad motifs are pinned to a very specific Border place.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://sacred-texts.com/neu/eng/child/ch039.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sacred-texts.com">[Internet Sacred Text Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sacred-texts.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on sacred-texts.com.</span></span></span>

Modern retellings have made Tam Lin one of the most durable Scottish fairy stories in English-language culture. Folk performers, fantasy writers, podcasts and illustrated retellings repeatedly return to Carterhaugh because the story offers a rare combination of romance, threat, female agency and fairy otherworldliness. That modern popularity should not be mistaken for proof that every detail is ancient, but it does show how a Selkirkshire place-name has travelled far beyond the county.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://artsandculture.google.com/story/myths-and-tales-from-the-scottish-borders-national-library-of-scotland/6wWxxemLDPDSkQ?hl=en" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: artsandculture.google.com">[Google Arts &amp; Culture]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">artsandculture.google.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on google.com.</span></span></span>

## Yarrow: ballad country, stones and sorrow

The Yarrow valley is Selkirkshire’s other great folklore landscape. Its best-known song is “The Dowie Dens of Yarrow”, also called “The Braes of Yarrow” or simply “Yarrow”. The ballad tells of a lover ambushed and killed, often after a woman rejects richer suitors or family-approved men. The word “dowie” carries the sense of sadness or dreariness, which fits the song’s emotional power: Yarrow becomes a place where love, feud and mourning are inseparable.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://walterscott.eu/education/ballads-of-love-and-loss/the-dowie-dens-of-yarrow/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: walterscott.eu">[Walter Scott]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">walterscott.eu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Walter Scott The Dowie Dens of Yarrow</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Walter Scott The Dowie Dens of Yarrow</span></span></span>

Sir Walter Scott helped fix the ballad in the antiquarian imagination. A paper in the *Proceedings of the Society of Antiquaries of Scotland* notes that Scott suggested the fight in “The Dowie Dens of Yarrow” had taken place near the standing stones of Annan Street, but also criticises him for reshaping local details and apparently turning “Annan Street” into “Annan’s Treat”. This is a good example of how Selkirkshire folklore often works: oral tradition, real antiquities and Romantic-era literary handling are all present, but they are not the same thing.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://journals.socantscot.org/index.php/psas/article/download/7294/7263/7251" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journals.socantscot.org">[Society of Antiquaries Journals]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journals.socantscot.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on socantscot.org.</span></span></span>

The Yarrow Stone gives this ballad landscape a deeper time-depth. Historic Environment Scotland protects the Yarrow Stone, an inscribed stone east of Whitefield, as a scheduled monument. Other heritage accounts describe it as an early medieval inscribed stone associated with burial, found when moorland at Annan Street was ploughed in the early nineteenth century; beneath it were human bones, and the surrounding area had cairns.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://portal.historicenvironment.scot/apex/f?p=1505%3A300%3A%3A%3A%3A%3AVIEWTYPE%2CVIEWREF%3Adesignation%2CSM1727" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: portal.historicenvironment.scot">[historicenvironment.scot]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">portal.historicenvironment.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on historicenvironment.scot.</span></span></span>

Recent archaeological interpretation strengthens the sense that Yarrow is not merely a literary scene. The South East Scotland Archaeological Research Framework notes that at Yarrow the stone was found near a long-cist cemetery, with one grave recently radiocarbon-dated to the sixth century. Tourism material often says the inscription commemorates two princes of the British kingdom of Strathclyde, while more cautious archaeological summaries emphasise its early medieval Christian memorial character.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://scarf.scot/regional/sesarf/8-early-medieval/8-4-religion-and-ritual/8-4-2-early-inscribed-stones/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: scarf.scot">[Scarf]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">scarf.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on scarf.scot.</span></span></span>

The lesson for readers is not that the Yarrow Stone “proves” the ballad. It does not. Rather, Yarrow shows how folklore clusters around places already heavy with visible remains: cairns, stones, graves, old roads and river crossings. A valley with early medieval burial evidence can become, over centuries, a place where people expect old griefs to have happened.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://journals.socantscot.org/index.php/psas/article/download/7294/7263/7251" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journals.socantscot.org">[Society of Antiquaries Journals]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journals.socantscot.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on socantscot.org.</span></span></span>

## James Hogg and the Ettrick imagination

James Hogg, the “Ettrick Shepherd”, is central to Selkirkshire’s folklore identity. Born in the Ettrick Valley in 1770, he lived for much of his life in Ettrick or Yarrow and grew out of a local world rich in ballad and legend. Local heritage material stresses that Hogg had little formal schooling but inherited a vivid tradition of song and story, and that he helped Walter Scott collect ballads for the *Border Minstrelsy*.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ettrickandyarrow.org.uk/james-hogg-exhibition" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ettrickandyarrow.org.uk">[ettrickandyarrow.org.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ettrickandyarrow.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">james hogg exhibition</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">james hogg exhibition</span></span></span>

Hogg’s importance is that he stood close to both oral tradition and literary invention. He was not simply a collector writing down rural tales unchanged, nor simply a novelist using folklore as decoration. His fiction repeatedly turns local belief into narrative machinery. *The Brownie of Bodsbeck*, published in 1818, is especially useful for understanding this Border mixture: it is set in the Scottish Borders in 1685, during the persecution of the Covenanters, and uses belief in a Brownie to disguise human political and religious danger.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Brownie_of_Bodsbeck" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Brownie of Bodsbeck</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Brownie of Bodsbeck</span></span></span>

The Brownie in Hogg’s tale is therefore not only a household spirit. It becomes a cover story, a way for characters to interpret unexplained movements, hidden people and fear in the hills. This is very Selkirkshire: supernatural language is used to make sense of real social pressure. The result is folklore with a double edge. On one level, people believe in brownies, bogles, fairies and uncanny presences; on another, such beliefs can protect secrets, misdirect authorities, or explain danger without naming it directly.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Brownie_of_Bodsbeck" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Brownie of Bodsbeck</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Brownie of Bodsbeck</span></span></span>

Hogg’s continuing local presence also matters. The James Hogg exhibition at the former Ettrick School and related trail material keep him tied to the Ettrick and Yarrow landscape, rather than treating him only as a national literary figure. For a Selkirkshire folklore page, Hogg is one of the main bridges between oral tradition, Border history and modern cultural tourism.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://exploreselkirk.co.uk/james-hogg/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: exploreselkirk.co.uk">[Explore Selkirk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">exploreselkirk.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on exploreselkirk.co.uk.</span></span></span>

## Selkirk Common Riding: living custom, Flodden memory and civic identity

Selkirk Common Riding is the county’s most visible living tradition. Common Ridings across the Borders preserve the practice of riding boundaries, but Selkirk’s version has an especially strong emotional centre in the memory of Flodden in 1513. Local tourism material explains that the ceremony culminates in the Casting of the Colours in the Market Place, where the Royal Burgh Standard Bearer and other standard bearers cast their flags.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://exploreselkirk.co.uk/selkirk-common-riding/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: exploreselkirk.co.uk">[Explore Selkirk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">exploreselkirk.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on exploreselkirk.co.uk.</span></span></span>

The popular story is that Selkirk sent around eighty men to Flodden and only one, often named Fletcher, returned. In the ceremony, the casting or sweeping of the flag is said to recall that survivor signalling the town’s loss. South of Scotland tourism material presents this as the symbolism of the closing ceremony, and the British Film Institute’s record of early footage describes the day as recalling medieval Border disputes and the Battle of Flodden, with the colours linked by legend to a bloodied English flag.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://scotlandstartshere.com/ridings-festivals/selkirk-common-riding/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: scotlandstartshere.com">[Scotland Starts Here]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">scotlandstartshere.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on scotlandstartshere.com.</span></span></span>

As with much folklore, the Common Riding works whether or not every explanatory detail can be proved in its most dramatic form. A separate local-history discussion notes debate around the precise meaning of the Casting of the Colours, contrasting the military-triumph explanation with more practical or civic interpretations. That tension is valuable rather than embarrassing: it shows how custom gathers meanings over time, and how a community can use ceremony to hold grief, pride, boundary memory and public belonging together.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://discoverscottishborders.com/debunking-the-myths-of-selkirk-common-riding-fact-vs-folklore/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: discoverscottishborders.com">[Discover Scottish Borders]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">discoverscottishborders.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Discover Scottish Borders Debunking the Myths of Selkirk Common Riding: Fact vs</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Discover Scottish Borders Debunking the Myths of Selkirk Common Riding: Fact vs</span></span></span>

For readers interested in folklore rather than pageantry, the key point is that Common Riding is not a museum object. It is an enacted tradition. People ride, gather, sing, watch, remember and argue about meanings. Selkirkshire’s folklore is therefore not only in old ballad books; it is also in the annual choreography of streets, horses, flags and local memory.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://exploreselkirk.co.uk/selkirk-common-riding/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: exploreselkirk.co.uk">[Explore Selkirk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">exploreselkirk.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on exploreselkirk.co.uk.</span></span></span>

## St Mary’s Loch and the sacred landscape of Yarrow

St Mary’s Loch adds a quieter strand to Selkirkshire folklore: sacred place, poetic landscape and local legend. The loch takes its name from a church dedicated to St Mary that once stood on its northern shore, now represented by the surviving kirkyard. Saints in Scottish Place-Names identifies St Mary’s Church of the Lowes, on the shore of St Mary’s Loch, as the original parish kirk of Yarrow, also known as St Mary “of the Forest” or “of the Lowes”.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://saintsplaces.gla.ac.uk/place.php?id=1358871550" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: saintsplaces.gla.ac.uk">[saintsplaces.gla.ac.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">saintsplaces.gla.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on gla.ac.uk.</span></span></span>

The site is important because it places Christian sacred geography inside the old forest landscape. Scotland Starts Here describes St Mary’s Kirkyard as the site of an ancient place of worship overlooking the loch and notes claims that it may be the “Kirk in the Forest” associated in tradition with William Wallace’s proclamation as Guardian of Scotland. Border Archaeology gives the documentary base more cautiously, pointing to evidence of a religious settlement at St Mary’s Loch by 1275 and a 1292 reference to the Church of the Blessed Mary of Farmainishope.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://scotlandstartshere.com/point-of-interest/st-marys-kirkyard/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: scotlandstartshere.com">[Scotland Starts Here]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">scotlandstartshere.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on scotlandstartshere.com.</span></span></span>

Local legend also gives the loch itself an uncanny quality. Wikishire records the tradition that St Mary’s Loch has no bottom and is reputed to be the coldest loch in Scotland. Such claims are common in lake folklore across Britain: “bottomless” water turns a natural feature into a place of depth, danger and mystery, even when modern geography can measure it.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://wikishire.co.uk/wiki/St_Mary%27s_Loch" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wikishire.co.uk">[Wikishire]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wikishire.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">St Mary&#x27;s Loch</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">St Mary&#x27;s Loch</span></span></span>

The loch’s literary afterlife reinforces its folklore role. It is associated with James Hogg and Tibbie Shiel’s Inn, and the Yarrow landscape was famously taken up by Romantic poets including Wordsworth. For Selkirkshire, St Mary’s Loch is less a monster-haunted lake than a reflective sacred and poetic place: a setting where church memory, Border nationalism, shepherd-poets and tourist imagination meet.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St_Mary%27s_Loch" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">St Mary&#x27;s Loch</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">St Mary&#x27;s Loch</span></span></span>

## Witches, marginal graves and cautious evidence

Selkirkshire does have witchcraft material, but it should be handled carefully. The Survey of Scottish Witchcraft, a University of Edinburgh resource covering Scottish witchcraft accusations from 1563 to 1736, is the proper starting point for historical witchcraft evidence. Its guidance and archival listings include Selkirk kirk-session material, showing that Selkirk belongs within the documentary world of Scottish witchcraft records, even if the county is not as famous for witch trials as some other areas.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://witches.hca.ed.ac.uk/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: witches.hca.ed.ac.uk">[Witches]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">witches.hca.ed.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on ed.ac.uk.</span></span></span>

A more local and folkloric case is Tibbie Tamson’s Grave near Selkirk. Scottish Borders Council walking-route material describes a grave marked “Tamson 1790” and a plaque whose verse calls her “a witch, perhaps”. The same council leaflet gives a more human story: Tibbie was said to be a simple-minded Selkirk woman caught stealing yarn, who hanged herself in shame and, being regarded as a criminal, was buried outside consecrated ground by sympathetic townsfolk.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.scotborders.gov.uk/downloads/file/682/selkirk.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: scotborders.gov.uk">[Scottish Borders Council]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">scotborders.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Scottish Borders Councilaround Selkirk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Scottish Borders Councilaround Selkirk</span></span></span>

This is a good example of the difference between “witch folklore” and a proven witch trial. Tibbie Tamson is remembered through a grave, a rhyme, a walking route and a local label of “witch”, but the story presented by the council is not a formal early modern witch-trial narrative. It is closer to the folklore of exclusion: who could be buried in consecrated ground, how communities remembered shame, and how later generations turned a marginal burial into a mysterious landmark.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.scotborders.gov.uk/downloads/file/682/selkirk.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: scotborders.gov.uk">[Scottish Borders Council]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">scotborders.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Scottish Borders Councilaround Selkirk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Scottish Borders Councilaround Selkirk</span></span></span>

The wider Scottish context matters here. Scotland’s witch-hunt formally belongs to the period of the Witchcraft Act, 1563 to 1736, and modern projects have mapped thousands of accused people from archival records. For Selkirkshire, the safest public-facing approach is to distinguish documented witchcraft records, later “witch” place-stories, and modern heritage retellings rather than merging them into one dramatic narrative.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.heritagefund.org.uk/news/mapping-scotlands-accused-witches-through-open-data" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: heritagefund.org.uk">[The National Lottery Heritage Fund]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">heritagefund.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">mapping scotlands accused witches through open data</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">mapping scotlands accused witches through open data</span></span></span>

## What is old tradition, and what is later retelling?

Selkirkshire’s folklore is powerful because it has been retold so often, but that also makes source awareness essential. Tam Lin is old and well attested in ballad tradition, with early literary references and multiple collected versions, but the versions most readers encounter today usually come through Child, Burns, folk revival performance, fantasy literature or modern storytelling.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://sacred-texts.com/neu/eng/child/ch039.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sacred-texts.com">[sacred-texts.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sacred-texts.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on sacred-texts.com.</span></span></span>

“The Dowie Dens of Yarrow” is likewise a traditional ballad rooted in the Yarrow landscape, but attempts to pin it to a single historical fight are uncertain. Scott’s proposed location near the stones helped shape the legend, while later antiquarian criticism reminds us that Romantic writers could preserve tradition and distort it at the same time.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://walterscott.eu/education/ballads-of-love-and-loss/the-dowie-dens-of-yarrow/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: walterscott.eu">[Walter Scott]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">walterscott.eu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Walter Scott The Dowie Dens of Yarrow</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Walter Scott The Dowie Dens of Yarrow</span></span></span>

The Yarrow Stone is different again. It is not folklore in the narrow sense; it is an early medieval monument with archaeological and heritage significance. Yet it has become part of the county’s legendary landscape because people connect visible stones with remembered battles, graves, princes and songs. The best interpretation keeps both levels in view: the stone as protected material heritage, and the stone as a focus for story.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://portal.historicenvironment.scot/apex/f?p=1505%3A300%3A%3A%3A%3A%3AVIEWTYPE%2CVIEWREF%3Adesignation%2CSM1727" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: portal.historicenvironment.scot">[historicenvironment.scot]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">portal.historicenvironment.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on historicenvironment.scot.</span></span></span>

Common Riding is a living custom with historical roots and legendary explanation. Its Flodden story gives emotional meaning to the ceremony, while debate over details shows that tradition is not the same as a court transcript. In folklore terms, the question is not only “did it happen exactly like that?” but “how has this community chosen to remember itself?”<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://exploreselkirk.co.uk/selkirk-common-riding/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: exploreselkirk.co.uk">[exploreselkirk.co.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">exploreselkirk.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on exploreselkirk.co.uk.</span></span></span>

## How Selkirkshire connects to neighbouring folklore

Selkirkshire folklore should be linked naturally to the wider Scottish Borders. Tam Lin connects the county to fairy traditions across Scotland and northern England, especially stories of fairy abduction, fairy hills, greenwood encounters and rescue from the otherworld. It also sits beside other Border fairy and prophetic traditions, including Thomas the Rhymer in neighbouring Roxburghshire.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tam_Lin" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Tam Lin</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Tam Lin</span></span></span>

Yarrow and Ettrick link Selkirkshire to Border ballad culture more broadly: tragic love songs, reiver memory, family feud, hidden graves and violent meetings in remote valleys. Scott’s *Minstrelsy* did not preserve Selkirkshire alone; it placed the county within a larger Border song-world stretching across neighbouring historic counties.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minstrelsy_of_the_Scottish_Border" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Minstrelsy of the Scottish Border</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Minstrelsy of the Scottish Border</span></span></span>

The county also connects to Scottish traditions of sacred water and old kirkyards through St Mary’s Loch and St Mary of the Lowes. Unlike counties with famous healing wells or saintly miracle cults, Selkirkshire’s sacred folklore is more atmospheric: a vanished kirk, a loch with bottomless legend, old burial ground, poetic memory and the lingering title “Kirk in the Forest”.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://saintsplaces.gla.ac.uk/place.php?id=1358871550" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: saintsplaces.gla.ac.uk">[gla.ac.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">saintsplaces.gla.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on gla.ac.uk.</span></span></span>

Finally, Selkirk Common Riding links the county to the Border custom of riding marches. Its particular Flodden symbolism is local, but its structure belongs to a regional habit of turning boundaries into public performance. That makes Selkirkshire a useful county for understanding how folklore can be sung, walked, ridden and re-enacted rather than only told.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://scotlandstartshere.com/ridings-festivals/selkirk-common-riding/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: scotlandstartshere.com">[Scotland Starts Here]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">scotlandstartshere.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on scotlandstartshere.com.</span></span></span>

## What readers should remember

Selkirkshire’s folklore is not a catalogue of random marvels. It is a landscape tradition built from fairy ballad, river valley, old forest, stone, song and annual civic ritual. Carterhaugh gives the county one of Britain’s great fairy stories in Tam Lin. Yarrow gives it a tragic ballad landscape where archaeology, antiquarian speculation and poetic memory overlap. Ettrick gives it James Hogg, whose writing shows how local belief could become literature without losing its hill-country roots. Selkirk itself gives it Common Riding, where Flodden memory and civic identity are still performed in public.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://artsandculture.google.com/story/myths-and-tales-from-the-scottish-borders-national-library-of-scotland/6wWxxemLDPDSkQ?hl=en" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: artsandculture.google.com">[google.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">artsandculture.google.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on google.com.</span></span></span>

The most honest way to read Selkirkshire is to let its traditions keep their layers. Some elements are old oral ballad; some are early medieval archaeology; some are Romantic-era reshaping; some are local tourism; some are still living custom. Together they make Selkirkshire one of the most rewarding small historic counties for readers interested in how folklore attaches itself to real ground.

    return url + joiner + params.join('&');
  }
  function domainLabel(marketId) {
    var cfg = markets[marketId] || markets[defaultMarket] || {};
    return cfg.domain_label || 'eBay';
  }
  function flagClass(marketId) {
    return 'fr-ebay-market-flag fr-ebay-market-flag--' + String(marketId || '').toLowerCase().replace(/_/g, '-').replace(/[^a-z0-9-]+/g, '');
  }
  function closeMenu(section) {
    var menu = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-menu]');
    var trigger = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-trigger]');
    var picker = section.querySelector('.fr-ebay-market-picker');
    if (menu) menu.hidden = true;
    if (trigger) trigger.setAttribute('aria-expanded', 'false');
    if (picker) picker.classList.remove('fr-ebay-market-picker--open');
  }
  function availableMarkets(section) {
    var select = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-select]');
    if (select) {
      return Array.prototype.slice.call(select.options)
        .map(function (option) { return option.value; })
        .filter(function (marketId) { return !!markets[marketId]; });
    }
    return Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]'))
      .map(function (button) { return button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button'); })
      .filter(function (marketId) { return !!markets[marketId]; });
  }
  function applyMarket(section, marketId, persist) {
    var available = availableMarkets(section);
    if (available.indexOf(marketId)< 0) marketId = available[0] || defaultMarket;
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-localized-link]')).forEach(function (link) {
      var query = link.getAttribute('data-ebay-query') || '';
      var reference = link.getAttribute('data-ebay-reference') || '';
      link.setAttribute('href', buildUrl(query, reference, marketId));
    });
    var select = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-select]');
    if (select && available.indexOf(marketId) >= 0) select.value = marketId;
    var selectedText = marketId;
    if (select && select.selectedIndex >= 0) selectedText = select.options[select.selectedIndex].text;
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-selected-market-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = selectedText;
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-selected-market-flag], [data-ebay-trigger-market-flag]')).forEach(function (flag) {
      flag.className = flagClass(marketId);
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-trigger-market-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = selectedText;
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-domain-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = domainLabel(marketId);
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-option]')).forEach(function (button) {
      button.setAttribute('aria-selected', button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-option') === marketId ? 'true' : 'false');
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      var active = button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button') === marketId;
      button.setAttribute('aria-pressed', active ? 'true' : 'false');
    });
    section.setAttribute('data-ebay-selected-market', marketId);
    if (persist) {
      try { if (window.localStorage) localStorage.setItem('phoenix-ebay-market', marketId); } catch (err) {}
    }
    closeMenu(section);
  }
  sections.forEach(function (section) {
    if (section.getAttribute('data-ebay-localized-init') === '1') return;
    section.setAttribute('data-ebay-localized-init', '1');
    var available = availableMarkets(section);
    var marketId = defaultMarket;
    if (window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation && window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation.inferMarket) {
      marketId = window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation.inferMarket(available, {
        storageKey: 'phoenix-ebay-market',
        defaultMarket: defaultMarket
      });
    } else if (available.indexOf(defaultMarket)< 0) {
      marketId = available[0] || defaultMarket;
    }
    var select = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-select]');
    if (select) {
      select.addEventListener('change', function () {
        applyMarket(section, select.value, true);
      });
    }
    var trigger = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-trigger]');
    var menu = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-menu]');
    if (trigger && menu) {
      trigger.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
        event.stopPropagation();
        var expanded = trigger.getAttribute('aria-expanded') === 'true';
        menu.hidden = expanded;
        trigger.setAttribute('aria-expanded', expanded ? 'false' : 'true');
        var picker = trigger.closest('.fr-ebay-market-picker');
        if (picker) picker.classList.toggle('fr-ebay-market-picker--open', !expanded);
      });
      Array.prototype.slice.call(menu.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-option]')).forEach(function (button) {
        button.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
          event.stopPropagation();
          applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-option'), true);
        });
      });
      document.addEventListener('click', function () { closeMenu(section); });
    }
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      button.addEventListener('click', function () {
        applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button'), true);
      });
    });
    applyMarket(section, marketId, false);
  });
})();
</script>
