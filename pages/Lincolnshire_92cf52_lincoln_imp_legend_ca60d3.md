## Introduction

The Lincoln Imp is a small medieval grotesque in Lincoln Cathedral that became one of [Lincolnshire]({{ 'what-haunts-lincolnshires-fields-fens/' | relative_url }})’s most recognisable legends. The carving itself is old: it sits high in the Angel Choir, the richly decorated eastern part of the cathedral built in the later thirteenth century to house the shrine of St Hugh. The familiar story, however, is younger and more fluid. In its modern form, two imps are blown to Lincoln by the Devil, make mischief in the cathedral, and one is turned to stone by an angel, leaving the other outside in the wind.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://lincolnshirefolktalesproject.com/2024/02/20/the-lincoln-imp/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: lincolnshirefolktalesproject.com">[lincolnshirefolktalesproject.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">lincolnshirefolktalesproject.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on lincolnshirefolktalesproject.com.</span></span></span>



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/Lincolnshire_92cf52_lincoln_imp_legend_ca60d3-overview.webp" | relative_url }}" alt="Overview image for Lincoln Imp" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

That mix of genuine medieval stonework and later storytelling is what makes the Lincoln Imp so useful as folklore. It is not simply an “ancient legend” preserved unchanged from the Middle Ages. It is a cathedral object whose meaning has been enlarged by guidebooks, Victorian souvenir culture, local pride, football, tourism and children’s retellings. The Imp matters because it shows how Lincolnshire can turn one small carving into a county emblem: mischievous, memorable, commercial, sacred-adjacent and unmistakably local.

## The carving in the Angel Choir

The first thing to separate is the object from the story. The Imp is a small cross-legged grotesque, usually described as about twelve inches or thirty centimetres high, set high in the Angel Choir of Lincoln Cathedral. The Lincolnshire Folk Tales Project places it above the tomb of St Hugh, overlooking the altar, while Visit Lincoln describes it as tucked high on a pillar between two arches on the north side of the choir.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://lincolnshirefolktalesproject.com/2024/02/20/the-lincoln-imp/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: lincolnshirefolktalesproject.com">[Lincolnshire Folk Tales Project]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">lincolnshirefolktalesproject.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on lincolnshirefolktalesproject.com.</span></span></span>

The location matters. This is not an isolated roadside monster, a village ghost, or a creature from a fenland tale. It is embedded in one of Lincolnshire’s great ecclesiastical spaces. RIBA’s record for the Angel Choir notes that it was built between 1250 and 1280 to house St Hugh’s shrine at the east end of the cathedral. That gives the Imp its likely broad medieval setting: it belongs to the same thirteenth-century world of carved capitals, angels, saints’ shrines and playful stone ornament.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ribapix.com/lincoln-cathedral-the-angel-choir_riba91991" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ribapix.com">[ribapix.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ribapix.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Lincoln Cathedral: the Angel Choir &#124; RIBA pix</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Lincoln Cathedral: the Angel Choir &#124; RIBA pix</span></span></span>

A “grotesque” is a decorative carving of a strange, comic, ugly, hybrid or monstrous figure. It is not the same as a gargoyle in the strict architectural sense, because a gargoyle usually functions as a waterspout. The Lincoln Imp is a grotesque: a carved figure that looks creaturely and demonic but is part of the cathedral’s ornamental sculpture rather than a piece of drainage engineering. Lincoln Cathedral’s own shop description, preserved in search results, calls it the best known of the cathedral’s grotesques and says its half-human, half-animal form was probably intended to represent the Devil, a popular theme for thirteenth-century sculptors.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://lincolncathedral.com/product/the-legend-of-the-lincoln-imp/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: lincolncathedral.com">[lincolncathedral.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">lincolncathedral.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Legend Of The Lincoln Imp &#124; Booklet</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Angel Choir. The Lincoln Imp is the best known of all the grotesque carved figures in the Cathedral. Its half human, half animal form was...</span></span></span>

This is where the carving becomes intriguing. Medieval churches often used monsters, devils and odd little beasts in sacred spaces. They could express evil overcome, moral warning, artistic humour, or the sheer inventiveness of masons working in stone. The Lincoln Imp is especially striking because it appears among the splendour of the Angel Choir. As the Lincolnshire Folk Tales Project notes, it is not certain why a demon-like figure should be placed in such a sacred part of the building, and that uncertainty has helped the carving become famous.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://lincolnshirefolktalesproject.com/2024/02/20/the-lincoln-imp/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: lincolnshirefolktalesproject.com">[Lincolnshire Folk Tales Project]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">lincolnshirefolktalesproject.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on lincolnshirefolktalesproject.com.</span></span></span>

## The angel, the Devil and the escaped imp

The best-known version of the legend is simple enough to retell in a few sentences. The Devil sends two imps out into the world. After causing trouble elsewhere, they reach Lincoln Cathedral and begin wrecking things: knocking people over, breaking windows, and generally behaving like small supernatural vandals. An angel tells them to stop. One imp hides, while the other answers defiantly, and the angel turns him into stone. The second imp escapes, and the wind outside the cathedral is said to be him still waiting, or raging, for his companion’s return.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.visitlincoln.com/blog/post/the-legend-of-the-lincoln-imp/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: visitlincoln.com">[Visit Lincoln]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">visitlincoln.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Visit Lincoln The Legend of the Lincoln Imp &#124; Visit Lincoln</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Visit Lincoln The Legend of the Lincoln Imp &#124; Visit Lincoln</span></span></span>

That story works because it explains three things at once. It explains the carving: the Imp is not just a mason’s joke, but a creature petrified in the act of mischief. It explains the weather: the exposed hilltop cathedral becomes windy because the Imp’s companion is still outside. And it explains moral order: the Angel Choir, full of sacred sculpture, defeats disorder by turning the offender into part of the building.

The tale also makes the Imp more charming than frightening. He is devilish, but not grandly satanic. He is a nuisance, a trickster, a child-sized troublemaker. The word “imp” helps here, because it carries the double sense of a small devil and a mischievous child. The result is a creature that can be naughty without becoming too dark for guidebooks, school visits or souvenirs.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://lincolnshirefolktalesproject.com/2024/02/20/the-lincoln-imp/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: lincolnshirefolktalesproject.com">[Lincolnshire Folk Tales Project]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">lincolnshirefolktalesproject.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on lincolnshirefolktalesproject.com.</span></span></span>

Modern retellings often add a wider route. Visit Lincoln’s version says the imps first stopped at Chesterfield, where they allegedly twisted the spire of St Mary and All Saints, before reaching Lincoln. That detail links the Imp to a broader English habit of explaining unusual church features through stories of devils, saints, winds or supernatural punishment. It also shows how a legend can gather extra places as it travels: Lincoln remains the centre, but the tale borrows a famous Derbyshire landmark to give the imps a little journey before their cathedral downfall.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.visitlincoln.com/blog/post/the-legend-of-the-lincoln-imp/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: visitlincoln.com">[Visit Lincoln]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">visitlincoln.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Visit Lincoln The Legend of the Lincoln Imp &#124; Visit Lincoln</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Visit Lincoln The Legend of the Lincoln Imp &#124; Visit Lincoln</span></span></span>

## How old is the Lincoln Imp legend?

The stone carving is medieval, but the fully familiar legend is not securely documented from the thirteenth century. This is the point most easily lost in casual retellings. The Imp feels ancient because it is attached to an ancient building. The evidence for the named “Lincoln Imp” story, however, becomes much clearer in the late nineteenth century and early twentieth century.

The folklore record shows older Lincoln traditions about the Devil, the wind and the cathedral, but not always about the specific Imp carving. In *County Folklore*, published by the Folklore Society, the Lincoln material preserves sayings such as “He looks as the devil did over Lincoln” and discusses traditions about the Devil looking over the city, the wind waiting outside the Minster, and grotesque carvings on the cathedral. The same section is cautious about the Imp as folklore, noting a query over whether a superstition attached to Lincoln Imp trinkets was “made to order” and observing that evidence was not then forthcoming that the Imp had long been connected with the city’s devil legend.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/stream/countyfolklore05folkuoft/countyfolklore05folkuoft_djvu.txt" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Internet Archive Full text of &quot;County folklore</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Internet Archive Full text of &quot;County folklore</span></span></span>

That caution is important. It does not mean the legend is fake in a simple sense. It means the tradition seems to have grown by combining several ingredients: a real medieval grotesque, older sayings about the Devil and Lincoln, local stories about wind around the cathedral, and the late Victorian appetite for picturesque local legends. Folklore is often made this way. A story can be young in print, older in oral hints, and still powerful in local identity.

A key printed milestone is Arnold Frost’s *The Ballad of the Wind, the Devil and Lincoln Minster*, published in the 1890s. WorldCat records an 1898 third edition, and the Folklore Society’s *County Folklore* quotes lines from the ballad in connection with the Imp and the wind.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://worldcat.org/oclc/38703416" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: worldcat.org">[WorldCat]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">worldcat.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on worldcat.org.</span></span></span> Lincolnshire Life gives the earliest recorded rendition as Frost’s ballad, while noting Frost’s own claim that he had heard the story from an older North Lincolnshire man.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.lincolnshirelife.co.uk/culture/tales-of-the-lincoln-imp/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: lincolnshirelife.co.uk">[Lincolnshire Life]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">lincolnshirelife.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on lincolnshirelife.co.uk.</span></span></span>

The safest way to put it is this: the carving is thirteenth-century; Lincoln had older devil-and-wind traditions; the named Lincoln Imp legend becomes well-attested in print around the late nineteenth century; and the polished tourist version familiar today is a modern descendant of those materials.

## Why a mischievous demon belongs in a holy place

For a first-time visitor, the puzzle is obvious: why would a cathedral have a little devil in the Angel Choir? The answer is not that medieval builders were secretly celebrating evil. Medieval churches often staged evil, disorder, animals, monsters and comic ugliness in stone so that sacred order could contain it. Devils appear in church art as warnings, defeated enemies, jokes, boundary creatures or reminders of the world outside holiness.

The Lincoln Imp gains extra force because he sits in a place associated with angels and St Hugh. The Angel Choir was built to house the shrine of a canonised bishop, and RIBA identifies it as part of the cathedral’s thirteenth-century eastern expansion.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ribapix.com/lincoln-cathedral-the-angel-choir_riba91991" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ribapix.com">[ribapix.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ribapix.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Lincoln Cathedral: the Angel Choir &#124; RIBA pix</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Lincoln Cathedral: the Angel Choir &#124; RIBA pix</span></span></span> A tiny imp in that setting becomes visually and narratively satisfying: mischief is literally held within the sacred fabric. The legend then turns that visual contrast into a story. The angel does not merely defeat the Imp; the angel makes him into architecture.

This is why the Imp works better than a generic demon tale. A freestanding story about two devils causing trouble would be forgettable. A peculiar stone figure high in a famous cathedral gives the tale a fixed address. Visitors can look up, search for the carving, and feel that they have found the exact place where the story “happened”. The legend is therefore anchored in a real object, even if the story explaining it is not as old as the stone.

The wind motif also belongs to the building’s landscape. Lincoln Cathedral stands high above the surrounding city, exposed to weather and visible across the county. Older traditions quoted in *County Folklore* explain the wind around the Minster by saying that the Devil went inside while the wind waited outside, or that the Devil was afraid to come out and be blown away.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/stream/countyfolklore05folkuoft/countyfolklore05folkuoft_djvu.txt" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Internet Archive Full text of &quot;County folklore</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Internet Archive Full text of &quot;County folklore</span></span></span> The modern two-imp story keeps that atmospheric memory but makes it more playful: one imp is stone, the other is still outside in the air.

## From cathedral story to civic mascot

The Imp’s rise from carving to emblem owes a great deal to late Victorian and early twentieth-century popular culture. James Ward Usher, a Lincoln jeweller and collector, played a major role. Lincoln Museum credits Usher with reviving the Imp’s popularity in the late 1800s. After taking charge of the family business in 1874, he looked for a novelty that would attract customers, settled on the Lincoln Imp, and by the 1880s had obtained a registered patent design giving him a temporary monopoly on Imp-themed goods. His firm produced brooches, cuff links, tie pins, spoons and thimbles.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.lincolnmuseum.com/blog/view/spotlight-on-..-the-lincoln-imp" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: lincolnmuseum.com">[Lincoln Museum]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">lincolnmuseum.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on lincolnmuseum.com.</span></span></span>

This matters because it shows how folklore can be spread by objects as much as by storytellers. An Imp brooch or spoon is a portable legend. It lets the visitor take the cathedral creature home, wear it, gift it, and explain it. Lincoln Museum notes that the popularity of Usher’s Imp goods became so strong that letters addressed only to “the Silversmith who makes and sells the Lincoln Imp” could still find their way to his shop.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.lincolnmuseum.com/blog/view/spotlight-on-..-the-lincoln-imp" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: lincolnmuseum.com">[Lincoln Museum]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">lincolnmuseum.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on lincolnmuseum.com.</span></span></span>

The Lincolnshire Folk Tales Project likewise emphasises Usher’s role, noting that he acquired a patent in 1890 and that original Imp brooches and spoons can be seen in the Usher Gallery.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://lincolnshirefolktalesproject.com/2024/02/20/the-lincoln-imp/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: lincolnshirefolktalesproject.com">[Lincolnshire Folk Tales Project]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">lincolnshirefolktalesproject.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on lincolnshirefolktalesproject.com.</span></span></span> In other words, the Imp’s modern fame was not only a matter of antiquarian books or cathedral guides. It was also built through retail, display, novelty design and the Victorian tourist economy.

From there the Imp became a wider city sign. Visit Lincoln points visitors towards imps on door knockers, jewellery, gift-shop items and Lincoln City Football Club.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.visitlincoln.com/blog/post/the-legend-of-the-lincoln-imp/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: visitlincoln.com">[Visit Lincoln]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">visitlincoln.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Visit Lincoln The Legend of the Lincoln Imp &#124; Visit Lincoln</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Visit Lincoln The Legend of the Lincoln Imp &#124; Visit Lincoln</span></span></span> The Association of English Cathedrals reported in 2025 on a red sign at Lincoln Cathedral celebrating the origin of Lincoln City Football Club’s nickname, “The Imps”, and described Poacher the Imp as a six-foot incarnation of the Lincoln Imp seen at matches at the LNER Stadium.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://englishcathedrals.co.uk/latest-news/lincoln-cathedral-lincoln-city-football-club/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: englishcathedrals.co.uk">[The Association of English Cathedrals]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">englishcathedrals.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Association of English Cathedrals Lincoln Cathedral</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Association of English Cathedrals Lincoln Cathedral</span></span></span>

That football link is not a side note. It shows the Imp’s shift from cathedral curiosity to shared civic mascot. The figure can stand in a sacred building, a museum case, a jeweller’s window, a club badge, a tourist trail and a match-day crowd. Few county legends travel so easily between heritage and popular life.

## What the Lincoln Imp reveals about Lincolnshire folklore

The Lincoln Imp is not Lincolnshire’s deepest oral tradition in the way that [Tiddy Mun]({{ 'tiddy-mun/' | relative_url }}) is tied to fenland drainage, wetland fear and environmental change. Nor is it a ghost story dependent on witness accounts. Its power is different. It is an object legend: a story grown around a visible thing.

That makes it especially durable. People may forget the exact wording of Arnold Frost’s ballad, or the older sayings about the Devil looking over Lincoln, but they remember the idea that a naughty imp was turned to stone in the cathedral. The story is short, visual and easy to pass on. It can be told to children without much explanation, used by guides without slowing a tour, and adapted by businesses without losing its recognisable shape.

It also captures a distinctively Lincolnshire balance between grandeur and mischief. Lincoln Cathedral is one of the county’s great landmarks: serious, sacred, architecturally ambitious and historically dominant. The Imp cuts that grandeur down to human size. He lets people encounter the cathedral not only as an achievement of Gothic building, but as a place with a joke hidden in the stonework. That is one reason the legend has stayed friendly rather than frightening.

For folklore readers, the most honest reading is also the most interesting. The Lincoln Imp is medieval stone, late Victorian promotion, cathedral legend, souvenir brand and living city symbol all at once. Its story has been polished over time, but that does not make it empty. It shows how local tradition often works: a real place gives rise to a memorable explanation; printed versions stabilise it; shops and museums circulate it; and a community keeps choosing it as a sign of itself.

## How the Imp is retold today

Today, most people meet the Lincoln Imp through heritage interpretation, tourism pages, museum collections, school materials, local businesses, football culture or children’s books. Visit Lincoln’s current account gives the familiar angel-and-two-imps version and directs visitors to look for the carving high in the choir, with a spotlight available to help them find it.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.visitlincoln.com/blog/post/the-legend-of-the-lincoln-imp/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: visitlincoln.com">[Visit Lincoln]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">visitlincoln.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Visit Lincoln The Legend of the Lincoln Imp &#124; Visit Lincoln</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Visit Lincoln The Legend of the Lincoln Imp &#124; Visit Lincoln</span></span></span> Lincoln Museum frames the Imp through Usher’s jewellery and souvenir trade, showing how the creature became famous beyond the cathedral.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.lincolnmuseum.com/blog/view/spotlight-on-..-the-lincoln-imp" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: lincolnmuseum.com">[Lincoln Museum]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">lincolnmuseum.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on lincolnmuseum.com.</span></span></span> The Lincolnshire Folk Tales Project places the Imp within the county’s wider story culture, while being careful about the difference between the medieval carving and later fame.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://lincolnshirefolktalesproject.com/2024/02/20/the-lincoln-imp/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: lincolnshirefolktalesproject.com">[Lincolnshire Folk Tales Project]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">lincolnshirefolktalesproject.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on lincolnshirefolktalesproject.com.</span></span></span>

That layered retelling is healthier than pretending the legend is unchanged medieval oral tradition. The Imp’s value lies in the way each period has added something. Medieval masons gave Lincoln a strange little figure in stone. Older local sayings gave the city a devilish wind-haunted atmosphere. Late Victorian writers and jewellers gave the Imp a name, a market and a wider audience. Modern Lincoln has made him a mascot.

The result is a legend that is both small and unusually expansive. The carving is tiny, half-hidden above the Angel Choir. The cultural footprint is large: cathedral guidebooks, museum displays, Usher jewellery, football identity, local branding and Lincolnshire storytelling. For a county folklore map, the Lincoln Imp belongs exactly where readers expect to find him: in Lincoln Cathedral, grinning from the stone, halfway between sacred art and local mischief.
