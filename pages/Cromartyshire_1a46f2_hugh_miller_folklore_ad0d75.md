## Introduction

Hugh Miller matters to Cromarty folklore because he did something rare: he turned a small Highland town’s fireside memory into a printed record before much of it could fade. Born in Cromarty in 1802, Miller became famous as a geologist, journalist and religious controversialist, but his first major literary success was *Scenes and Legends of the North of Scotland; or, The Traditional History of Cromarty*, first published in 1835. The book gathered local history, family memory, supernatural belief, sea stories, customs and moral tales from Cromarty and its neighbouring shores, making it the single most important source for the folklore of historic [Cromartyshire]({{ 'where-cromartyshire-folklore-meets-sea/' | relative_url }}).<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nts.org.uk/visit/places/hugh-millers-birthplace/hugh-miller" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nts.org.uk">[National Trust for Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nts.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">hugh miller</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Miller would first taste literary success in 1835 with the publication of Scenes and Legends...</span></span></span>

His legacy is not simply that he “collected folklore”. Miller shaped Cromarty’s remembered world: witches at Tarbat Ness, mermaids in the Moray Firth, Halloween rites, fairy belief, prophetic second sight, ghosts, uncanny houses, old wells, fishing disasters and legends tied to named streets, rocks, caves and kirkyards. Modern Cromarty still returns to him through Hugh Miller’s Birthplace Cottage and Museum, local walks, storytelling events and children’s retellings, so his nineteenth-century book remains part archive, part literary landmark and part living doorway into Cromartyshire’s legendary landscape.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nts.org.uk/visit/places/hugh-millers-birthplace" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nts.org.uk">[National Trust for Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nts.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Trust for ScotlandHugh Miller&#x27;s Birthplace Cottage &amp; MuseumHumble abode of inspirational geologist, folklorist and fossil hunter...</span></span></span>

## Why Cromarty Needed Hugh Miller

Cromartyshire is awkward folklore territory because the historic county was never a neat block on the map. Its stories sit around Cromarty town and the Black Isle, but also reach towards Tarbat, Portmahomack, the Moray Firth and other detached or overlapping Highland districts. That makes a local source especially valuable: without someone like Miller, many tales would be scattered across parish memory, family anecdote and later “Ross and Cromarty” references rather than recognised as part of Cromartyshire’s own story culture.

Miller’s authority comes from proximity. He was not a distant antiquarian arriving to extract picturesque tales from the Highlands. He was a Cromarty man, born in the town, raised among its artisan, seafaring and churchgoing families, and shaped by the same shoreline, quarries, raised beaches and narrow streets that recur in his writing. The National Trust for Scotland identifies him as born in Cromarty on 10 October 1802 and describes *Scenes and Legends* as a collection of local historic and folkloric stories gathered during his time in and around Cromarty.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nts.org.uk/visit/places/hugh-millers-birthplace/hugh-miller" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nts.org.uk">[National Trust for Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nts.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">hugh miller</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Miller would first taste literary success in 1835 with the publication of Scenes and Legends...</span></span></span>

That local rootedness matters because Cromarty’s folklore is strongly place-bound. Miller’s stories are rarely floating “once upon a time” tales. They attach to the Bay of Cromarty, Tarbat Ness, the Sutors, old houses, kirkyards, wells, caves, fishing grounds and remembered families. The result is a folklore record that lets readers connect story to landscape: a witch is not just “a Highland witch”, but [Stine Bheag]({{ 'stine-bheag/' | relative_url }}) of Tarbat; a sea marvel is not just “a mermaid tale”, but a Moray Firth narrative connected to John Reid and the washing of the mermaid; a ghost is not just a generic spectre, but part of Cromarty’s own haunted domestic memory.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://electricscotland.com/history/cromarty/index.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: electricscotland.com">[electricscotland.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">electricscotland.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on electricscotland.com.</span></span></span>

## Cromarty Childhood and Oral Tradition

Miller grew up in a world where spoken memory still carried weight. His father, a ship’s captain, died when Miller was young, and biographical accounts emphasise the role of family, uncles, schooling, apprenticeship and self-education in forming him. His later life as a stonemason also mattered: working and travelling gave him access to quarries, shorelines and working people’s stories, while his fascination with fossils trained him to notice traces embedded in place.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.undiscoveredscotland.co.uk/usbiography/m/hughmiller.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: undiscoveredscotland.co.uk">[undiscoveredscotland.co.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">undiscoveredscotland.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on undiscoveredscotland.co.uk.</span></span></span>

The key point for folklore is that Miller belonged to both oral and print culture. He had heard local tales in the social world where they still functioned as memory, warning, entertainment and explanation, but he also had the literary skill to turn them into durable prose. Friends of Hugh Miller note that he was known locally as a storyteller, while modern heritage bodies still present him as a “folklorist” alongside his better-known identities as fossil hunter, geologist, writer and campaigner.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.thefriendsofhughmiller.org.uk/hugh-miller-a-brief-history" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: thefriendsofhughmiller.org.uk">[thefriendsofhughmiller.org.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">thefriendsofhughmiller.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on thefriendsofhughmiller.org.uk.</span></span></span>

This double identity is why Miller is useful but must be read carefully. He did not record tales like a modern fieldworker with audio equipment, informant notes and strict separation between narrator and editor. He retold, arranged, moralised and dramatised. Yet for Cromartyshire that is precisely the point: he preserves the old local material in the form through which many nineteenth-century readers actually encountered it. His book is not raw oral tradition; it is oral tradition filtered through a local literary mind.

## What *Scenes and Legends* Preserved

*Scenes and Legends of the North of Scotland* is subtitled *The Traditional History of Cromarty*, and that subtitle is revealing. Miller was not writing a narrow ghost-book or a modern folklore index. He treated Cromarty’s past as a mixture of landscape observation, local memory, supernatural belief, family tradition, religious history, political recollection and everyday custom. The 1835 edition is catalogued with the subjects “Legends — Scotland” and “Cromarty”, and later editions and digital versions preserve a wide-ranging table of contents that shows how broad Miller’s idea of tradition was.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/details/sceneslegendsofn00mill" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Internet ArchiveScenes and legends of the north of Scotland...14 Aug 2008 — Scenes and legends of the north of Scotland, or The traditio...</span></span></span>

Among the most important folklore material are the chapters dealing with “Remains of the Old Mythology”, Halloween rites, charms, “extinct spectres”, Morial’s Den and the Guardian Cock. Later chapters include Tarbat Ness and Stine Bheag, the Mermaid, the Dropping Cave, Fiddler’s Well, the Churchyard Ghost and the Green Lady. This makes the book a compact archive of Cromartyshire motifs: witches, sea spirits, ghosts, holy or uncanny places, omens, charms and seasonal rites all appear within one strongly local frame.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://electricscotland.com/history/cromarty/index.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: electricscotland.com">[electricscotland.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">electricscotland.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on electricscotland.com.</span></span></span>

For a reader interested in local mystery, the range is striking. Miller does not give Cromarty a single “signature monster”. Instead, he shows a town whose folklore grew from overlapping pressures: danger at sea, religious change, old Catholic and Reformed memory, weather anxiety, poverty, epidemics, clan and estate history, market life, childhood fright, and the uneasy border between natural explanation and supernatural interpretation.

That last border is especially important in Miller’s case. He became one of Scotland’s best-known popularisers of geology, and modern scientific accounts still stress his fossil work around Cromarty and the Old Red Sandstone. Yet the same man preserved stories in which stones, caves, wells, sea creatures and strange weather still carried legendary meanings. His legacy therefore sits at a fascinating junction: Cromarty as a place of scientific observation and Cromarty as a place of inherited wonder.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.scottishgeologytrust.org/geology/scotlands-geology/famous-scottish-geologists/hugh-miller/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: scottishgeologytrust.org">[Scottish Geology Trust]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">scottishgeologytrust.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">hugh miller</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">hugh miller</span></span></span>

## The Best-Known Tales Miller Kept Alive

Miller’s Cromarty folklore legacy is easiest to grasp through the stories that still travel. These are not all “Cromarty town” tales in the strictest street-by-street sense, but they belong to the Cromartyshire and neighbouring Moray Firth world that Miller helped fix in print.

**Stine Bheag of Tarbat** is one of the clearest examples. The tale centres on a woman associated with Tarbat Ness and weather-working at sea. Modern scholarship on Scottish coastal witch belief treats the Stine Bheag legend as an eighteenth-century tale recorded from oral tradition by Miller, involving weather manipulation and dangerous incidents at sea.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://eprints.gla.ac.uk/152413/13/152413.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: eprints.gla.ac.uk">[Enlighten Publications]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">eprints.gla.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on gla.ac.uk.</span></span></span>

The story has lasted because it brings together several things Cromartyshire folklore does well: fishing crews, fear of storms, a morally charged woman with uncanny power, and the dangerous bargain between practical need and religious disapproval. A National Trust for Scotland children’s retelling still presents Stine Bheag as a witch near Tarbat Ness, showing how Miller’s nineteenth-century material continues to be softened, adapted and performed for modern audiences.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nts.org.uk/stories/fireside-tales-scottish-folklore-for-children" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nts.org.uk">[National Trust for Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nts.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">fireside tales scottish folklore for children</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Trust for ScotlandFireside tales: Scottish folklore for children28 Feb 2022 — Hear from children&#x27;s storyteller Lizzie McDougall...</span></span></span>

**The Mermaid** is another strong example. Miller’s contents place “The Mermaid”, “The Story of John Reid” and “The Washing of the Mermaid” together, signalling a sea-lore cluster rather than a decorative fairy-tale episode. The tale survives in modern Miller-themed storytelling, including National Trust material that foregrounds giants, witches and mermaids as part of his Scottish folklore repertoire.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://electricscotland.com/history/cromarty/index.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: electricscotland.com">[electricscotland.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">electricscotland.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on electricscotland.com.</span></span></span>

**The Guardian Cock, Morial’s Den and the Green Lady** show a different side of the collection. These are not grand national legends but localised hauntings, omens and uncanny places. Their value lies in how they preserve Cromarty’s small-scale supernatural geography: dens, mills, shore lights, churchyards and houses become memory sites. Modern local resources such as Seaboard Stories continue to index Miller’s tales by episode and page reference, showing that his book still functions as a usable map of story fragments for readers, teachers and storytellers.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.seaboardstories.co.uk/hugh-millers-stories" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: seaboardstories.co.uk">[Seaboard Stories]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">seaboardstories.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Seaboard Stories Hugh Miller&#x27;s Stories</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Seaboard StoriesHugh Miller&#x27;s Stories - Seaboard StoriesFishing At Tarbat Ness And Stine Bheag P281. **Kenneth Ore and second sight** connect Miller to a wider Highland prophetic tradition. His account of Kenneth Ore, a seer who could look through a white stone, later overlaps with the broader Brahan Seer tradition, although later writers and historians debate how much of that tradition is historical, literary or retrospective legend-making. For Cromartyshire, the important point is that Miller captured an early version of a Highland second-sight motif before later popular retellings hardened it into a more famous prophetic cycle.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brahan_Seer" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Brahan Seer</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Brahan Seer</span></span></span>

## How Reliable Is Miller as Folklore Evidence?

Miller is indispensable, but he should not be treated as a transparent window onto an ancient past. His work is best understood as nineteenth-century literary folklore: rooted in oral tradition, but shaped for print. He selected material, arranged it into chapters, used narrative suspense and moral contrast, and wrote with the assumptions of a Protestant, self-educated Victorian intellectual.

That does not weaken his value. In some ways it makes the evidence more interesting. Miller shows not only what Cromarty people remembered, but how an early nineteenth-century Cromarty writer thought those memories should be interpreted. He often moves between belief and scepticism, giving space to old supernatural claims while also placing them in a reflective, historical or moral frame. This helps modern readers distinguish between the tale as tradition and the tale as proof. The former is Miller’s strength; the latter is not what his book can honestly provide.

The publication history also matters. *Scenes and Legends* appeared in 1835, drew attention to Miller beyond Cromarty, and was reprinted in later editions. HathiTrust records later nineteenth-century editions, while Project Gutenberg now makes an 1869 edition widely accessible. That chain of republication helped stabilise Cromarty folklore in print: once Miller’s versions circulated, later readers often met the tales through him rather than through living oral performance.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/details/sceneslegendsofn00mill" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[archive.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Internet ArchiveScenes and legends of the north of Scotland...14 Aug 2008 — Scenes and legends of the north of Scotland, or The traditio...</span></span></span>

This creates a useful caution for modern retellings. A story being “from Cromarty” may sometimes mean “from Miller’s Cromarty”, not necessarily a tale independently collected from many later informants. That is still a real tradition, but it is a tradition mediated by a powerful local author. In Cromartyshire folklore, Miller is not merely a witness; he is part of the transmission system.

## The Museum Keeps the Folklore Local

Hugh Miller’s Birthplace Cottage and Museum in Cromarty is central to how his folklore legacy is remembered today. The National Trust for Scotland describes the site as the home of an “inspirational geologist, folklorist and fossil hunter”, and highlights the thatched cottage, Miller’s fossils, tools, manuscripts and garden. This matters because it keeps folklore attached to a real townscape rather than reducing Miller to a name on an old book cover.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nts.org.uk/visit/places/hugh-millers-birthplace" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nts.org.uk">[National Trust for Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nts.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Trust for ScotlandHugh Miller&#x27;s Birthplace Cottage &amp; MuseumHumble abode of inspirational geologist, folklorist and fossil hunter...</span></span></span>

The museum also helps visitors understand the whole Miller, not just the folklore collector. His fossil work, stonemasonry, manuscripts, religious life and writing career sit alongside the tales. That combined presentation is especially fitting for Cromarty, where landscape and story are so closely linked. The same shore that yielded fossils also generated sea legends; the same town that produced a geologist preserved ghost stories and witchcraft tales.

Friends of Hugh Miller add another layer by supporting the museum, acquisitions and public memory. Their work includes collecting Miller-related books and objects and promoting his legacy in Cromarty. This kind of local stewardship matters for folklore because tales survive not only in texts, but in institutions, volunteer groups, walks, talks and seasonal events that give people reasons to keep retelling them.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.thefriendsofhughmiller.org.uk/what-we-do" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: thefriendsofhughmiller.org.uk">[thefriendsofhughmiller.org.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">thefriendsofhughmiller.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on thefriendsofhughmiller.org.uk.</span></span></span>

Recent heritage activity shows that Miller remains a living cultural figure rather than a sealed Victorian monument. In 2025 the National Trust for Scotland reported a partnership with Cromarty Arts Trust to reimagine Hugh and Lydia Miller through new forensic facial depictions for display at the birthplace museum. That project was not specifically a folklore edition, but it shows the continuing effort to make Miller present and recognisable to modern visitors.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nts.org.uk/stories/forensic-artist-reimagines-the-millers" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nts.org.uk">[National Trust for Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nts.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">forensic artist reimagines the millers</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">forensic artist reimagines the millers</span></span></span>

## Modern Retellings: From Fireside to Family Heritage

Miller’s folklore now reaches readers and listeners through formats he could not have imagined: museum programming, online story pages, education resources, local heritage websites and festival-style storytelling. National Trust for Scotland’s “Fireside tales” project, created during Scotland’s Year of Stories in 2022, explicitly presented children’s storyteller Lizzie McDougall bringing four of Miller’s Scottish tales to life, including stories of giants, witches and mermaids.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nts.org.uk/stories/fireside-tales-scottish-folklore-for-children" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nts.org.uk">[National Trust for Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nts.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">fireside tales scottish folklore for children</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Trust for ScotlandFireside tales: Scottish folklore for children28 Feb 2022 — Hear from children&#x27;s storyteller Lizzie McDougall...</span></span></span>

This is a good example of modern folklore transmission. The old tale is not simply copied word for word. It is adapted for children, softened in tone, performed aloud and framed as heritage entertainment. In that process, Stine Bheag can become less terrifying, the mermaid more storybook-like, and the darker religious and social tensions less prominent. The tale survives, but its emotional temperature changes.

Other projects keep Miller’s stories connected to place and education. Cromarty Courthouse Museum’s learning material links Miller with Cromarty’s fossil beds and local story origins, while Seaboard Stories gives accessible references to episodes from *Scenes and Legends*, including ships seen on land, fairy-related material, Stine Bheag, the mermaid and the black dog.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cromartymuseum.org.uk/learning/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cromartymuseum.org.uk">[Cromarty Museum]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cromartymuseum.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cromartymuseum.org.uk.</span></span></span>

The Scottish Geology Trust has also used Miller as a bridge between science and folklore. A 2025 exhibition at Hugh Miller’s Birthplace, “Stories in Stone – Fossils and their Folklore”, invited visitors to explore older superstitious explanations for fossils before geology offered scientific answers. That is exactly the kind of boundary Miller’s own career crossed: he preserved a wonder-filled local imagination while helping popularise evidence-based geology.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.scottishgeologytrust.org/event/stories-in-stone-fossils-and-their-folklore/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: scottishgeologytrust.org">[Scottish Geology Trust]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">scottishgeologytrust.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on scottishgeologytrust.org.</span></span></span>

## Why His Legacy Shapes Cromartyshire Identity

Hugh Miller gives Cromartyshire folklore a rare documentary anchor. Many historic counties have scattered legends known from late guidebooks, antiquarian snippets or tourist retellings. Cromartyshire has a named local writer, a dated nineteenth-century source, a surviving museum, and a continuing chain of retelling. That makes the county’s folklore unusually legible for modern readers.

His importance also lies in scale. Miller did not only preserve spectacular supernatural tales. He preserved a texture of local life: markets, widows, ministers, fishermen, caves, wells, storms, quarrels, illnesses, family memories and old houses. Folklore here is not a separate fantasy layer pasted over history. It is the way a town remembered danger, character, morality and place.

For Cromartyshire as a historic county, Miller helps solve a problem created by geography. Because the county’s boundaries were fragmented and later administrative language often folded the area into Ross and Cromarty, Cromartyshire can be hard to imagine as a folklore unit. Miller’s work recentres Cromarty itself. From that centre, the stories radiate naturally to Tarbat Ness, the Moray Firth, Easter Ross and the Black Isle without losing their local voice.

His legacy should therefore be read in three linked ways:

* **As evidence:** *Scenes and Legends* is the key nineteenth-century source for Cromarty’s traditional story world.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/details/sceneslegendsofn00mill" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Internet ArchiveScenes and legends of the north of Scotland...14 Aug 2008 — Scenes and legends of the north of Scotland, or The traditio...</span></span></span>
* **As literature:** Miller shaped oral tradition into memorable prose, so his versions are crafted retellings rather than untouched transcripts.
* **As modern memory:** museums, local groups, schools and storytellers continue to use Miller as the main gateway into Cromarty folklore.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nts.org.uk/visit/places/hugh-millers-birthplace" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nts.org.uk">[National Trust for Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nts.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Trust for ScotlandHugh Miller&#x27;s Birthplace Cottage &amp; MuseumHumble abode of inspirational geologist, folklorist and fossil hunter...</span></span></span>

</div>

That combination is why Hugh Miller still shapes Cromarty folklore. He did not invent Cromarty’s legendary landscape, but he gave it a durable form. Without him, Stine Bheag, the mermaid, the Guardian Cock, the Green Lady and many smaller fragments of Cromartyshire tradition would be harder to locate, harder to verify and far easier to forget.

      var reference = link.getAttribute('data-ebay-reference') || '';
      link.setAttribute('href', buildUrl(query, reference, marketId));
    });
    var select = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-select]');
    if (select && available.indexOf(marketId) >= 0) select.value = marketId;
    var selectedText = marketId;
    if (select && select.selectedIndex >= 0) selectedText = select.options[select.selectedIndex].text;
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-selected-market-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = selectedText;
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-selected-market-flag], [data-ebay-trigger-market-flag]')).forEach(function (flag) {
      flag.className = flagClass(marketId);
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-trigger-market-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = selectedText;
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-domain-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = domainLabel(marketId);
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-option]')).forEach(function (button) {
      button.setAttribute('aria-selected', button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-option') === marketId ? 'true' : 'false');
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      var active = button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button') === marketId;
      button.setAttribute('aria-pressed', active ? 'true' : 'false');
    });
    section.setAttribute('data-ebay-selected-market', marketId);
    if (persist) {
      try { if (window.localStorage) localStorage.setItem('phoenix-ebay-market', marketId); } catch (err) {}
    }
    closeMenu(section);
  }
  sections.forEach(function (section) {
    if (section.getAttribute('data-ebay-localized-init') === '1') return;
    section.setAttribute('data-ebay-localized-init', '1');
    var available = availableMarkets(section);
    var marketId = defaultMarket;
    if (window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation && window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation.inferMarket) {
      marketId = window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation.inferMarket(available, {
        storageKey: 'phoenix-ebay-market',
        defaultMarket: defaultMarket
      });
    } else if (available.indexOf(defaultMarket)< 0) {
      marketId = available[0] || defaultMarket;
    }
    var select = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-select]');
    if (select) {
      select.addEventListener('change', function () {
        applyMarket(section, select.value, true);
      });
    }
    var trigger = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-trigger]');
    var menu = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-menu]');
    if (trigger && menu) {
      trigger.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
        event.stopPropagation();
        var expanded = trigger.getAttribute('aria-expanded') === 'true';
        menu.hidden = expanded;
        trigger.setAttribute('aria-expanded', expanded ? 'false' : 'true');
        var picker = trigger.closest('.fr-ebay-market-picker');
        if (picker) picker.classList.toggle('fr-ebay-market-picker--open', !expanded);
      });
      Array.prototype.slice.call(menu.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-option]')).forEach(function (button) {
        button.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
          event.stopPropagation();
          applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-option'), true);
        });
      });
      document.addEventListener('click', function () { closeMenu(section); });
    }
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      button.addEventListener('click', function () {
        applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button'), true);
      });
    });
    applyMarket(section, marketId, false);
  });
})();
