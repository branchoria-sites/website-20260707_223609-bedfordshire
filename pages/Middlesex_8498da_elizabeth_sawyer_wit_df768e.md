## Introduction

Elizabeth Sawyer’s case is one of [Middlesex]({{ 'middlesex/' | relative_url }})’s clearest examples of how witchcraft belief moved from parish quarrel to courtroom record, printed pamphlet, stage play and lasting local legend. Sawyer lived at Edmonton, then a parish and town in historic Middlesex, now usually understood as part of north London. In April 1621 she was tried at the Old Bailey, convicted of witchcraft in connection with the death of her neighbour Agnes Ratcleife, and hanged at Tyburn on 19 April. The story survived because Henry Goodcole, Newgate prison chaplain, published an authorised pamphlet about her, and because Thomas Dekker, John Ford and William Rowley quickly turned the case into the Jacobean play *The Witch of Edmonton*.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://earlymodernscribbling.com/2021/06/29/the-witch-of-edmonton-ballad-mongers-and-early-modern-fake-news/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: earlymodernscribbling.com">[earlymodernscribbling.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">earlymodernscribbling.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Early Modern Scribbling The Witch of Edmonton, Ballad-Mongers, and Early</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Early Modern Scribbling The Witch of Edmonton, Ballad-Mongers, and Early</span></span></span>



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/Middlesex_8498da_elizabeth_sawyer_wit_df768e-overview.webp" | relative_url }}" alt="Overview image for Witches" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

For folklore readers, Sawyer matters not because the supernatural claims can be accepted at face value, but because the case shows a local legend being made in real time. A poor, disliked woman, a quarrel over animals and household goods, a fatal illness, a supposed devil-dog familiar, a prison confession, a public execution and a London theatre all helped make “the Witch of Edmonton” one of Middlesex’s best-documented witch stories.

## Elizabeth Sawyer and the Edmonton accusation

Edmonton belonged firmly to Middlesex in Sawyer’s time. Wikishire describes Edmonton as a town in eastern Middlesex near the Essex border, while Victoria County History’s account of Edmonton places the parish on the Lea-side edge of the county, with the River Lea forming part of the eastern boundary.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://wikishire.co.uk/wiki/Edmonton" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wikishire.co.uk">[Wikishire]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wikishire.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wikishire.co.uk.</span></span></span> That geography matters because the story is often filed under “London witchcraft”, but its local setting was a Middlesex parish within reach of London’s courts, prisons, printers and playhouses.

The main accusation centred on Agnes Ratcleife, Sawyer’s neighbour. Later summaries of the case, drawing on Goodcole’s pamphlet, describe a small domestic quarrel: one of Sawyer’s sows was said to have eaten or licked Ratcleife’s soap; Ratcleife struck the animal; Sawyer threatened that the blow would prove costly; Ratcleife then fell ill and died.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://kids.kiddle.co/Elizabeth_Sawyer" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: kids.kiddle.co">[Kiddle]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">kids.kiddle.co</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Elizabeth Sawyer Facts for Kids</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Elizabeth Sawyer Facts for Kids</span></span></span> In an early modern village, that sequence could be read as more than coincidence. Curses, grudges, animal sickness and sudden human illness were the everyday materials out of which witchcraft accusations were built.

Sawyer was not presented as a mysterious outsider who arrived fully formed as a legendary witch. She was remembered as an elderly, poor and marginal woman, already suspected by neighbours before the formal charge. Early modern witch accusations in England often attached themselves to people who were socially vulnerable but verbally defiant: women who begged, quarrelled, cursed or were thought to have a dangerous “bad tongue”. The University of Toronto’s *Early Theatre* article on *The Witch of Edmonton* stresses that Sawyer’s stage character is defined by lack of control: old, poor, at odds with neighbours, and reduced to the power of speech, curses and reputation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://jps.library.utoronto.ca/index.php/eth/article/download/31989/24419/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: jps.library.utoronto.ca">[Journal Production Services]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">jps.library.utoronto.ca</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Journal Production Services</span></span></span>

That is the core of the Middlesex folklore value of the case. Sawyer’s “witchcraft” was not an isolated marvel story about magic in the countryside. It grew from the social life of a parish: household goods, livestock, resentment, poverty, gossip and the belief that words spoken in anger could damage bodies, crops and animals.

## How suspicion became a trial

Sawyer was tried at the Old Bailey on 14 April 1621 and executed five days later, on 19 April. Modern accounts agree that the surviving record is not a full neutral trial transcript. The most important near-contemporary source is Henry Goodcole’s *The wonderfull discouerie of Elizabeth Savvyer a witch late of Edmonton*, published in 1621. The Oxford Text Archive identifies Goodcole as the author, gives the date of publication as 1621, and preserves the title’s claim that the pamphlet describes Sawyer’s conviction, condemnation, death and the Devil’s access to her.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ota.bodleian.ox.ac.uk/repository/xmlui/handle/20.500.12024/A01874" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ota.bodleian.ox.ac.uk">[ota.bodleian.ox.ac.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ota.bodleian.ox.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on ox.ac.uk.</span></span></span>

Goodcole was not simply a detached reporter. He was a minister and Newgate visitor who prepared condemned prisoners for death. The Grub Street Project records the pamphlet as a London publication, printed for William Butler and sold in Fleet Street, with a woodcut illustration on the title page and the running title “The wonderfull discouery of the witch of Edmonton”.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.grubstreetproject.net/publications/S117575/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: grubstreetproject.net">[Grub Street Project]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">grubstreetproject.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on grubstreetproject.net.</span></span></span> In other words, Sawyer’s case entered print through the world of crime pamphlets: moral warning, courtroom digest, religious lesson and commercial curiosity all at once.

Goodcole also wrote defensively. A scholarly study of the play notes that he presented his account as a correction to “base and false” ballads already circulating after the execution, including exaggerated tales of bewitched corn, animal spirits and strange prison events.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://jps.library.utoronto.ca/index.php/renref/article/download/8689/5656" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: jps.library.utoronto.ca">[Journal Production Services]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">jps.library.utoronto.ca</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Journal Production Servicesren25_2.pdf</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Journal Production Servicesren25_2.pdf</span></span></span> That detail is crucial. The legend of Elizabeth Sawyer did not begin centuries later with antiquarians or ghost writers. It was already being contested in 1621, with ballads, pamphlets and theatre competing to say what had “really” happened.

The evidence that convicted her was shaped by assumptions now rightly treated with scepticism. Reports of a witch’s mark, a familiar spirit and a confession belonged to the legal and demonological culture of the time, not to modern standards of proof. One study notes that Goodcole’s pamphlet pressed Sawyer for explicit details about the Devil in dog form and the bodily mark where the Devil was said to suck her blood.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://jps.library.utoronto.ca/index.php/renref/article/download/8689/5656" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: jps.library.utoronto.ca">[Journal Production Services]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">jps.library.utoronto.ca</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Journal Production Servicesren25_2.pdf</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Journal Production Servicesren25_2.pdf</span></span></span> Another article points out that the play itself makes visible contemporary doubts about witchcraft’s reality, even while using the language of devils and familiars.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://jps.library.utoronto.ca/index.php/eth/article/download/31989/24419/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: jps.library.utoronto.ca">[Journal Production Services]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">jps.library.utoronto.ca</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Journal Production Services</span></span></span>

The result is a case that is both well-attested and deeply unstable. We know Sawyer was tried, convicted and executed. We know how Goodcole wanted readers to understand her. We know the theatre rapidly reshaped her into “Mother Sawyer”. What we do not have is Sawyer’s life in her own unfiltered voice.

## Court records beyond the famous execution

The phrase “witch trial” can make Sawyer’s case sound like a single dramatic courtroom scene, but the surviving trail is more scattered. The famous execution is the end point of a process that moved through parish suspicion, local complaint, magistrate action, Newgate imprisonment, Old Bailey trial, prison examination, printed confession and public hanging.

Goodcole’s pamphlet is the central document because ordinary court paperwork for Sawyer’s trial has not survived in a way that gives us a full verbatim record. A Writ in the Margins teaching guide therefore warns that what is known about Sawyer is overwhelmingly drawn from Goodcole’s pamphlet, written by a member of the prison ministry after her conviction.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://writinthemargins.com/wp-content/uploads/2022/07/witm-ep-guide-1.7-the-witch-of-edmonton.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: writinthemargins.com">[Writ in the Margins Podcast]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">writinthemargins.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Writ in the Margins Podcast Wit M Ep Guide</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Writ in the Margins Podcast Wit M Ep Guide</span></span></span> That does not make the pamphlet useless. It makes it a source to be read carefully: it preserves names, accusations and beliefs, while also arranging them into a moral drama.

The case also shows how Middlesex witchcraft entered London’s justice system. Edmonton was local and parish-based; the trial and execution were metropolitan. Sawyer’s story moved from the northern Middlesex edge of the Lea valley to Newgate Prison, the Old Bailey and Tyburn. London Dungeon’s public-facing retelling, though theatrical in its own right, keeps this geography clear: Sawyer was convicted in 1621 and hanged at Tyburn, near modern Marble Arch.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.thedungeons.com/london/information/news/who-was-elizabeth-sawyer-anniversary-of-her-death/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: thedungeons.com">[The Dungeons]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">thedungeons.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Dungeons Who is Elizabeth Sawyer and why is she considered one</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Dungeons Who is Elizabeth Sawyer and why is she considered one</span></span></span>

The strongest reader takeaway is that Middlesex witchcraft was often preserved through institutions rather than through purely oral tale-collecting. Sawyer’s legend passed through law, prison chaplaincy, print and drama. This makes it unusually traceable, but also means the surviving version is shaped by powerful voices: male clergy, judges, printers, playwrights and later collectors.

## The devil-dog familiar

The most memorable folkloric image in the Sawyer story is the devil appearing as a dog. In Goodcole’s account, the Devil was said to have come to Sawyer in animal form; in the play, this becomes the black dog Tom, a speaking familiar who tempts, serves, mocks and finally abandons her. Early Modern Scribbling summarises the play as a tragi-comedy centred on the Devil in the form of a black dog named Tom, with three plotlines: Sawyer’s witchcraft, Frank Thorney’s domestic tragedy and Cuddy Banks’s comic encounters with the Devil.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://earlymodernscribbling.com/2021/07/10/witchcraft-demonism-and-agency-in-the-witch-of-edmonton-1621/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: earlymodernscribbling.com">[Early Modern Scribbling]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">earlymodernscribbling.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">witchcraft demonism and agency in the witch of edmonton 1621</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">witchcraft demonism and agency in the witch of edmonton 1621</span></span></span>

This dog matters because it connects Sawyer’s case to a wider English supernatural pattern. Black dogs in folklore can be omens, guardians, devils, road-haunters or churchyard beasts, depending on place and telling. In Sawyer’s case, the dog is not a free-roaming ghost of a lane or bridge, but a familiar: a demonic helper tied to witchcraft accusation. That makes it different from later Middlesex and London spectral-dog stories, but close enough in image to sit naturally beside them in a county folklore map.

The play’s dog is more than a monster. He exposes the moral structure of the drama. Early Modern Scribbling notes that the Devil’s presence links the plots but also makes witchcraft part of a wider landscape of sin, pressure and responsibility. The dog can tempt, mislead and exploit, but the play repeatedly shows that human cruelty, greed and sexual misconduct are already active without him.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://earlymodernscribbling.com/2021/07/10/witchcraft-demonism-and-agency-in-the-witch-of-edmonton-1621/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: earlymodernscribbling.com">[Early Modern Scribbling]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">earlymodernscribbling.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">witchcraft demonism and agency in the witch of edmonton 1621</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">witchcraft demonism and agency in the witch of edmonton 1621</span></span></span>

That is why the devil-dog remains such a strong legend image. He gives the story a memorable supernatural figure, but he also makes the audience ask who is really doing harm: the accused witch, the Devil, the neighbours who make her a witch, or the wider society that needs a scapegoat.

## The Witch of Edmonton and the making of a public legend

*The Witch of Edmonton* was first performed in 1621, the same year as Sawyer’s trial, conviction and execution. The play was written by Thomas Dekker, John Ford and William Rowley, staged by Prince Charles’s Men at the Cockpit Theatre, later performed at court, and published in 1658.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://earlymodernscribbling.com/2021/07/10/witchcraft-demonism-and-agency-in-the-witch-of-edmonton-1621/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: earlymodernscribbling.com">[Early Modern Scribbling]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">earlymodernscribbling.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">witchcraft demonism and agency in the witch of edmonton 1621</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">witchcraft demonism and agency in the witch of edmonton 1621</span></span></span> This speed is remarkable. A Middlesex court case became theatre almost immediately.

The play does not simply repeat Goodcole. It makes Sawyer more sympathetic and more troubling. In one of her most famous speeches, she asks why the world throws malice on her because she is poor, deformed and ignorant. The *Early Theatre* article quotes and analyses this opening speech, arguing that Sawyer is shown as someone taught to be a witch by the accusations constantly pressed upon her.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://jps.library.utoronto.ca/index.php/eth/article/download/31989/24419/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: jps.library.utoronto.ca">[Journal Production Services]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">jps.library.utoronto.ca</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Journal Production Services</span></span></span> That is a powerful dramatic turn: the community does not merely discover a witch; it helps manufacture one.

The play also widens the blame. Sawyer attacks not only village enemies but also corruption among elites, lawyers and fashionable city women. The same scholarly analysis notes that her courtroom speeches ask why the law looks at a poor old woman while ignoring more socially powerful forms of moral harm.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://jps.library.utoronto.ca/index.php/eth/article/download/31989/24419/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: jps.library.utoronto.ca">[Journal Production Services]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">jps.library.utoronto.ca</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Journal Production Services</span></span></span> This does not make the play modern in a simple sense. It still stages Sawyer as guilty within its world. But it gives her enough voice to make the accusation itself feel morally uncomfortable.

Another study describes the play’s treatment of Sawyer as ambivalent: it criticises the scapegoating of marginal women, yet also participates in the public punishment culture that condemned them.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://jps.library.utoronto.ca/index.php/renref/article/download/8689/5656" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: jps.library.utoronto.ca">[Journal Production Services]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">jps.library.utoronto.ca</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Journal Production Servicesren25_2.pdf</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Journal Production Servicesren25_2.pdf</span></span></span> That tension is exactly why the story endured. Sawyer is not only a “witch” for stage spectacle. She is a figure through whom the theatre explores poverty, gender, neighbourly cruelty, law and belief.

## What is old belief, and what is later retelling?

Sawyer’s story has several layers, and keeping them separate helps the legend make sense.

**The historical core** is the 1621 Edmonton case: Sawyer, Ratcleife, the accusation of witchcraft, the Old Bailey conviction, Newgate, Tyburn and Goodcole’s pamphlet. This is the best-attested layer, though filtered through hostile and institutional sources.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ota.bodleian.ox.ac.uk/repository/xmlui/handle/20.500.12024/A01874" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ota.bodleian.ox.ac.uk">[ota.bodleian.ox.ac.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ota.bodleian.ox.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on ox.ac.uk.</span></span></span>

**The popular print layer** includes ballads and pamphlet culture. Goodcole himself complained that false ballads were already spreading lurid claims about the case, which means Sawyer was becoming folklore before the ink was dry on the official account.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://jps.library.utoronto.ca/index.php/renref/article/download/8689/5656" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: jps.library.utoronto.ca">[Journal Production Services]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">jps.library.utoronto.ca</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Journal Production Servicesren25_2.pdf</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Journal Production Servicesren25_2.pdf</span></span></span>

**The theatrical layer** is *The Witch of Edmonton*, where the devil-dog Tom, Cuddy Banks, Morris dancing and the Frank Thorney plot transform a local trial into a complex stage world. The Royal Shakespeare Company’s synopsis still presents the play as based on supposedly real-life events in Edmonton, where a shunned old woman seeks revenge on her neighbours.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.rsc.org.uk/the-witch-of-edmonton" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: rsc.org.uk">[Royal Society of Chemistry]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">rsc.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on rsc.org.uk.</span></span></span>

**The antiquarian and image layer** includes later visual memorials. The Wellcome Collection holds a 1794 etching titled “Elizabeth Sawyer, of Edmonton, Middlesex, who was executed for witchcraft in 1621,” showing that she remained a recognisable figure for collectors and print audiences long after the trial.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://wellcomecollection.org/works/agg7sjee" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wellcomecollection.org">[Wellcome Collection]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wellcomecollection.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wellcomecollection.org.</span></span></span>

**The modern heritage layer** places Sawyer in books, theatre revivals, London attractions and local-history writing. These retellings often emphasise injustice, misogyny, poverty and the making of a scapegoat. They can be valuable, but they should not be mistaken for unmediated oral tradition from Edmonton itself.

## Why Sawyer belongs at the centre of Middlesex witchcraft

Elizabeth Sawyer’s case belongs at the centre of Middlesex witchcraft because it joins local place, legal process and public storytelling unusually tightly. Edmonton was not a remote village cut off from print and performance. It was a Middlesex community close enough to London for suspicion to become news, pamphlet, play and metropolitan spectacle.

The case also shows the county’s distinctive folklore pattern. Middlesex legends are often urbanising legends: stories caught between parish life and London publicity. Sawyer’s neighbours interpreted illness and misfortune through witchcraft belief; London’s courts turned that belief into a capital conviction; Goodcole turned it into moral print; the Cockpit Theatre turned it into drama; later collectors turned it into an image of “the Witch of Edmonton”.

That chain is why the story still matters. It is not simply a tale about whether a woman was believed to have a devil-dog. It is a record of how a community under pressure found a person to blame, how the law gave that blame deadly force, and how the machinery of early modern London transformed a Middlesex woman’s death into one of England’s most enduring witch legends.
