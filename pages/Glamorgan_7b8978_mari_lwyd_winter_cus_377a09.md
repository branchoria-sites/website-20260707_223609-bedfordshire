## Introduction

The Mari Lwyd is a south Wales winter visiting custom in which a decorated horse skull, carried under a white cloth, comes to doors with singers, comic attendants and a demand to be let in. It matters in [Glamorgan]({{ 'where-glamorgans-legends-still-meet-the/' | relative_url }}) because the county is one of the strongest recorded homes of the custom: not just a place where the Mari Lwyd is now performed for visitors, but one of the landscapes where it was collected, remembered, revived and turned into a modern emblem of Welsh winter folklore. Museum Wales describes it as a Christmas-season custom once popular in nineteenth-century south Wales, involving a real horse skull with ribbons, rosettes, glass-bottle eyes, a snapping jaw and a hidden carrier beneath a long white cloth.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://museum.wales/articles/1187/Christmas-Traditions-The-Mari-Lwyd/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: museum.wales">[Museum Wales]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">museum.wales</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on museum.wales.</span></span></span>



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/Glamorgan_7b8978_mari_lwyd_winter_cus_377a09-overview.webp" | relative_url }}" alt="Overview image for Mari Lwyd" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

For a first-time reader, the central point is simple: the Mari Lwyd is not just a “creepy horse skull”. It is a social performance. The drama happens at the threshold between outside and inside, winter darkness and household warmth, visitors and hosts, old year and new. In Glamorgan, especially around places such as Llangynwyd, Bridgend, Cardiff district, Neath, St Fagans, Llantrisant and the Vale-and-valleys borderland, the custom shows how folklore survives when people keep singing it, staging it, arguing with it and adapting it.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mari_Lwyd" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Mari Lwyd</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Mari Lwyd</span></span></span>

## The horse skull, songs and doorstep contest

The Mari Lwyd figure is usually described as a horse skull fixed to a pole, decorated and carried by a person hidden under a sheet. The most memorable detail is the moving jaw: Museum Wales notes that the lower jaw was fixed on a spring so it could shut with a loud snap, while its glass-bottle eyes, ribbons and rosettes made the creature both festive and unsettling.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://museum.wales/articles/1187/Christmas-Traditions-The-Mari-Lwyd/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: museum.wales">[Museum Wales]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">museum.wales</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on museum.wales.</span></span></span> A Museum Wales collection record gives an unusually concrete example: a skull covered in cream stockinet, with white cotton sheeting, green glass eyes, ribbons, tinsel, straw, sequins, bells and a carrying handle.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://museum.wales/collections/online/object/6b367e94-a597-37d2-be93-8ea001c6d9da/Mari-Lwyd/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: museum.wales">[Museum Wales]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">museum.wales</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Wales Mari Lwyd</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wales Mari Lwyd</span></span></span>

That mixture is why the Mari Lwyd feels so different from a simple Christmas procession. It is a puppet, a mask, a visiting party and a moving piece of folk theatre. The person inside the cloth animates the skull, while companions sing, dance, bargain and make mischief. Museum Wales describes the older visiting group as usually all male, sometimes including named characters such as Punch and Judy, the Sergeant and the Merryman, depending on locality and the size of the party.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://museum.wales/articles/1187/Christmas-Traditions-The-Mari-Lwyd/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: museum.wales">[Museum Wales]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">museum.wales</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on museum.wales.</span></span></span> The Horniman Museum gives the same broad pattern: the horse goes from house to house with attendants, asks to enter through song, and is eventually admitted for food, drink and entertainment.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.horniman.ac.uk/story/the-mystery-of-mari-lwyd/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: horniman.ac.uk">[Horniman Museum and Gardens]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">horniman.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">the mystery of mari lwyd</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">the mystery of mari lwyd</span></span></span>

The crucial performance is the doorstep contest. The visitors sing to ask permission to enter; the householders answer back, often refusing at first. This sung exchange, commonly called a battle of rhyme and verse, is part of the custom’s real genius: the house is not simply “visited”, it has to be won. Wales.com describes the exchange as a ritual of rude rhymes or songs between the Mari Lwyd party and the people inside; if the Mari wins entry, the household is said to gain good luck for the year.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.wales.com/about/history-and-heritage/welsh-traditions-myths-and-legends/mari-lwyd" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wales.com">[Wales]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wales.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Mari Lwyd</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Mari Lwyd</span></span></span>

Museum Wales preserves a version of the Mari Lwyd song with an English rendering that begins with the visitors politely asking leave to sing, then warns that if no leave is granted the singers will explain their departure in song. That is a useful reminder that the custom was not only visual spectacle. It depended on memory, wit, timing and local singers who knew how to turn a cold winter door into a public contest.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://museum.wales/collections/folksongs/?id=2" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: museum.wales">[Museum Wales]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">museum.wales</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on museum.wales.</span></span></span>

The performance also had a practical edge. Like wassailing elsewhere in Britain, it linked good wishes, song, hospitality and seasonal exchange. The visitors hoped for food, ale or other welcome; the household gained the honour of receiving the Mari and joining the joke. The Horniman Museum places the Mari Lwyd within this wider house-to-house wassailing pattern, while noting its distinctive horse-skull form and Welsh festive setting.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.horniman.ac.uk/story/the-mystery-of-mari-lwyd/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: horniman.ac.uk">[Horniman Museum and Gardens]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">horniman.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">the mystery of mari lwyd</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">the mystery of mari lwyd</span></span></span> In Glamorgan terms, that makes the custom both uncanny and neighbourly: frightening enough to remember, sociable enough to repeat.

## Glamorgan’s place in the south Wales custom

The Mari Lwyd is a south Wales custom rather than a tradition confined to one modern county boundary, but Glamorgan has an especially strong claim in the evidence. Wales.com notes that the earliest written record is from J. Evans’s 1800 account, while also stressing that the tradition is best known for its practice in Glamorgan and Gwent.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.wales.com/about/history-and-heritage/welsh-traditions-myths-and-legends/mari-lwyd" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wales.com">[Wales]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wales.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Mari Lwyd</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Mari Lwyd</span></span></span> Folklore summaries drawing on E. C. Cawte’s mapping of ritual animal disguise traditions describe the Mari Lwyd as principally associated with Glamorgan, with a major share of recorded examples falling there, though the custom also reached Monmouthshire, Carmarthenshire and a few other Welsh locations.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mari_Lwyd" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Mari Lwyd</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Mari Lwyd</span></span></span>

Historic Glamorgan is important here because the custom does not sit neatly inside today’s administrative map. The county includes Cardiff and Swansea, the Vale, Gower, the Bridgend area, the old industrial valleys and places now described through different council names. The Mari Lwyd belongs particularly to that south Wales belt where valleys, villages, chapels, pubs and old visiting routes shaped winter sociability. That is why Llangynwyd near Maesteg, Llantrisant, Caerphilly, St Fagans, Cardiff district, Bridgend and Neath all matter in different ways: they are not random performance sites, but part of the Glamorgan and south-east Wales geography in which the custom was remembered and revived.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mari_Lwyd" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Mari Lwyd</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Mari Lwyd</span></span></span>

Llangynwyd is one of the strongest Glamorgan anchors. Accounts of the custom often return to the Llangynwyd and Maesteg area because it retained a living or revived association with the Mari Lwyd when many other local performances had faded. Folk Wales notes that Trefor M. Owen’s Welsh folk-custom work includes photographs of the Llangynwyd Mari Lwyd party from around 1908, while later local accounts connect the custom with the Old House and with remembered performers who could sustain the sung exchange.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.folk.wales/mari-lwyd-llangynwyd.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: folk.wales">[folk.wales]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">folk.wales</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on folk.wales.</span></span></span>

Other Glamorgan places show the custom’s uneven survival. A broad historical summary records that in 1909 Marie Trevelyan described the Llandaff Mari Lwyd as still annual, while the Llantwit Major Mari, which visited several Vale of Glamorgan locations, had become sporadic rather than yearly. The same summary notes that Iorwerth Peate, writing in 1935, still found the custom in the Cardiff district, Bridgend, Llangynwyd, Neath and other Glamorgan districts, including an observed Christmas Eve 1934 example in the Mumbles, Swansea.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mari_Lwyd" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Mari Lwyd</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Mari Lwyd</span></span></span>

That unevenness is part of the story. The Mari Lwyd was never a fixed “national ceremony” performed identically everywhere. It was a case family of related winter visiting practices: some with a full verbal contest, some with stock characters, some closer to wassailing, some centred on pubs rather than private houses, and some preserved later by museums, folk clubs and revival groups. Glamorgan’s role is not that every village had the same Mari, but that enough places kept records, memories and performances for the county to become one of the custom’s main historic homes.

## How old is it, and what can safely be said about its origins?

The Mari Lwyd is often described online as ancient, pagan or pre-Christian, but the evidence needs careful handling. The safest statement is that it is a well-attested south Wales winter custom from the modern historical record, especially the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, with possible older roots that cannot be proved in a simple way. Wales.com is cautious on this point: it notes proposed meanings such as “Grey Mare” and “Grey Mary”, mentions links people have made to pale horses in older Celtic and British mythology or to a nativity legend, but also says that pagan origins are impossible to prove.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.wales.com/about/history-and-heritage/welsh-traditions-myths-and-legends/mari-lwyd" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wales.com">[Wales]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wales.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Mari Lwyd</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Mari Lwyd</span></span></span>

That uncertainty does not make the custom less interesting. In fact, it makes it more useful as folklore. The Mari Lwyd sits at the meeting point of several things people in Wales cared about: winter visiting, improvised song, household luck, horse symbolism, Christmas and New Year hospitality, and the pleasure of controlled disorder. The horse skull may feel like a survival from deep antiquity, but the documented custom we can discuss responsibly is the performed winter visit: the skull, the singers, the challenge, the welcome and the local variations.

The name itself is part of the uncertainty. Museum Wales gives the common meaning as “Grey Mare”.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://museum.wales/articles/1187/Christmas-Traditions-The-Mari-Lwyd/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: museum.wales">[Museum Wales]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">museum.wales</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on museum.wales.</span></span></span> Other explainers note that “Grey Mary” has also been suggested, sometimes linking the figure to Christian storytelling around Mary and the nativity.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.horniman.ac.uk/story/the-mystery-of-mari-lwyd/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: horniman.ac.uk">[Horniman Museum and Gardens]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">horniman.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">the mystery of mari lwyd</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">the mystery of mari lwyd</span></span></span> The important thing for a public folklore page is not to force a single origin story. The Mari Lwyd can carry several meanings at once: a mare, a Mary-like figure, a luck-bringer, a comic intruder, a wassailing emblem and a winter threshold creature.

The stronger historical question is not “was it pagan?” but “who preserved it, and how was it performed?” Here the evidence is more grounded. Museum collections preserve physical examples; folk-song archives preserve words and melodies; local accounts attach performances to named Glamorgan places; and modern museums and groups restage the custom for new audiences. Museum Wales’s object record is especially valuable because it turns the Mari Lwyd from a vague legend into a made thing: cloth, stockinet, glass eyes, ribbons, tinsel, bells, wood and metal.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://museum.wales/collections/online/object/6b367e94-a597-37d2-be93-8ea001c6d9da/Mari-Lwyd/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: museum.wales">[Museum Wales]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">museum.wales</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Wales Mari Lwyd</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wales Mari Lwyd</span></span></span>

## Why the custom declined

The Mari Lwyd declined during the twentieth century, but not simply because people “stopped believing” in it. Museum Wales gives one practical reason: the contest was almost always sung and performed in Welsh, so the decrease in Welsh speakers made it harder for householders to answer the visiting group in the expected way.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://museum.wales/articles/1187/Christmas-Traditions-The-Mari-Lwyd/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: museum.wales">[Museum Wales]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">museum.wales</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on museum.wales.</span></span></span> This matters because the custom was not a passive display. Without people who could sing back, improvise, tease and hold the door, the central mechanism weakened.

There were also wider changes in social life. Older house-visiting customs depended on communities where winter evenings, pubs, chapels, farms, terraces and village routes created a shared seasonal stage. As work patterns, language use, religious attitudes and entertainment changed, the need and permission for such visits changed too. Historical summaries of the custom also point to opposition from some Christian clergy and changing social conditions as part of the decline.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mari_Lwyd" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Mari Lwyd</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Mari Lwyd</span></span></span>

The decline should not be imagined as a clean disappearance. Glamorgan’s evidence suggests patchiness: some places stopped, some became sporadic, some retained a memory, some carried on through particular families, pubs or groups, and some later revived the performance with a strong sense that it belonged locally. Llandaff, Llantwit Major, Llangynwyd, the Maesteg area and the Mumbles appear in historical references precisely because the tradition’s presence varied by place and period.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mari_Lwyd" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Mari Lwyd</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Mari Lwyd</span></span></span>

This is a useful lesson for reading county folklore more broadly. A custom can be “old” without being continuous everywhere. It can fade in one village, persist in another, be collected by a folklorist, be preserved in a museum, and then return as a deliberate revival. That does not make the modern Mari Lwyd fake. It means the tradition survives through a chain of performance, memory and re-use rather than through an unbroken script.

## Revival, performance and modern meaning

The modern Mari Lwyd is both a revival and a living performance. St Fagans National Museum of History in Cardiff is central to that story because it presents the custom in a public museum setting and keeps it visible as part of Wales’s Christmas-season heritage. Museum Wales has staged Mari Lwyd and Hunting of the Wren performances at St Fagans, describing them as two well-known Welsh Christmas customs involving singing, dancing and a horse’s skull.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://museum.wales/stfagans/whatson/12104/Christmas-Traditions-The-Mari-Lwyd-Performances/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: museum.wales">[Museum Wales]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">museum.wales</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on museum.wales.</span></span></span>

Llantrisant is another key revival site within historic Glamorgan’s cultural orbit. Folk Wales says Llantrisant’s Mari Lwyd custom was revived by members of the Llantrisant Folk Club, deliberately drawing on the style in which the custom was being performed elsewhere.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.folk.wales/the-mari-lwyd.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: folk.wales">[folk.wales]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">folk.wales</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on folk.wales.</span></span></span> This is important because it shows revival as an implementation choice, not a museum label. People had to decide how to build a Mari, what songs to use, how to handle the doorstep exchange, which houses or pubs to visit, and how much older mischief a modern audience would tolerate.

Llangynwyd shows a different kind of continuity: a local place where the Mari Lwyd is tied to pub memory, New Year visiting and the old social geography of the Llynfi Valley. Folk Wales’s Llangynwyd material records remembered performers and the custom’s relationship with the Old House, while more recent calendar-custom accounts still treat Llangynwyd as one of the places where people look for the Mari around New Year.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.folk.wales/mari-lwyd-llangynwyd.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: folk.wales">[folk.wales]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">folk.wales</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on folk.wales.</span></span></span> The local setting matters. A Mari Lwyd in a village lane or at a pub door does something different from a Mari on a stage: it reactivates the old threshold between host and visitor.

Modern performances often make the Mari Lwyd more family-friendly, more scheduled and more visible to visitors. That changes the custom, but it also protects it. A nineteenth-century Mari Lwyd might arrive late, loud and demanding; a museum or festival Mari must work within safeguarding, accessibility, event timing and mixed-language audiences. The gain is that more people can encounter the tradition. The loss is that some of the old uncertainty — will the Mari come, will the household answer, will the party be let in? — becomes harder to recreate.

Even so, the custom has kept its bite. The Mari Lwyd still works because it is visually unforgettable and socially active. It does not merely represent winter; it interrupts it. It asks for a response. It makes a room, street or pub doorway briefly theatrical. In a county such as Glamorgan, where industrial memory, Welsh-language history, urban growth and village identity overlap, that makes the Mari Lwyd a powerful local emblem: a tradition that can belong to old valleys communities, museum courtyards, folk clubs, pubs and contemporary Welsh culture at the same time.

## What the Mari Lwyd reveals about Glamorgan folklore

The Mari Lwyd reveals a side of Glamorgan folklore that is performed rather than only told. Many local legends are attached to stones, castles, wells, saints or haunted houses; the Mari Lwyd is attached to movement. It travels through streets, knocks at doors, sings for entry and turns hospitality into a contest. That makes it one of the county’s best examples of folklore as action.

It also links Glamorgan to neighbouring counties without losing the county focus. The custom belongs to south Wales more broadly, especially Glamorgan, Gwent and parts of Carmarthenshire, and it has parallels with other British hooded-animal and wassailing traditions. Wales.com compares it with customs such as Kentish hoodening, the Cotswold broad and the Old Tup in Derbyshire, all of which involve animal disguise, house visiting or winter exchange.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.wales.com/about/history-and-heritage/welsh-traditions-myths-and-legends/mari-lwyd" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wales.com">[Wales]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wales.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Mari Lwyd</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Mari Lwyd</span></span></span> The comparison helps, but the Glamorgan version has its own texture: Welsh song, the horse skull, the doorstep contest, and a strong revival line through places such as Llangynwyd, Llantrisant and St Fagans.

For readers exploring Glamorgan’s wider folklore, the Mari Lwyd also sits naturally beside other local traditions of thresholds and uncanny places. Fairy legends at ruined castles, ghost stories at old houses, saintly wells and winter visiting customs all ask similar questions: who may enter, who belongs, what old power lingers in a place, and how do communities retell it? The Mari Lwyd answers those questions not with a quiet tale, but with a snapping jaw and a song at the door.

That is why the custom has become one of the most recognisable images of Welsh winter folklore. It is strange enough to catch modern attention, but grounded enough to withstand scrutiny. Its power does not depend on proving a single ancient origin. It depends on the fact that people in Glamorgan and neighbouring parts of south Wales kept making, carrying, singing, resisting and welcoming the grey horse — and still do.
