## Introduction

Glastonbury became Avalon because its landscape was unusually ready to carry sacred stories. The Tor rises like an island above the [Somerset]({{ 'where-somerset-turns-into-legend/' | relative_url }}) Levels; the abbey was one of medieval England’s richest and most prestigious monasteries; and later writers, monks, pilgrims and visitors kept layering Arthurian, Christian and alternative spiritual meanings onto the same small cluster of places. The result is not one simple legend, but a dense Somerset storyscape in which King Arthur’s grave, Joseph of Arimathea, the Holy Thorn, the Grail, the Tor and the abbey ruins all reinforce one another.

The important distinction is between belief, story and evidence. Glastonbury Abbey’s monks claimed in 1191 to have found the bodies of Arthur and Guinevere, and that claim helped identify Glastonbury with the Isle of Avalon. Modern scholarship treats the discovery with scepticism, but the legend itself remains historically powerful because it shows how medieval religious houses could use story, relics and landscape to create meaning, attract pilgrims and shape regional identity. Glastonbury is therefore one of Somerset’s clearest examples of sacred story-making: a place where folklore is not just told about the land, but built into how people visit, interpret and remember it.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://research.reading.ac.uk/glastonburyabbeyarchaeology/myths/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: research.reading.ac.uk">[reading.ac.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">research.reading.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Research Reading Myths</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The monks found an inscribed lead cross in a grave with the bodies of a tall man...Read more...</span></span></span>

## Why this Somerset town could become Avalon

Glastonbury’s Avalon identity depends first on geography. Before modern drainage, the low-lying Somerset Levels often behaved like a watery inland world, with higher ground standing out as islands or peninsulas. The Tor’s isolated shape makes the old idea of an “isle” feel plausible to the imagination even when the modern visitor arrives by road. The National Trust notes the long tradition that Glastonbury is the Isle of Avalon, the place to which Arthur was taken after his last battle; it also records the abbey monks’ claim to have found his grave in 1191.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nationaltrust.org.uk/visit/somerset/glastonbury-tor/history-of-glastonbury-tor" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nationaltrust.org.uk">[National Trust]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nationaltrust.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Trust History and legends of Glastonbury Tor</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National TrustHistory and legends of Glastonbury Tor - SomersetJesus&#x27; uncle, Joseph of Arimathea. That landscape mattered because Avalon was not originally a clearly mapped Somerset address. In Geoffrey of Monmouth’s twelfth-century Arthurian account, Arthur is taken to Avalon, but the place is not securely fixed as Glastonbury. The decisive move came when Glastonbury’s medieval community connected an already famous abbey and a dramatic wetland hillscape with a national heroic myth. Once that link was made, the local geography seemed to confirm the story: the Tor became the island-like height, the abbey became the burial place, and the surrounding watery Levels became the memory of an older, enchanted terrain.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://research.reading.ac.uk/glastonburyabbeyarchaeology/digital/arthurs-tomb-c-1331/arthurian-myth/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: research.reading.ac.uk">[Research Reading]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">research.reading.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Research ReadingArthurian Myth - Glastonbury Abbey Archaeology - ResearchThe cross was inscribed “Here lies buried the renowned King Arth...</span></span></span>

This is why Glastonbury is so important within Somerset folklore. Many county legends attach to a single cave, tree, well or stone circle; Glastonbury gathers several kinds of sacred object and legendary claim into one walkable landscape. The visitor can move from the abbey ruins to the Tor, from the Holy Thorn tradition to Chalice Well, and from medieval Christian story to modern pilgrimage culture without leaving the town’s immediate mythic field. That closeness gives the legends unusual force.

## Arthur’s grave and the Isle of Avalon

The best-known Glastonbury legend is the claimed discovery of King Arthur’s grave at the abbey. In 1191, the monks announced that they had uncovered a grave containing two bodies, described as a tall man and a woman, along with an inscribed lead cross. The inscription identified the dead as Arthur and Guinevere and placed them “in the Isle of Avalon”, apparently giving written confirmation that Glastonbury and Avalon were the same place. Gerald of Wales, writing soon afterwards, gives the earliest account of the discovery.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://research.reading.ac.uk/glastonburyabbeyarchaeology/myths/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: research.reading.ac.uk">[Research Reading]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">research.reading.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Research Reading Myths</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The monks found an inscribed lead cross in a grave with the bodies of a tall man...Read more...</span></span></span>

The timing matters. Glastonbury Abbey had suffered a major fire in 1184, and the rebuilding of such a great religious house required money, prestige and renewed attention. Historic England records that by the time of the Domesday Book in 1086 Glastonbury was the wealthiest monastic house in England, and that its medieval fame as Arthur’s legendary burial place later attracted pilgrims alongside its claims to St Dunstan’s relics.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://historicengland.org.uk/listing/the-list/list-entry/1021077" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: historicengland.org.uk">[Historic England]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">historicengland.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Historic EnglandGlastonbury AbbeyThe abbey benefited in the medieval period from its fame as the legendary burial place of King Arthur; t...</span></span></span>

Modern readers should not treat the 1191 discovery as straightforward archaeological proof. The physical evidence has not survived in a form that can be tested, and modern historians and archaeologists are generally cautious or sceptical about the claim. The University of Reading’s Glastonbury Abbey Archaeology project explains that the lead cross’s style and wording look suspicious in relation to the supposed date of Arthur’s burial, while modern accounts of the abbey’s archaeology emphasise how easily earlier interpretation was shaped by the power of the legends themselves.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://research.reading.ac.uk/glastonburyabbeyarchaeology/digital/arthurs-tomb-c-1331/arthurian-myth/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: research.reading.ac.uk">[Research Reading]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">research.reading.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Research ReadingArthurian Myth - Glastonbury Abbey Archaeology - ResearchThe cross was inscribed “Here lies buried the renowned King Arth...</span></span></span>

Yet the story cannot be dismissed as merely a fake and therefore unimportant. For folklore, the crucial question is not only “Was Arthur really buried here?” but “What did the claim allow people to believe, do and remember?” It gave Somerset a central place in Britain’s Arthurian imagination. It turned Glastonbury Abbey into a destination for pilgrims and the curious. It also made Avalon visible: not a distant literary island, but a ruin, a grave-marker, a hill and a town that could be visited.

The legend also changes the emotional meaning of Arthur. In some traditions he is not simply dead, but hidden, sleeping or waiting to return. Glastonbury’s grave tradition pulls him in another direction: it gives the once-and-future king a tomb in a specific Somerset religious house. That tension between Arthur as sleeping hero and Arthur as buried monarch is part of the fascination. Glastonbury does not settle Arthurian myth; it localises it.

## Joseph of Arimathea and the Holy Thorn

Glastonbury’s sacred story-making is not only Arthurian. Its Christian legends are just as important, especially the tradition that Joseph of Arimathea came to Britain and founded, or inspired, the earliest Christian presence at Glastonbury. In the New Testament, Joseph is the wealthy follower of Christ who provides his own tomb for Jesus after the Crucifixion. Glastonbury tradition extends that biblical figure into Somerset, making him a missionary, pilgrim and bearer of sacred objects.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.glastonburyabbey.com/myths-and-legends.php" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: glastonburyabbey.com">[glastonburyabbey.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">glastonburyabbey.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on glastonburyabbey.com.</span></span></span>

The abbey promoted a claim that it developed from the first church in Britain, founded by Joseph and twelve followers in AD 63. William of Malmesbury, writing in the twelfth century, described the revered “Old Church” at Glastonbury as an ancient wattle building, later covered in wooden planks and roofed with lead. That does not prove Joseph’s personal presence, but it shows how Glastonbury’s medieval identity was built around antiquity: the abbey was not merely old, but imagined as the root of Christianity in Britain.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://research.reading.ac.uk/glastonburyabbeyarchaeology/myths/the-old-church/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: research.reading.ac.uk">[Research Reading]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">research.reading.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Research Reading The Old Church</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Research Reading The Old Church</span></span></span>

The Holy Thorn gives this claim a living, visible emblem. According to the legend, Joseph arrived at Avalon, climbed Wearyall Hill, thrust his staff into the ground, and by morning it had taken root as a miraculous thorn tree that flowered twice a year. Glastonbury Abbey’s own account notes that by the 1530s, shortly before the Dissolution of the Monasteries, three unusual thorn trees were growing on Wearyall Hill, flowering in spring around Easter and again at Christmas.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://research.reading.ac.uk/glastonburyabbeyarchaeology/myths/the-holy-thorn/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: research.reading.ac.uk">[Research Reading]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">research.reading.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Research Reading The Holy Thorn</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Research Reading The Holy Thorn</span></span></span>

That twice-flowering habit is central to the Thorn’s folklore. A legend about a saintly traveller becomes more persuasive when linked to a plant that behaves unusually. The tree is not just a prop in the story; it is a seasonal sign. At Christmas, the thorn’s blossom turns doctrine, wonder and local pride into something people can gather around, cut, display and pass on.

The Thorn also shows how Glastonbury legends survive through replacement and propagation. The original or claimed original tree has been damaged, cut down and replaced through cuttings and descendants. A pamphlet in Glastonbury Abbey’s collection, dating from around 1730, preserves an account of the Thorn legend and its association with Joseph, showing that the story had become part of the town’s printed devotional and antiquarian culture by the early eighteenth century.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.glastonburyabbey.com/collections/the-recovery-appeal-collection/holy-thorn-pamphlet.php" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: glastonburyabbey.com">[glastonburyabbey.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">glastonburyabbey.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on glastonburyabbey.com.</span></span></span>

A striking modern episode came in December 2010, when the Holy Thorn on Wearyall Hill was attacked and cut down. Contemporary reporting described local distress, prayers and decorations at the site, and the continued custom of sending a flowering sprig from a related tree to the monarch. The incident matters because it revealed how strongly a legendary tree could still function as a communal symbol: not simply as a tourist curiosity, but as a living emblem of Glastonbury’s Christian and local identity.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.theguardian.com/environment/2010/dec/09/glastonbury-mourns-felling-thorn-tree" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theguardian.com">[The Guardian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theguardian.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on theguardian.com.</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 9, 2010</span></span></span>

## How medieval monks made sacred history

Glastonbury’s legends are sometimes presented as if they are timeless survivals from an ancient Celtic past. The more interesting picture is more layered. The abbey’s story-world was shaped by medieval religious writing, monastic competition, relic culture, Arthurian romance, local topography and later antiquarian retelling. It is less a single inherited myth than a long process of sacred history-making.

The abbey had a practical reason to emphasise deep antiquity. Medieval monasteries competed for patronage, pilgrims, gifts and status. A house that could claim saints, relics, royal burials and apostolic origins stood out. Glastonbury had real historical importance, but its legendary claims magnified that importance into a sacred narrative: Joseph brought Christianity, Arthur lay buried there, the Old Church predated other English churches, and the landscape itself carried signs of holiness.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://research.reading.ac.uk/glastonburyabbeyarchaeology/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: research.reading.ac.uk">[Research Reading]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">research.reading.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Research Reading Glastonbury Abbey Archaeology</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Research Reading Glastonbury Abbey Archaeology</span></span></span>

John of Glastonbury’s fourteenth-century chronicle shows this process in action. His work drew on earlier writers such as William of Malmesbury and Adam of Damerham, but also wove together chronicle, hagiography and romance to construct a grand history of the abbey. Later Joseph traditions, including material associated with the mysterious figure Melkin, helped connect Joseph, Avalon, hidden relics and the Grail more tightly to Glastonbury’s sacred geography.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://memorireadinggroup.wordpress.com/tag/joseph-of-arimathea/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: memorireadinggroup.wordpress.com">[memorireadinggroup.wordpress.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">memorireadinggroup.wordpress.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wordpress.com.</span></span></span>

This does not mean every monk was cynically inventing stories from nothing. Medieval historical writing often worked differently from modern evidence-based history. Sacred truth, inherited authority, local memory, political need and symbolic geography could be blended. A place’s holiness was argued through documents, relics, miracles, tombs, ancient buildings and revered traditions. Glastonbury excelled at that blend.

The abbey’s later ruin added another layer. After the Dissolution of the Monasteries in the sixteenth century, the physical abbey ceased to function as a monastic institution, but the ruins became even more available to memory and imagination. Broken arches, empty spaces and lost tombs can be powerful folklore engines. They invite visitors to ask what once stood there, what was destroyed, and what may still be hidden beneath the grass.

## Pilgrimage, tourism and modern sacred meaning

Glastonbury’s legends did not end with the Middle Ages. They have been reworked through Anglican, Catholic, esoteric, New Age, artistic, ecological and tourist cultures. This is why the town feels different from many other legendary sites: it is not only a place where old stories are interpreted, but a place where new sacred meanings are actively made.

The abbey remains central to this. Its visitor material openly recognises that it is connected with legend to a degree “unparalleled by any other abbey in England”, especially through Joseph of Arimathea and King Arthur. That public framing is important. Glastonbury’s heritage presentation does not need to prove every legend literally true in order to explain why the legends matter. The stories are part of the site’s history because they shaped how people used, funded, visited and imagined the abbey.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.glastonburyabbey.com/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: glastonburyabbey.com">[glastonburyabbey.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">glastonburyabbey.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on glastonburyabbey.com.</span></span></span>

The Tor works differently. It is a visual and bodily experience: a climb, a skyline, a landmark visible from the Levels. The National Trust records traditions linking the Tor not only with Avalon and Joseph, but also with otherworldly or fairy associations, including stories of Gwyn ap Nudd, a ruler of the Otherworld in Welsh tradition. That widens Glastonbury’s sacred story beyond Christian and Arthurian frames into a broader British mythic landscape.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nationaltrust.org.uk/visit/somerset/glastonbury-tor/history-of-glastonbury-tor" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nationaltrust.org.uk">[National Trust]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nationaltrust.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Trust History and legends of Glastonbury Tor</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National TrustHistory and legends of Glastonbury Tor - SomersetJesus&#x27; uncle, Joseph of Arimathea. Chalice Well adds another modern layer. The Chalice Well Trust describes the site as a living sanctuary, a place of pilgrimage, rest, quiet contemplation, healing and spiritual activity open to people of all religious and spiritual paths. This is a good example of modern Glastonbury’s distinctive tone: Christian, Arthurian and alternative spiritual meanings do not simply replace one another, but coexist, sometimes uneasily and sometimes creatively.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://chalicewell.org.uk/about/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: chalicewell.org.uk">[Chalice Well Trust]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">chalicewell.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Chalice Well Trust About Us</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Chalice Well Trust About Us</span></span></span>

Modern pilgrimage routes make this layering explicit. The British Pilgrimage Trust’s Glastonbury route describes the town as a place of healing water and overlapping traditions, where stories from different religious, cultural and historical periods create spaces of encounter. That is a contemporary version of the same process that medieval Glastonbury used: linking movement through the landscape with story, devotion and interpretation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.britishpilgrimage.org/portfolio/glastonbury-pilgrimage-in-a-day" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: britishpilgrimage.org">[British Pilgrimage Trust]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">britishpilgrimage.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on britishpilgrimage.org.</span></span></span>

Tourism can flatten folklore into souvenirs, but in Glastonbury it also keeps the legends active. Visitors buy books, climb the Tor, attend services, meditate at wells, visit the abbey, follow waymarked walks and argue about what Avalon means. Some come for Christian pilgrimage, some for Arthur, some for goddess spirituality, some for local history, and some simply for the atmosphere. The town’s modern identity depends on that mixture.

## What is old tradition, and what is later invention?

A useful way to read Glastonbury is to separate its layers without stripping away their power.

**The landscape layer is older than the named legends.** The Tor, the Levels, springs and hill paths existed before Arthurian romance or Joseph traditions were attached to them. Their shape made the area unusually open to island, otherworld and sacred-centre stories.

**The medieval monastic layer gave the legends authority.** The abbey’s writers and community connected Glastonbury with Joseph, the Old Church, Arthur and Avalon. These claims served devotional, institutional and imaginative purposes, especially after the 1184 fire and during the age of relics and pilgrimage.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://research.reading.ac.uk/glastonburyabbeyarchaeology/myths/the-old-church/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: research.reading.ac.uk">[Research Reading]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">research.reading.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Research Reading The Old Church</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Research Reading The Old Church</span></span></span>

**The Arthurian layer is literary as well as local.** Avalon belongs to the wider Arthurian world, but Glastonbury’s grave claim gave the literary island a Somerset address. This is why the Glastonbury version is so influential: it fuses national myth with a specific county landscape.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://research.reading.ac.uk/glastonburyabbeyarchaeology/digital/arthurs-tomb-c-1331/arthurian-myth/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: research.reading.ac.uk">[Research Reading]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">research.reading.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Research ReadingArthurian Myth - Glastonbury Abbey Archaeology - ResearchThe cross was inscribed “Here lies buried the renowned King Arth...</span></span></span>

**The Joseph and Holy Thorn layer is devotional and botanical.** Joseph’s supposed journey to Glastonbury links biblical history with British Christian origins, while the Thorn turns that claim into a visible seasonal sign. The survival of Thorn descendants and customs shows how a legend can persist through living practice rather than through text alone.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://research.reading.ac.uk/glastonburyabbeyarchaeology/myths/the-holy-thorn/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: research.reading.ac.uk">[Research Reading]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">research.reading.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Research Reading The Holy Thorn</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Research Reading The Holy Thorn</span></span></span>

**The modern layer is participatory.** Contemporary Glastonbury is not just preserving medieval folklore. It is continually remaking Avalon through pilgrimage, spiritual tourism, festivals, retreats, interpretation boards, local shops, ceremonies and personal quests.

These layers sometimes contradict one another. A historically cautious account of Arthur’s grave may sit beside a devotional account of Joseph, a goddess-centred reading of the landscape, or a tourist map of sacred sites. But that is exactly why Glastonbury matters. Its folklore is not tidy; it is cumulative.

## Why Glastonbury still defines Somerset’s mythic identity

For Somerset, Glastonbury is more than a famous town with famous ruins. It is the county’s strongest example of a mythic landscape recognised far beyond the county boundary. Other Somerset traditions may be more local, humorous or eerie: the Witch of Wookey Hole, the Gurt Dog of the Quantocks, orchard wassailing, pixie stories, holy wells and stone-circle legends. Glastonbury operates on a larger symbolic scale because it attaches Somerset to the origin stories of Britain: Arthur, Avalon, the Grail and the coming of Christianity.

That scale can obscure the local place beneath the myth. The most grounded way to understand Glastonbury is not as a single answer to “Where was Avalon?” but as a Somerset landscape where people have repeatedly made sacred meaning from terrain, ruins, plants, water and story. The Tor gives height and visibility. The Levels give the memory of islandness. The abbey gives medieval authority. The Thorn gives seasonal wonder. The wells give living pilgrimage. Together, they make the town feel like a place where legend has a geography.

Glastonbury’s power also lies in its openness. A visitor can approach it as folklore, faith, history, literature, local identity or spiritual experiment. The same site can be read as a medieval monastic success story, an Arthurian invention, a Christian shrine, a modern pilgrimage centre, or a living example of how communities keep remaking tradition. None of those readings fully cancels the others.

That is why the question “Why did Glastonbury become Avalon?” has a richer answer than “because medieval monks said so”. It became Avalon because the claim met a landscape that could carry it, an abbey that could promote it, writers who could elaborate it, pilgrims who could travel to it, and later communities who found new meanings in it. In Somerset folklore, few places show more clearly how legend is made: not in a single moment, but by centuries of retelling around a hill, a ruin, a thorn tree and the idea of a sacred island.

    } else if (available.indexOf(defaultMarket)< 0) {
      marketId = available[0] || defaultMarket;
    }
    var select = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-select]');
    if (select) {
      select.addEventListener('change', function () {
        applyMarket(section, select.value, true);
      });
    }
    var trigger = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-trigger]');
    var menu = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-menu]');
    if (trigger && menu) {
      trigger.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
        event.stopPropagation();
        var expanded = trigger.getAttribute('aria-expanded') === 'true';
        menu.hidden = expanded;
        trigger.setAttribute('aria-expanded', expanded ? 'false' : 'true');
        var picker = trigger.closest('.fr-ebay-market-picker');
        if (picker) picker.classList.toggle('fr-ebay-market-picker--open', !expanded);
      });
      Array.prototype.slice.call(menu.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-option]')).forEach(function (button) {
        button.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
          event.stopPropagation();
          applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-option'), true);
        });
      });
      document.addEventListener('click', function () { closeMenu(section); });
    }
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      button.addEventListener('click', function () {
        applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button'), true);
      });
    });
    applyMarket(section, marketId, false);
  });
})();
