## Introduction

[Wigtownshire]({{ 'where-wigtownshires-hidden-stories-live/' | relative_url }})’s standing stones attract legends because they make ancient, half-understood places visible in the everyday landscape. Torhouse Stone Circle, just west of Wigtown in the Bladnoch Valley, is the clearest example: a prehistoric monument probably built around 4,000 years ago, later renamed in local tradition as “King Gauldus’s Tomb”. Its archaeology belongs to the Late Neolithic or Bronze Age, but its folklore belongs to a much later habit of turning mysterious stones into royal graves, battlefield memorials and proof of deep local identity. Historic Environment Scotland records Torhouse as a circle of 19 granite boulders, with three central stones noted in 1684 by Andrew Symson as King Gauldus’s Tomb.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.historicenvironment.scot/visit/all/torhouse-stone-circle/history-and-stories/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: historicenvironment.scot">[Historic Environment Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">historicenvironment.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-title">history and stories</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">It consists of 19 dumpy granite boulders in a 20m-wide circle. They&#x27;re graded in height...Read more...</span></span></span>



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/Wigtownshire_1186ed_torhouse_standing_st_f6c6a3-overview.webp" | relative_url }}" alt="Overview image for Stones" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

That difference between monument and story is the heart of Torhouse’s fascination. The stones are older than the recorded legend, older than any Roman-era “King Galdus”, and older than the county name traditions that later writers tried to attach to them. Yet the legend is not worthless because it is historically unlikely. It shows how Wigtownshire and wider Galloway made its prehistoric remains meaningful: by giving them names, heroes, graves, warnings and routes through memory.

## Torhouse Stone Circle and King Gauldus’s Tomb

Torhouse Stone Circle stands near Wigtown, in the Machars of historic Wigtownshire, a little off the modern B733. It is not a huge monument in the Stonehenge sense. Its power comes from survival, setting and strangeness: 19 squat granite stones form a near-circle about 20 to 22 metres across, graded in height, with the tallest stones in the south-eastern arc. Near the centre is a line of three stones, a feature that became the focus for the later tale of King Gauldus or Galdus.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://portal.historicenvironment.scot/apex/f?p=1505%3A300%3A%3A%3A%3A%3AVIEWTYPE%2CVIEWREF%3Adesignation%2CSM90304" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: portal.historicenvironment.scot">[Historic Environment Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">portal.historicenvironment.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on historicenvironment.scot.</span></span></span>

Historic Environment Scotland describes Torhouse as one of the best-preserved stone circles in the British Isles and places it within a landscape once rich in ritual and funerary monuments. Nearby were other standing stones and cairns, some of them now damaged or almost removed for field dykes. This matters for folklore because Torhouse was never just a ring of stones in isolation. It sat among other visible prehistoric remains, giving later people plenty of material for story-making: a central “tomb”, outlying “commanders”, and cairns that could be imagined as the graves of ordinary soldiers.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://app-hes-pubs-prod-neu-01.azurewebsites.net/api/file/93a899ce-1ac8-4e8d-aa86-b04600afbc6c" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: app-hes-pubs-prod-neu-01.azurewebsites.net">[HES Publications]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">app-hes-pubs-prod-neu-01.azurewebsites.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">HES Publications Torhouse Stone Circle</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">HES Publications Torhouse Stone Circle</span></span></span>

The key early source is Andrew Symson, minister of Kirkinner, whose *Large Description of Galloway* was written in 1684. The National Library of Scotland catalogue identifies the original manuscript as a response to questions circulated by Sir Robert Sibbald in 1682 for a proposed Scottish atlas, and notes that an edition was later published in 1823.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://manuscripts.nls.uk/repositories/2/resources/15722" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: manuscripts.nls.uk">[Manuscripts NLS]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">manuscripts.nls.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nls.uk.</span></span></span> In the printed text, Symson describes the “Moor, or Standing Stones of Torhouse” on the highway between Wigtown and Portpatrick, naming the three large central stones as “King Galdus’s tomb” and noting the surrounding 19 stones.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://mckeefamiliesfromdonegal.com/wp-content/uploads/2018/11/A_Large_Description_of_Galloway_Andrew_Symson.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: mckeefamiliesfromdonegal.com">[McKee Families from Donegal]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">mckeefamiliesfromdonegal.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on mckeefamiliesfromdonegal.com.</span></span></span>

The tradition expanded naturally from there. If the three central stones were a king’s tomb, then the nearby cairns and single stones could become the graves of soldiers, commanders or “men of note”. This is a classic folklore pattern: an impressive old structure is explained as the remains of a known dramatic event, even when its true makers and purpose have passed out of memory. Torhouse offered exactly the right ingredients: a visible circle, a central setting, other stones nearby, and a road on which local people and travellers could repeat the explanation.

## How old are the stones, and how old is the story?

The stones and the story are not the same age. Torhouse is probably prehistoric, with Historic Environment Scotland placing its construction in the Late Neolithic or Bronze Age and its popular visitor history suggesting it was probably built around 4,000 years ago. The recorded Gauldus tradition, by contrast, is securely visible in the late seventeenth century, when Symson wrote it down in 1684.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.historicenvironment.scot/visit/all/torhouse-stone-circle/history-and-stories/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: historicenvironment.scot">[Historic Environment Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">historicenvironment.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-title">history and stories</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">It consists of 19 dumpy granite boulders in a 20m-wide circle. They&#x27;re graded in height...Read more...</span></span></span>

That gap is important. It means Torhouse should not be read as archaeological evidence for King Gauldus. The legend is a later explanation attached to a much older monument. Historic Environment Scotland is careful on this point: the central stones were recorded as King Gauldus’s Tomb, but their original purpose is now lost, and the wider site has not been excavated enough to say much with certainty.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.historicenvironment.scot/visit/all/torhouse-stone-circle/history-and-stories/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: historicenvironment.scot">[Historic Environment Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">historicenvironment.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-title">history and stories</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">It consists of 19 dumpy granite boulders in a 20m-wide circle. They&#x27;re graded in height...Read more...</span></span></span>

The uncertainty is not a weakness of the page; it is the interesting part. Torhouse sits at the meeting point of three kinds of evidence:

**The archaeology:** a prehistoric stone circle, unusually well preserved, with a central stone setting and nearby ritual or funerary remains. The site has strong archaeological potential, but very little excavation has been carried out.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://app-hes-pubs-prod-neu-01.azurewebsites.net/api/file/93a899ce-1ac8-4e8d-aa86-b04600afbc6c" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: app-hes-pubs-prod-neu-01.azurewebsites.net">[HES Publications]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">app-hes-pubs-prod-neu-01.azurewebsites.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">HES Publications Torhouse Stone Circle</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">HES Publications Torhouse Stone Circle</span></span></span>

**The antiquarian record:** a seventeenth-century written description by Symson, preserving the name “King Galdus’s tomb” and showing that the heroic explanation was already current, at least in learned or local descriptive tradition, by 1684.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://manuscripts.nls.uk/repositories/2/resources/15722" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: manuscripts.nls.uk">[Manuscripts NLS]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">manuscripts.nls.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nls.uk.</span></span></span>

**The later folklore and tourism layer:** modern guide pages, heritage interpretation and local retellings continue to present the central stones as linked to the mythical King Gauldus, while usually adding the necessary warning that the monument is far older than the story.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.undiscoveredscotland.co.uk/wigtown/torhousestonecircle/index.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: undiscoveredscotland.co.uk">[Undiscovered Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">undiscoveredscotland.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on undiscoveredscotland.co.uk.</span></span></span>

For readers, this helps avoid two common mistakes. One is to treat the legend as a proven prehistoric memory. The other is to dismiss it as mere nonsense. The better reading is that Torhouse preserves a real tradition about how people in Wigtownshire and Galloway explained ancient stones when archaeology, folklore, patriot history and local pride were not neatly separated.

## Who was King Gauldus?

King Gauldus, also spelled Galdus, is best treated as a mythical or legendary Scottish king rather than a secure historical ruler. In local retellings he is often imagined as a heroic figure who fought the Romans, and some versions link him to the origin of the name Galloway. Undiscovered Scotland summarises the tradition but also notes the problem clearly: the supposed link between Gauldus and the place name Galloway is not accepted by modern experts, and any burial at Torhouse would long predate the Roman period in Britain.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.undiscoveredscotland.co.uk/wigtown/torhousestonecircle/index.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: undiscoveredscotland.co.uk">[Undiscovered Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">undiscoveredscotland.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on undiscoveredscotland.co.uk.</span></span></span>

The story becomes even more revealing when Torhouse is compared with Cairn Holy, another major prehistoric site in Galloway. Historic Environment Scotland says Cairn Holy II is also said to be the tomb of the mythical King Galdus, while making clear that the tomb itself is much older and may have held important people nearly 5,000 years ago.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.historicenvironment.scot/visit/all/cairn-holy-chambered-cairns/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: historicenvironment.scot">[Historic Environment Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">historicenvironment.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on historicenvironment.scot.</span></span></span> In other words, Galdus has more than one “grave”. That is not unusual in legend. Heroes often acquire multiple burial places because different communities want a share in the story, or because several impressive ancient monuments invite the same explanation.

This double-attribution changes how Torhouse should be understood. The question is not “Which site is really Galdus’s tomb?” but “Why did Galloway attach Galdus to more than one prehistoric monument?” The likely answer is that the legendary king gave a human face to stones and cairns whose original builders were unknown. He made the landscape feel historical, ancestral and heroic.

A useful modern local-history caution comes from the argument that the Galdus tradition may owe much to early Scottish historical writing rather than to an unbroken oral memory from antiquity. One Galloway-focused discussion points to Hector Boece’s sixteenth-century history as a possible literary source for the idea of Galdus as a Roman-fighting king, later mapped onto monuments such as Torhouse and Cairnholy by readers looking for a suitable ancient tomb.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://greengalloway.blogspot.com/2017/12/no-grave-for-king-galdus.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: greengalloway.blogspot.com">[Greengalloway]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">greengalloway.blogspot.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">no grave for king galdus</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">no grave for king galdus</span></span></span> That does not prove every detail of the tradition’s transmission, but it is a sensible warning: some “old folklore” may be a blend of local naming, antiquarian learning and patriotic storytelling.

## How monuments gain new names and meanings

Torhouse shows a repeated process in Wigtownshire folklore: old stones become story landmarks when people need them to answer questions the archaeology cannot yet answer. Who made them? Why are they here? Why are some stones larger? Why is there a line of three in the middle? Why are there other stones and cairns nearby? A named king and his followers give quick, memorable answers.

This is not unique to Torhouse, but Torhouse is a particularly clean case because the evidence layers are visible. The monument is prehistoric; the Gauldus name is recorded in 1684; modern heritage bodies keep the traditional name while separating it from archaeological certainty. Historic Environment Scotland’s formal statement calls the site “King Gauldus’s Tomb”, the Standing Stones of Torhouse and Torhousekie Stone Circle among its known names, but its archaeological assessment focuses on prehistoric form, preservation, comparison and research gaps rather than treating the royal legend as fact.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://app-hes-pubs-prod-neu-01.azurewebsites.net/api/file/93a899ce-1ac8-4e8d-aa86-b04600afbc6c" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: app-hes-pubs-prod-neu-01.azurewebsites.net">[HES Publications]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">app-hes-pubs-prod-neu-01.azurewebsites.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">HES Publications Torhouse Stone Circle</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">HES Publications Torhouse Stone Circle</span></span></span>

The three central stones are especially important. They invite interpretation because they look deliberate, separate and meaningful. HES notes that their purpose probably cannot be explained without excavation, though they may have framed a distant view; other theories for flattened stone circles include alignment with the rising moon or setting sun, or an architectural device that emphasised one part of the monument.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.historicenvironment.scot/visit/all/torhouse-stone-circle/history-and-stories/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: historicenvironment.scot">[Historic Environment Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">historicenvironment.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-title">history and stories</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">It consists of 19 dumpy granite boulders in a 20m-wide circle. They&#x27;re graded in height...Read more...</span></span></span> Folklore does not wait for excavation. Where archaeology says “unknown”, tradition says “tomb”.

The same process also explains why outlying stones can become soldiers or commanders. A cluster of prehistoric remains is transformed into a battlefield map. The central setting becomes the ruler’s grave; surrounding or nearby features become the social order around him. It is landscape interpretation by narrative. The result may not tell us what happened in the third or second millennium BC, but it does tell us how later Wigtownshire understood ancientness: through rank, memory, burial and heroic conflict.

## Standing stones as folklore landmarks in West Galloway

Torhouse belongs to a wider West Galloway habit of reading stones, cairns and carved rocks as meaningful presences in the landscape. Historic Environment Scotland’s significance statement links Torhouse with other locally related prehistoric places, including Cairn Holy, Drumtroddan Standing Stones, Claughreid Stone Circle, Glenquicken Stone Circle and the Wren’s Egg stones.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://app-hes-pubs-prod-neu-01.azurewebsites.net/api/file/93a899ce-1ac8-4e8d-aa86-b04600afbc6c" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: app-hes-pubs-prod-neu-01.azurewebsites.net">[HES Publications]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">app-hes-pubs-prod-neu-01.azurewebsites.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">HES Publications Torhouse Stone Circle</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">HES Publications Torhouse Stone Circle</span></span></span> Those places should not be collapsed into one story, but together they show why Wigtownshire and neighbouring Galloway are such fertile ground for stone folklore.

Several features make this kind of legend-making more likely. First, the monuments are visibly old but not self-explanatory. A ruined medieval tower tells the viewer roughly what kind of building it was; a stone circle does not. Secondly, many stand in working rural landscapes, close to roads, fields, farms and parish boundaries, so they remain part of ordinary local knowledge rather than sealed away as museum objects. Thirdly, the Machars and wider Wigtownshire landscape already carries strong traditions of saints, pilgrimage, [fairies]({{ 'fairies-f67c64/' | relative_url }}), haunted places and old routes, so prehistoric stones can be absorbed into a larger map of meaningful places.

Torhouse also sits in a region where the boundary between historic Wigtownshire and wider Galloway matters. The site itself is Wigtownshire, near Wigtown in the Bladnoch Valley, but the Galdus story moves across the old county frame into the Stewartry of Kirkcudbright through Cairn Holy. That does not weaken its Wigtownshire relevance. It shows how West Galloway’s folklore works: rooted in particular parishes and monuments, but willing to cross county edges when a hero, road, coastline or ancient landscape gives the story room to travel.

The modern visitor experience reinforces this. Torhouse is free to visit, open year-round, and promoted by heritage and tourism bodies as a small but rewarding ancient site near Wigtown.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.historicenvironment.scot/visit/all/torhouse-stone-circle/plan-your-visit/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: historicenvironment.scot">[Historic Environment Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">historicenvironment.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on historicenvironment.scot.</span></span></span> The legend of King Gauldus remains part of that public-facing identity because it gives non-specialist visitors a way into the place. The best modern interpretation does not say, “Here lies a proven king.” It says something more interesting: “Here is a prehistoric monument that later generations turned into a king’s tomb.”

## What the evidence can and cannot prove

The strongest evidence for Torhouse is archaeological and antiquarian, not supernatural or heroic. The physical site proves the existence of an important prehistoric stone circle. The early written record proves that by 1684 the central stones were being identified as King Galdus’s Tomb. The wider Galloway pattern proves that Galdus became attached to more than one ancient monument. None of this proves that a Roman-fighting king was buried at Torhouse.

The archaeological caution is especially strong because Torhouse has not been properly excavated. Historic Environment Scotland states that scarcely any archaeological survey or excavation has been carried out at Torhouse or the surrounding sites, and that the lack of investigation means remarkably little is known about its precise date, internal arrangements or relationship to other monuments.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://app-hes-pubs-prod-neu-01.azurewebsites.net/api/file/93a899ce-1ac8-4e8d-aa86-b04600afbc6c" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: app-hes-pubs-prod-neu-01.azurewebsites.net">[HES Publications]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">app-hes-pubs-prod-neu-01.azurewebsites.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">HES Publications Torhouse Stone Circle</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">HES Publications Torhouse Stone Circle</span></span></span> That leaves room for careful interpretation, but not for confident claims about a named burial.

The folklore evidence is still valuable. Symson’s 1684 notice is early enough to matter, and it captures a local or regional explanation before modern tourism packaged the stones for visitors. It also shows how the story was already attached to the roadscape between Wigtown and Portpatrick, not merely to an isolated archaeological plan.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://mckeefamiliesfromdonegal.com/wp-content/uploads/2018/11/A_Large_Description_of_Galloway_Andrew_Symson.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: mckeefamiliesfromdonegal.com">[McKee Families from Donegal]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">mckeefamiliesfromdonegal.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on mckeefamiliesfromdonegal.com.</span></span></span> That road context matters: stories travel with people, and a conspicuous stone circle beside a route is exactly the kind of place that gathers names.

The most honest conclusion is therefore balanced. Torhouse is not King Gauldus’s proven grave. It is a prehistoric stone circle whose central stones were turned into King Gauldus’s Tomb in later tradition. That makes it one of Wigtownshire’s best examples of “story-making stones”: monuments whose mystery did not fade when their original purpose was forgotten, but instead invited new names, new heroes and new explanations.

## Why Torhouse matters to Wigtownshire folklore

Torhouse matters because it shows Wigtownshire folklore being made in the open air. The county’s legends are not only tales told by the fireside; they are also acts of naming and noticing. A circle becomes a tomb. A line of stones becomes a royal burial. Nearby cairns become soldiers. A prehistoric landscape becomes a remembered kingdom.

This gives Torhouse a special role within the folklore of Wigtownshire. It connects the county’s deep prehistory with later habits of antiquarian description, local pride and public heritage. It also keeps the old county’s landscape legible: Wigtown, Bladnoch, the Machars and the road westwards are not just points on a map, but places where ancient remains and later stories overlap.

For modern readers, the value is not in choosing between “archaeology” and “legend”. The value is in seeing how they sit on top of one another. Archaeology tells us Torhouse is an important prehistoric monument in a once-rich ritual and funerary landscape. Folklore tells us that later Wigtownshire and Galloway communities wanted those stones to speak in the language of kings, graves and heroic memory. Held together, those two truths make Torhouse far more memorable than either a bare site description or an inflated legend on its own.

    var trigger = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-trigger]');
    var menu = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-menu]');
    if (trigger && menu) {
      trigger.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
        event.stopPropagation();
        var expanded = trigger.getAttribute('aria-expanded') === 'true';
        menu.hidden = expanded;
        trigger.setAttribute('aria-expanded', expanded ? 'false' : 'true');
        var picker = trigger.closest('.fr-ebay-market-picker');
        if (picker) picker.classList.toggle('fr-ebay-market-picker--open', !expanded);
      });
      Array.prototype.slice.call(menu.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-option]')).forEach(function (button) {
        button.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
          event.stopPropagation();
          applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-option'), true);
        });
      });
      document.addEventListener('click', function () { closeMenu(section); });
    }
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      button.addEventListener('click', function () {
        applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button'), true);
      });
    });
    applyMarket(section, marketId, false);
  });
