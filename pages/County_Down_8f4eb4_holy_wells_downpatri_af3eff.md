## Introduction

[County Down]({{ 'where-county-down-turns-landscape-into/' | relative_url }})’s holy wells around Downpatrick were powerful because they joined three things ordinary people could see and use: running water, saintly memory and repeated ritual. At Struell Wells, east of Downpatrick, the tradition attached cures to water said to have been blessed by Saint Patrick; at St Cooey’s Wells near Portaferry, three smaller wells divided healing practice into washing, eye cures and drinking. These were not vague “magic springs” in local imagination. They were worked into pilgrimage routes, midsummer customs, penitential stones, rag trees, ruined churches and stories of saints whose holiness was understood through place. The result is one of County Down’s clearest examples of Christian folklore layered over older sacred landscapes: a landscape where cure, prayer and memory flow through actual channels, stones and paths.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.communities-ni.gov.uk/heritage-sites/struell-bath-houses-wells" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: communities-ni.gov.uk">[communities-ni.gov.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">communities-ni.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on communities-ni.gov.uk.</span></span></span>

## Struell Wells and the Patrick Landscape

Struell Wells sits just outside Downpatrick on the Ardglass Road, in a secluded valley built around a stream. The Department for Communities describes it as an early site, “likely pre 1000AD”, strongly associated with Saint Patrick, while also stressing that the buildings visible today are much later. That distinction matters. The folklore is older and more fluid than the masonry; the stone structures are the later frame around a spring system whose sacred reputation had already taken hold.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.communities-ni.gov.uk/heritage-sites/struell-bath-houses-wells" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: communities-ni.gov.uk">[Department for Communities]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">communities-ni.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on communities-ni.gov.uk.</span></span></span>

The Patrick connection is unusually well attested for a holy-well tradition. Finbar McCormick’s peer-reviewed study of Struell describes it as the most extensive holy-well complex in Ireland, made up of wells, bath-houses, church ruins and the nearby natural feature known as St Patrick’s Chair. McCormick links the earliest reference to the site with the eighth-century Fíacc’s Hymn, which records Patrick’s presence there, and dates the earliest reference to the wells’ healing powers to the eleventh or twelfth century.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pure.qub.ac.uk/en/publications/struell-wells-pagan-past-and-christian-present" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pure.qub.ac.uk">[Queen&#x27;s University Belfast]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pure.qub.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Struell Wells: pagan past and Christian present - Queen&#x27;s University Belfast...</span></span></span>

For a folklore reader, Struell is valuable because the tradition is not just “Patrick passed this way”. The saint is imagined as physically using the place: immersed in the water by night, singing psalms, and resting by day on a stone. Modern pilgrim-route material still preserves this memorable version, saying that legend has Patrick singing psalms through the night at Struell, and now presenting the wells chiefly through their reputed healing powers.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pure.qub.ac.uk/en/publications/struell-bathing-at-midsummer-and-the-origins-of-holy-wells/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pure.qub.ac.uk">[Queen&#x27;s University Belfast]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pure.qub.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Struell: Bathing at midsummer and the origins of holy wells - Queen&#x27;s University Belfast...</span></span></span>

This makes Struell a mechanism of belief rather than a mere monument. The saint’s body in the water becomes a model for later pilgrims’ bodies in the water. The stone where he rested becomes part of the sacred furniture of the valley. The stream is not only scenic; it is the moving element through which blessing, cure and memory are imagined to pass.

## What Pilgrims Did at Struell

Struell’s most distinctive feature is that bathing was central to the ritual. Many holy wells were visited for prayer, drinking water, washing sore eyes or leaving offerings, but McCormick argues that Struell appears to be unique in making bathing an integral part of the pilgrim practice. The complex itself encouraged this: it included wells and bath-houses, not just a single spring in a field.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pure.qub.ac.uk/en/publications/struell-wells-pagan-past-and-christian-present" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pure.qub.ac.uk">[Queen&#x27;s University Belfast]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pure.qub.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Struell Wells: pagan past and Christian present - Queen&#x27;s University Belfast...</span></span></span>

The strongest recorded pattern was midsummer. McCormick’s later study notes that the main devotional activity at Struell took place on Midsummer Eve rather than on Saint Patrick’s Day, and that accounts from the seventeenth to nineteenth centuries describe large numbers of pilgrims bathing in the wells at midsummer. He suggests that this may preserve older midsummer customs concerned with health, protection and renewal, later absorbed into a Christian Patrick setting.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pure.qub.ac.uk/en/publications/struell-bathing-at-midsummer-and-the-origins-of-holy-wells/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pure.qub.ac.uk">[Queen&#x27;s University Belfast]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pure.qub.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Struell: Bathing at midsummer and the origins of holy wells - Queen&#x27;s University Belfast...</span></span></span>

That midsummer emphasis is one reason Struell is so important for County Down folklore. It does not fit neatly into a simple story of “pagan spring becomes Christian well” or “medieval pilgrimage becomes tourist site”. Instead, it shows several layers working at once:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **Saintly imitation:** pilgrims repeated, in ritual form, the story of Patrick entering the water.
* **Seasonal timing:** the great gathering centred on midsummer, a moment long associated with health, protection and turning points in the year.
* **Built control of water:** channels, wells and bath-houses turned the natural stream into a managed sacred route.
* **Local cure tradition:** the waters were believed to have curative powers, giving the site practical emotional force for people seeking relief.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.communities-ni.gov.uk/heritage-sites/struell-bath-houses-wells" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: communities-ni.gov.uk">[communities-ni.gov.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">communities-ni.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on communities-ni.gov.uk.</span></span></span>

</div>

This is why Struell feels more like a ritual landscape than a single holy well. A visitor today sees stone buildings, a stream and ruins. Older pilgrims encountered a sequence: arrive, pray, move, wash, bathe, drink, keep vigil, repeat inherited gestures and trust the saintly reputation of the place.

## Why the Cures Felt Local and Specific

Holy-well cures often become believable within communities because they are specific. A general promise that “water heals” is much weaker than a local instruction about which water to use, what part of the body to wash, when to go and whose saintly help to ask. Around Downpatrick and the Ards Peninsula, this specificity is visible at both Struell and St Cooey’s.

At Struell, the surviving tradition attaches curative power to a named Patrick site, with separate structures and a remembered bathing practice. At St Cooey’s Wells, the mechanism is more compact but even more clearly divided: the wells are known as the Washing, Eye and Drinking wells. Visit Ards and North Down describes the wells as reputed to have healing powers, with rags tied to nearby thorn trees testifying to the site’s healing reputation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.visitardsandnorthdown.com/things-to-do/st-cooeys-wells-p720191" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: visitardsandnorthdown.com">[Ards &amp; North Down]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">visitardsandnorthdown.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Ards &amp; North Down St Cooey&#x27;s Wells</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Ards &amp; North Down St Cooey&#x27;s Wells</span></span></span>

The eye-cure element is especially typical of holy-well folklore across Ireland and Britain, but St Cooey’s gives it a strong local form. A later local-history account describes the three wells as serving different purposes: one for washing hands, one associated with eye infections and sight problems, and one for drinking. It also preserves the folklore claim that the spring water remains cold and cannot be heated even when boiled.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.belfastentries.com/people/st-cooeys-wells/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: belfastentries.com">[Belfast Entries]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">belfastentries.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Belfast Entries Saint Cooey’s Wells at Temple Cooey</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Belfast Entries Saint Cooey’s Wells at Temple Cooey</span></span></span>

These details matter because they show how a cure tradition becomes memorable. The believer is not dealing with abstract doctrine. The body is mapped onto the site: hands, eyes, mouth. The cure is enacted through ordinary actions — washing, looking, drinking, tying cloth — but those actions are intensified by saintly association and inherited local expectation.

## St Cooey’s Wells and the Ards Peninsula

St Cooey’s Wells lie near Tara Bay, around two miles south-east of Portaferry, in the shadow of Tieveshilly Hill. The official local tourism account is careful about evidence: no documentary evidence for St Cooey himself has been discovered, although his name survives in local place-names such as Lough Cowey. The nearby church is thought to date from the seventh century, but only foundation stones remain.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.visitardsandnorthdown.com/things-to-do/st-cooeys-wells-p720191" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: visitardsandnorthdown.com">[Ards &amp; North Down]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">visitardsandnorthdown.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Ards &amp; North Down St Cooey&#x27;s Wells</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Ards &amp; North Down St Cooey&#x27;s Wells</span></span></span>

That uncertainty is not a weakness in folklore terms; it is part of how the tradition works. St Cooey is less a securely documented historical figure than a saint of local memory. His wells, ruined church, shore path and penitential stone preserve his presence more strongly than written biography does. The landscape is the archive.

The site also keeps a living ritual rhythm. Visit Ards and North Down records that pilgrimage takes place annually on the Sunday nearest to the feast of Saints Peter and Paul, and that the site was extensively restored in the mid 1970s by members of the local parish. The restoration added a modern altar incorporating some of the stones, showing how older sacred places can be repaired and re-presented without losing their folk character.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.visitardsandnorthdown.com/things-to-do/st-cooeys-wells-p720191" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: visitardsandnorthdown.com">[Ards &amp; North Down]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">visitardsandnorthdown.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Ards &amp; North Down St Cooey&#x27;s Wells</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Ards &amp; North Down St Cooey&#x27;s Wells</span></span></span>

St Cooey’s is therefore a quieter counterpart to Struell. Struell is tied to Ireland’s best-known saint and a major Downpatrick pilgrimage landscape. St Cooey’s is more local, more coastal, more dependent on oral tradition and parish memory. Together, they show two scales of County Down holy-well culture: the famous Patrick site and the smaller saint’s place whose authority comes from continuity, use and affection.

## Rags, Stones and the Physical Language of Cure

The most visible signs at St Cooey’s are not only the wells themselves but the things around them: thorn trees with rags, a path through reeds, and a slab by the shore said to carry the marks of the saint’s knees and hands. These features turn belief into something tactile. A person can tie a cloth, follow a path, touch water, look for hollows in stone and feel that they are moving through a story rather than only reading about one.

The rag custom is especially important. At St Cooey’s, the number of rags attached to nearby thorn trees is presented by the official visitor account as evidence of the well’s reputation for healing. Belfast Entries explains the custom as a traditional act in which cloth was tied to a branch when a saint’s intercession was requested or thanks was being given.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.visitardsandnorthdown.com/things-to-do/st-cooeys-wells-p720191" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: visitardsandnorthdown.com">[Ards &amp; North Down]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">visitardsandnorthdown.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Ards &amp; North Down St Cooey&#x27;s Wells</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Ards &amp; North Down St Cooey&#x27;s Wells</span></span></span>

The penitential stone adds another layer. A path from the wells leads through reedbed to the shore, where hollows in a rock slab are said to mark where St Cooey knelt to pray. In local tradition, the stone makes the saint’s devotion visible. Like St Patrick’s Chair at Struell, it anchors sanctity in a physical mark: the landscape appears to remember the saint’s body.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.visitardsandnorthdown.com/things-to-do/st-cooeys-wells-p720191" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: visitardsandnorthdown.com">[Ards &amp; North Down]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">visitardsandnorthdown.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Ards &amp; North Down St Cooey&#x27;s Wells</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Ards &amp; North Down St Cooey&#x27;s Wells</span></span></span>

This is one of the great mechanisms of saintly folklore in County Down. Water cures because a saint blessed, used or inhabited the place. Stone matters because it is said to preserve the saint’s posture. Trees matter because petitions can be left there. The landscape becomes a set of devotional tools.

## Older Sacred Places Beneath Christian Names

The Downpatrick holy-well traditions are Christian in their surviving form, but they also point towards older attitudes to water and seasonal power. McCormick is careful rather than sensational: he argues that the evidence from Struell strongly suggests it was an important sacred site in pre-Christian times, while also recognising the later Christian and Counter-Reformation shaping of holy-well devotion.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pure.qub.ac.uk/en/publications/struell-wells-pagan-past-and-christian-present" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pure.qub.ac.uk">[Queen&#x27;s University Belfast]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pure.qub.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Struell Wells: pagan past and Christian present - Queen&#x27;s University Belfast...</span></span></span>

This layered reading is more useful than trying to label the wells as either “pagan” or “Christian”. Struell’s power in local tradition comes from precisely the overlap. A stream in a secluded valley had long-standing sacred potential; Patrick’s story gave that power a Christian name and narrative; medieval and early modern practice built routines around it; later visitors, antiquarians, clergy, archaeologists and tourism bodies preserved different parts of the memory.

The same pattern appears, on a smaller scale, at St Cooey’s. The saint himself is not well documented, but his name fixes the wells to a local Christian identity. The threefold water use, rag trees and penitential stone then carry practices that feel older and broader than any single written life of the saint. That does not prove a direct survival from pre-Christian religion. It does show how sacred water traditions can persist through adaptation, renaming and repeated local use.

## How the Traditions Are Retold Today

Today, these wells are encountered through a mixture of heritage management, pilgrimage, tourism and local memory. Struell is listed by the Department for Communities as a free public heritage site, though access may be restricted during works, and its official description still foregrounds its Patrick association and curative waters.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.communities-ni.gov.uk/heritage-sites/struell-bath-houses-wells" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: communities-ni.gov.uk">[Department for Communities]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">communities-ni.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on communities-ni.gov.uk.</span></span></span>

Modern tourism places Struell within a wider Patrick route. Mourne Gullion Strangford’s visitor material links Downpatrick with ancient churches, healing wells and pilgrim trails, presenting the area as a landscape where Patrick’s story can still be followed on foot or by road. Its account of Struell describes the waters as miraculous in tradition and says the present wells and bathing houses have stood on the site since at least the twelfth century.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.visitmournegullionstrangford.com/explore/saint-patricks-story" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: visitmournegullionstrangford.com">[Visit Mourne Gullion Strangford]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">visitmournegullionstrangford.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Visit Mourne Gullion Strangford Saint Patrick&#x27;s Story</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Visit Mourne Gullion Strangford Saint Patrick&#x27;s Story</span></span></span>

St Cooey’s is presented differently: less as a major national saint’s destination and more as a restored local sacred site. Its official visitor page emphasises access from Ballyquintin Road, the annual pilgrimage, the restored altar, the three named wells, the rag trees and the path to the shore stone. That is exactly how smaller holy-well folklore often survives: not through grand interpretation, but through a handful of durable actions and visible signs.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.visitardsandnorthdown.com/things-to-do/st-cooeys-wells-p720191" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: visitardsandnorthdown.com">[Ards &amp; North Down]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">visitardsandnorthdown.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Ards &amp; North Down St Cooey&#x27;s Wells</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Ards &amp; North Down St Cooey&#x27;s Wells</span></span></span>

For County Down’s folklore identity, the wells matter because they keep saintly tradition grounded. Patrick is not only a figure in books, church history or national celebration; around Downpatrick, he is attached to water, stone, walking routes and cure-seeking. Cooey is even more dependent on place: without the wells, ruined church and local names, he would be almost invisible.

## What Made County Down’s Holy Wells Powerful?

County Down’s holy wells were powerful because they offered a practical meeting point between suffering, hope and place. People did not need to understand theology in abstract terms to approach Struell or St Cooey’s. They could wash sore eyes, drink from a named well, bathe at a powerful season, tie a rag, kneel by a stone, join a pilgrimage or simply stand in a landscape where generations had done the same.

The traditions around Downpatrick also show why holy wells are so resilient. They can survive changes in church authority, land ownership, medical belief and tourism language because their basic form is simple: water comes from the earth; a saint is remembered there; people bring needs to the place. At Struell, that simplicity became an elaborate Patrick pilgrimage landscape. At St Cooey’s, it remains intimate and local, held together by three wells, a rag tree, a shore stone and an annual act of return.

These wells are not best understood as proof of supernatural cures, nor as quaint relics of ignorance. They are County Down’s water-borne memory system: a way of giving illness, penitence, seasonal anxiety and saintly devotion a visible place in the landscape.
