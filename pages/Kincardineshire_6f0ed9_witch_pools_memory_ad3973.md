## Introduction

[Kincardineshire]({{ 'where-kincardineshire-turns-history-into/' | relative_url }})’s witch-pool tradition is not best read as a spooky proof of witchcraft. It is a local memory of ordeal: the idea that a suspected witch could be tested by water, with the cruel logic that sinking suggested innocence while floating suggested guilt. The most important Kincardineshire example is the pool in the Carron Water near [Dunnottar]({{ 'dunnottar/' | relative_url }}) Church, remembered in nineteenth-century folklore writing as a place where water was once used to “give evidence” against people accused of sorcery.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/files/56034/56034-h/56034-h.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Project Gutenberg Folklore of Scottish Lochs and Springs</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Project Gutenberg Folklore of Scottish Lochs and Springs</span></span></span>



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/Kincardineshire_6f0ed9_witch_pools_memory_ad3973-overview.webp" | relative_url }}" alt="Overview image for Witch Memory" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

That makes the tradition powerful, but also ethically awkward. The phrase “witch-pool” can sound picturesque on a walk map or in local talk, yet what it remembers belongs to Scotland’s wider history of fear, accusation and legal violence. The useful question is not whether witches really met at such pools, but how a burn, pool or place-name came to hold the memory of a community deciding who was dangerous, who could be blamed, and whose suffering could later be softened into legend.

## Where the Dunnottar witch-pool belongs

The Kincardineshire tradition is attached to the Carron Water near Dunnottar Church, close to Stonehaven and the wooded Dunnottar landscape. The old county, also known as the Mearns, lies on Scotland’s north-east coast between Aberdeenshire and Angus, with Stonehaven one of its principal historic settlements.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://wikishire.co.uk/wiki/Kincardineshire" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wikishire.co.uk">[Wikishire]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wikishire.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wikishire.co.uk.</span></span></span> The location matters because this is not an abstract Scottish witch story: it is tied to a real stream, a church landscape, and a county where older parish, estate and routeway memories often outlast modern administrative labels.

The main published notice of the Carron witch-pool appears in James M. Mackinlay’s *Folklore of Scottish Lochs and Springs*, first published in 1893. Mackinlay discusses water as a witness in witchcraft accusation and names “a pool in the Carron, near Dunnottar Church in Kincardineshire” as one of the places where such an ordeal was remembered.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/files/56034/56034-h/56034-h.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Project Gutenberg Folklore of Scottish Lochs and Springs</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Project Gutenberg Folklore of Scottish Lochs and Springs</span></span></span> This is not the same as a full trial record naming an accused person, a date and a sentence. It is a folklore-and-antiquarian source preserving the place-memory of a practice.

That distinction is important. A named trial can be checked against court papers, kirk-session records or the Survey of Scottish Witchcraft. A witch-pool tradition usually survives in a different way: as a place-name, a local explanation, a guidebook anecdote, or an antiquarian note. The Dunnottar pool therefore sits between history and folklore. It points towards real early modern procedures and beliefs, but the surviving public tradition is the memory of a mechanism rather than a full case file.

## The Dunnottar water ordeal tradition

The ordeal described in Scottish witchcraft lore worked through a grim inversion of common sense. The accused person was bound and put into water. If she sank, the water had accepted her and she was counted innocent; if she floated, the water had rejected her and she was treated as guilty. Mackinlay summarises this belief directly when he explains that, in cases of alleged sorcery, “innocence or guilt could be determined” by sinking or floating, before citing the Dunnottar-area pool as a Kincardineshire example.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/files/56034/56034-h/56034-h.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Project Gutenberg Folklore of Scottish Lochs and Springs</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Project Gutenberg Folklore of Scottish Lochs and Springs</span></span></span>

The theological explanation often given for this ordeal drew on King James VI’s *Daemonologie*. James argued that water would refuse to receive those who had rejected the sacred water of baptism. Mackinlay quotes this reasoning in his discussion of witch-pools, showing how a physical test was made to appear religiously meaningful.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/files/56034/56034-h/56034-h.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Project Gutenberg Folklore of Scottish Lochs and Springs</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Project Gutenberg Folklore of Scottish Lochs and Springs</span></span></span> For a modern reader, that is the disturbing heart of the tradition: the landscape was not merely a backdrop to belief, but was imagined as an active witness.

This did not make the ordeal fair. A bound person thrown into cold water was in danger whether she floated or sank. Floating could be read as guilt; sinking could be fatal before anyone chose to rescue her. The pool’s remembered function was therefore not neutral “testing” in any modern sense. It was a ritualised form of pressure, humiliation and danger within a culture already prepared to believe that misfortune might have a human culprit.

The Dunnottar example also shows how water traditions could change meaning. In Kincardineshire and neighbouring north-east counties, wells, burns and pools often carry saintly, healing or warning associations. But the Carron witch-pool belongs to a darker category: water not as cure, blessing or omen, but as a tool for sorting the accused from the supposedly guilty. The same physical feature that might elsewhere be picturesque becomes a marker of coercion.

## How accusation became folklore

A witch-pool tradition begins with accusation, but it survives by being retold. Over time, a specific act of communal fear can become a place-story: “this was where witches were tried”, “this was where they were drowned”, or “this pool was used to test them”. The details may thin out, but the emotional shape remains. The place is remembered as uncanny because something cruel, shameful or frightening was believed to have happened there.

The Survey of Scottish Witchcraft, created by University of Edinburgh researchers Julian Goodare, Louise Yeoman, Lauren Martin and Joyce Miller, is useful here because it shows what lies behind the word “witch” in historical records. The project defines an accused witch as a person denounced for witchcraft where evidence shows that ecclesiastical, civil or both kinds of action were pursued.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://doc.ukdataservice.ac.uk/doc/4667/mrdoc/pdf/guide.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: doc.ukdataservice.ac.uk">[UK Data Service]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">doc.ukdataservice.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">UK Data Service Survey of Scottish Witchcraft</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UK Data Service Survey of Scottish Witchcraft</span></span></span> In other words, many “witches” in Scottish memory were not magical figures at all, but people drawn into formal or semi-formal processes of suspicion.

The same database was designed to examine accused people, accusation patterns, community and legal procedures, regional variation, chronology and geography.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://datashare.ed.ac.uk/handle/10283/45" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: datashare.ed.ac.uk">[Edinburgh DataShare]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">datashare.ed.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on ed.ac.uk.</span></span></span> That broader framework helps interpret Dunnottar’s pool. Even where a local pool tradition does not preserve a named victim, it belongs to a wider culture in which accusations moved through neighbours, parish discipline, local elites, courts and stories.

The folklore mechanism is simple but powerful:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **A misfortune needs a cause.** Illness, livestock loss, bad weather, failed crops or personal conflict could be interpreted through witchcraft belief.
* **Suspicion attaches to a person.** The accused was often someone already marked by dispute, reputation, vulnerability or difference.
* **A test gives fear a ritual shape.** The water ordeal made communal suspicion look like judgement by nature or God.
* **The place keeps the memory.** When names and legal details fade, the pool, stone, hill or burn may remain as a local label.

</div>

This is why the Dunnottar witch-pool should not be treated as a charming oddity. It is a small Kincardineshire doorway into the social machinery of accusation.

## What Scottish witch memory adds to the local story

The Dunnottar pool is a local tradition, but its meaning becomes clearer against Scotland’s wider witch-trial history. The National Trust for Scotland notes that around 4,000 people were formally accused of witchcraft in Scotland in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, with perhaps 2,500 executed. It also stresses that the majority of the accused were women, often ordinary members of rural Lowland communities rather than the cartoonish figures of modern Halloween imagery.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nts.org.uk/stories/the-myths-of-witches" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nts.org.uk">[National Trust for Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nts.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Trust for Scotland The myths of witches &#124; National Trust for Scotland</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Trust for Scotland The myths of witches &#124; National Trust for Scotland</span></span></span>

That point matters for Kincardineshire because it changes the tone of the story. A “witch-pool” might sound like a supernatural attraction. A better reading is that it records the treatment of accused neighbours: women and men who were spoken about, watched, examined, denounced or punished under beliefs that had legal force. The pool is therefore part of social history as much as folklore.

The Survey of Scottish Witchcraft also warns readers not to flatten every reference into a neat trial story. Its guide distinguishes a “case” from a trial, explaining that a case may include denunciation, investigation, arrest, trial and outcome, but does not simply mean that a formal trial definitely took place.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://doc.ukdataservice.ac.uk/doc/4667/mrdoc/pdf/guide.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: doc.ukdataservice.ac.uk">[UK Data Service]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">doc.ukdataservice.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">UK Data Service Survey of Scottish Witchcraft</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UK Data Service Survey of Scottish Witchcraft</span></span></span> This is a useful caution for Dunnottar. The Carron pool tradition is evidence of local memory about ordeal; it should not be inflated into a detailed execution narrative unless named records can support that step.

The modern map of accused witches has made these histories more publicly visible. The National Lottery Heritage Fund describes how University of Edinburgh data was opened up and mapped, allowing modern users to explore where accused witches lived and how widespread Scottish witchcraft accusation was.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.heritagefund.org.uk/news/mapping-scotlands-accused-witches-through-open-data" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: heritagefund.org.uk">[The National Lottery Heritage Fund]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">heritagefund.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on heritagefund.org.uk.</span></span></span> This matters for places like Kincardineshire because it encourages a move away from vague “witchy” atmosphere and towards careful remembrance of actual accused people and documented systems.

## Retelling witch stories without blaming victims

The first rule for retelling the Dunnottar witch-pool tradition is not to call the accused “witches” as though the charge were true. Plain wording helps: “people accused of witchcraft”, “suspected witches”, or “those remembered in witch-pool tradition” keeps the historical accusation visible without endorsing it.

The second rule is to avoid making the pool into a thrill stop. A visitor can still find the story compelling: a wooded burn, an old church landscape, Dunnottar’s wider atmosphere of cliff, castle and memory. But the emotional centre should be injustice, not entertainment. Scotland’s current public conversation has moved strongly in that direction. The Witches of Scotland campaign seeks a pardon, apology and national memorial for those accused under the Witchcraft Act 1563, and notes that a formal apology was issued by then First Minister Nicola Sturgeon on International Women’s Day 2022.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.witchesofscotland.com/about" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: witchesofscotland.com">[Witches of Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">witchesofscotland.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on witchesofscotland.com.</span></span></span> A Scottish Parliament petition lodged in 2024 similarly framed posthumous pardon as recognition of a historic wrong affecting predominantly female victims.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.parliament.scot/~/media/committ/9661/Petition-PE2122" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: parliament.scot">[Scottish Parliament]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">parliament.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Petition PE2122</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Petition PE2122</span></span></span>

The third rule is to be honest about evidence. The Dunnottar pool is well worth including in Kincardineshire folklore because Mackinlay’s nineteenth-century account specifically places a witch-ordeal memory on the Carron near Dunnottar Church.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/files/56034/56034-h/56034-h.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Project Gutenberg Folklore of Scottish Lochs and Springs</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Project Gutenberg Folklore of Scottish Lochs and Springs</span></span></span> But that is not the same as knowing the names of everyone tested there, how often the ordeal occurred, or exactly when the tradition began. A responsible retelling can say: this place preserves the memory of a water ordeal tradition; the wider Scottish record shows that witchcraft accusations had devastating consequences; the local details are fragmentary.

The fourth rule is to watch the language of “belief”. Early modern people were not simply foolish villagers in a ghost story. Witchcraft accusation was shaped by law, theology, local conflict, gender, fear and authority. The National Trust for Scotland’s warning against modern stereotypes is useful here: the accused were often ordinary people, not the pointy-hatted figures later popular culture invented.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nts.org.uk/stories/the-myths-of-witches" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nts.org.uk">[National Trust for Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nts.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Trust for Scotland The myths of witches &#124; National Trust for Scotland</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Trust for Scotland The myths of witches &#124; National Trust for Scotland</span></span></span>

## Why the witch-pool still matters in Kincardineshire

The Dunnottar witch-pool matters because it gives Kincardineshire folklore a moral depth that is easy to miss. The county’s better-known legendary landscape includes Dunnottar Castle, saints’ wells, coastal drama, Stonehaven customs and Pictish remains. The witch-pool adds a different kind of memory: not heroic, picturesque or miraculous, but anxious and accusatory.

It also shows how folklore can preserve uncomfortable history when formal records are incomplete or hard to access. A pool-name may outlive the paperwork. A local phrase may carry the outline of a practice long after the community has stopped believing in the test. A walk through Dunnottar Woods or the Carron-side landscape can therefore become more than scenic: it can prompt the question of how ordinary places inherit stories of fear.

There is a wider lesson for the whole Kincardineshire branch of folklore. Not every old story should be polished into mystery. Some should be handled as warnings. The witch-pool tradition remembers a world in which suspicion could become procedure, procedure could become punishment, and punishment could later become a half-told local tale. Its value today lies in reversing that softening: seeing the accused not as monsters in a legend, but as people caught in a dangerous system of belief.

## A careful way to read the place

The most balanced reading of the Dunnottar witch-pool is this: it is a locally grounded tradition, preserved in antiquarian folklore writing, that links the Carron Water near Dunnottar Church with the Scottish water ordeal for accused witches. It is not a licence to invent names, executions or supernatural claims beyond the evidence. It is a prompt to remember how accusation worked and how landscapes store moral memory.

For Kincardineshire, that makes the witch-pool a small but significant site of “dark folklore”. It belongs beside the county’s wells, stones, castles and customs, but it asks for a different tone. The story is not that witches haunted the Carron. The story is that people once believed water could judge the accused, and that the memory of that belief still clings to a Kincardineshire pool.
