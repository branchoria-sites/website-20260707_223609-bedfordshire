## Introduction

Charlotte Louisa Hawkins Dempster is one of the main reasons [Sutherland]({{ 'where-sutherlands-old-stories-still/' | relative_url }})’s nineteenth-century folklore is still readable today. In 1859 she gathered tales, sayings, beliefs, riddles and sketches from people in Sutherland, later publishing much of the material as “The Folk-Lore of Sutherlandshire” in *The Folk-Lore Journal* in 1888. Her work matters because it preserves a county-level record of fairies, changelings, water beings, omens, animal lore and heroic legend at a moment when Gaelic oral storytelling was under pressure from social change, schooling, print culture and religious disapproval. It also matters because Dempster did not simply “save” the material unchanged: she selected it, translated or mediated it for an English-reading audience, and framed it through the comparative folklore interests of her own time.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Folk-Lore_Journal/Volume_6/Folk-Lore_of_Sutherlandshire_%28September%29" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: en.wikisource.org">[Wikisource]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">en.wikisource.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Folk-Lore Journal/Volume 6/Folk-Lore of Sutherlandshire (September</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Folk-Lore Journal/Volume 6/Folk-Lore of Sutherlandshire (September) - Wikisource, the free online library...</span></span></span>

## Collecting tales in 1859

Dempster’s Sutherland collection began not as a late-Victorian library exercise but as field collection. In her own introduction, she said she had gathered “these tales and sayings from the mouths of the folk” in the summer of 1859, and she thanked the local people from whom she had received the lore. That opening sentence is important: it places the work close to oral transmission rather than treating Sutherland folklore as something invented by later writers for tourists or antiquarian romance.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Folk-Lore_Journal/Volume_6/Folk-Lore_of_Sutherlandshire_%28September%29" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: en.wikisource.org">[Wikisource]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">en.wikisource.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Folk-Lore Journal/Volume 6/Folk-Lore of Sutherlandshire (September</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Folk-Lore Journal/Volume 6/Folk-Lore of Sutherlandshire (September) - Wikisource, the free online library...</span></span></span>

The manuscript record is still identifiable. The National Library of Scotland lists *Tales from the people of Sutherland*: stories, beliefs, riddles, etc., collected by Miss Charlotte Louisa Dempster, dated 1859, as MS.2140 within the Society of Antiquaries of Scotland manuscript collection. Its subject headings include folk tales, riddles, short stories, sketches and indexes, which shows that the surviving record was broader than a neat set of supernatural legends alone.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://manuscripts.nls.uk/repositories/2/archival_objects/25033" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: manuscripts.nls.uk">[Manuscripts Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">manuscripts.nls.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">1859. &#124; Archives and Manuscripts Catalogue...</span></span></span>

The route from manuscript to print was not instant. Dempster said that two of the stories had already appeared through John Francis Campbell’s *Popular Tales of the West Highlands*, while the others were being added to the store of material that the Folk-Lore Society existed to preserve. The National Library of Scotland catalogue confirms that most of the tales were later published as “The Folk-lore of Sutherlandshire” in volume six of *The Folk-Lore Journal* in 1888.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Folk-Lore_Journal/Volume_6/Folk-Lore_of_Sutherlandshire_%28September%29" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: en.wikisource.org">[Wikisource]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">en.wikisource.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Folk-Lore Journal/Volume 6/Folk-Lore of Sutherlandshire (September</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Folk-Lore Journal/Volume 6/Folk-Lore of Sutherlandshire (September) - Wikisource, the free online library...</span></span></span>

That delay gives the collection a double time-depth. The tales were gathered in 1859, but they reached the organised folklore world nearly thirty years later, when folklore collecting had become a more formal scholarly and antiquarian movement. The Folklore Society itself was established in 1878, and its journal history runs from *The Folk-Lore Record* to *The Folk-Lore Journal* and then *Folklore*, so Dempster’s Sutherland material sits at a crossing-point between earlier Highland oral collecting and the emerging institutions of folklore study.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.folklore-society.com/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: folklore-society.com">[folklore-society.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">folklore-society.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Folklore SocietyThe Folklore Society, established in 1878, is a venerable institution dedicated to the study and preservation of folk...</span></span></span>

## What Dempster preserved and shaped

The printed “Folk-Lore of Sutherlandshire” is valuable because it does not preserve only one kind of story. Its first part begins with legends such as “The Death of Sweno”, where Norse-sounding maritime memory, dangerous headlands and named places such as Cape Wrath help root story in the northern seaboard. Later parts move into fairy stories, changelings, encounters by lochs and hills, and a “mythical zoology” that includes a white otter with a jewel in its forehead.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Folk-Lore_Journal/Volume_6/Folk-Lore_of_Sutherlandshire_%28September%29" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: en.wikisource.org">[Wikisource]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">en.wikisource.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Folk-Lore Journal/Volume 6/Folk-Lore of Sutherlandshire (September</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Folk-Lore Journal/Volume 6/Folk-Lore of Sutherlandshire (September) - Wikisource, the free online library...</span></span></span>

For a reader exploring Sutherland folklore, this variety is the point. Dempster’s record does not present the county as the home of one famous monster or one tidy myth-cycle. It gives a landscape of belief: crofts, barns, lochs, dogs with bad names, uncanny children, hidden beings, dangerous water, and animals that are half natural history and half wonder. In the fairy-changeling tale, for example, a crofting household and an ordinary farm servant become the setting for a story about a child who is not what it seems.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Folk-Lore_Journal/Volume_6/Folk-Lore_of_Sutherlandshire_%28December%29" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: en.wikisource.org">[Wikisource]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">en.wikisource.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Folk-Lore Journal/Volume 6/Folk-Lore of Sutherlandshire (December</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Folk-Lore Journal/Volume 6/Folk-Lore of Sutherlandshire (December</span></span></span>

Her material is also anchored in recognisable Sutherland places. One supernatural woman or water-being is met by a loch on the south side of Ben Stack; elsewhere Dempster’s text names Cape Wrath and other coastal danger-spots. These details matter because folklore is not just a set of plots. It is a way of making landscape memorable: a loch becomes a place of encounter, a hill gains an underworld, and a sea passage carries warning as well as geography.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Folk-Lore_Journal/Volume_6/Folk-Lore_of_Sutherlandshire_%28September%29" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: en.wikisource.org">[Wikisource]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">en.wikisource.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Folk-Lore Journal/Volume 6/Folk-Lore of Sutherlandshire (September</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Folk-Lore Journal/Volume 6/Folk-Lore of Sutherlandshire (September) - Wikisource, the free online library...</span></span></span>

Dempster also preserved traces of how stories were changing. She believed that some tales had a “measured prose” suggesting older formal composition, but she also argued that print, religious hostility and “modern progress” were displacing unwritten literature. Her wording is Victorian and judgemental in places, especially when she describes social division and the loss of “primitive life”, but the central observation is still useful: by the late nineteenth century, she saw Sutherland oral tradition as something already fragile.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Folk-Lore_Journal/Volume_6/Folk-Lore_of_Sutherlandshire_%28September%29" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: en.wikisource.org">[Wikisource]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">en.wikisource.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Folk-Lore Journal/Volume 6/Folk-Lore of Sutherlandshire (September</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Folk-Lore Journal/Volume 6/Folk-Lore of Sutherlandshire (September) - Wikisource, the free online library...</span></span></span>

The shaping hand is just as visible as the saving hand. Dempster told readers that she had annotated the material with parallels from other lands, while insisting that the notes illustrated rather than altered the folklore of Sutherland. That is a revealing distinction. She wanted the tales to stand as local evidence, but she also wanted them to make sense within the comparative folklore habits of her period, when collectors often read local traditions beside classical myth, European wonder-tales and “primitive” religion.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Folk-Lore_Journal/Volume_6/Folk-Lore_of_Sutherlandshire_%28September%29" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: en.wikisource.org">[Wikisource]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">en.wikisource.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Folk-Lore Journal/Volume 6/Folk-Lore of Sutherlandshire (September</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Folk-Lore Journal/Volume 6/Folk-Lore of Sutherlandshire (September) - Wikisource, the free online library...</span></span></span>

## The collector behind the page

Dempster was not only a folklore collector. At the Circulating Library’s Victorian fiction database identifies Charlotte Louisa Hawkins Dempster as a Scottish author born in 1835, raised partly in the Highlands, later resident in France, and active as a novelist, travel writer, Catholic devotional writer and contributor to periodicals. It also notes that she made many contributions to the Folklore Society’s journal and died in Cannes in 1913.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.victorianresearch.org/atcl/show_author.php?aid=2012" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: victorianresearch.org">[victorianresearch.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">victorianresearch.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">show author</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Author: Charlotte Louisa Hawkins Dempster...</span></span></span>

That background helps explain both the strengths and limits of her Sutherland record. She had literary skill, social access and a preservationist instinct. She also wrote from the position of an educated Victorian author addressing readers who were mostly outside the communities whose stories she recorded. Her prose can be vivid and grateful, but it is not a transparent recording device. It turns oral material into publishable English print.

This is not a reason to dismiss her. It is a reason to read her carefully. Without Dempster, much of this Sutherland material might be far harder to find; with Dempster, readers receive it through a particular nineteenth-century voice. The useful question is not “was she perfectly neutral?” but “what did her collecting preserve, what did her editing emphasise, and what might have been lost between performance, manuscript and journal publication?”

Her connection with John Francis Campbell is especially important. Campbell’s *Popular Tales of the West Highlands* was one of the landmark nineteenth-century projects for Scottish Gaelic oral narrative, and Dempster’s own introduction says that two of her stories had already been printed by him. The National Library of Scotland manuscript description also notes entries by various writers, including Campbell of Islay, with an index and a sketch. That places her work inside a wider Highland collecting network rather than as an isolated local notebook.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Folk-Lore_Journal/Volume_6/Folk-Lore_of_Sutherlandshire_%28September%29" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: en.wikisource.org">[Wikisource]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">en.wikisource.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Folk-Lore Journal/Volume 6/Folk-Lore of Sutherlandshire (September</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Folk-Lore Journal/Volume 6/Folk-Lore of Sutherlandshire (September) - Wikisource, the free online library...</span></span></span>

## Reading the old record without flattening it

The first rule for reading Dempster well is to keep the oral source and the printed text distinct. The tales may have come from Sutherland tellers, but the version most readers meet today is an English-language Victorian publication. A fairy tale in the journal is therefore both a trace of local oral tradition and a literary artefact shaped by collection, transcription, selection and publication.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Folk-Lore_Journal/Volume_6/Folk-Lore_of_Sutherlandshire_%28September%29" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: en.wikisource.org">[Wikisource]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">en.wikisource.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Folk-Lore Journal/Volume 6/Folk-Lore of Sutherlandshire (September</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Folk-Lore Journal/Volume 6/Folk-Lore of Sutherlandshire (September) - Wikisource, the free online library...</span></span></span>

The second rule is to avoid treating every item as equally ancient. Dempster herself noticed that some tales sounded formally composed, while others read more like sayings, beliefs or short notes. A changeling narrative, a place-legend, a riddle and a passing belief about an animal do not all have the same kind of evidence behind them. The National Library’s genre headings are helpful here because they remind us that the manuscript is a mixed folklore record, not a single mythological scripture.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://manuscripts.nls.uk/repositories/2/archival_objects/25033" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: manuscripts.nls.uk">[Manuscripts Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">manuscripts.nls.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">1859. &#124; Archives and Manuscripts Catalogue...</span></span></span>

The third rule is to resist turning Sutherland folklore into generic “Celtic myth”. Dempster’s comparative notes can be useful, and later writers such as David MacRitchie drew on her published work when discussing fairies, Fians and Picts. But Sutherland’s value lies in its local grain: Ben Stack, Cape Wrath, croft households, lochs, dogs, otters and the northern Highland setting. Comparison should illuminate those details, not wash them away.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/files/17926/17926-h/17926-h.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Project Gutenberg eBook of Fians, Fairies and Picts, by David MacRitchie...</span></span></span>

The fourth rule is to notice what the record cannot tell us. Dempster rarely gives the full social setting a modern folklorist would want: who exactly told each tale, in what language, before what audience, with what gestures, pauses, local jokes or remembered family associations. That absence is normal for much nineteenth-century folklore collecting, but it matters. It means the collection is strong evidence that these story types and beliefs were circulating in or around Sutherland, not a complete record of how every community in the county told them.

## Why this one collector matters for Sutherland

For Sutherland, Dempster’s importance is unusually large because so much of the county’s printed folklore record passes through her work. She gives the historic county a documented nineteenth-century voice at a time when oral tradition was being treated as something that might soon disappear. Her introduction may sound elegiac, but it is also practical: she saw loss coming and put material into circulation before it vanished from easy reach.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Folk-Lore_Journal/Volume_6/Folk-Lore_of_Sutherlandshire_%28September%29" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: en.wikisource.org">[Wikisource]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">en.wikisource.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Folk-Lore Journal/Volume 6/Folk-Lore of Sutherlandshire (September</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Folk-Lore Journal/Volume 6/Folk-Lore of Sutherlandshire (September) - Wikisource, the free online library...</span></span></span>

Her record also helps balance the way Sutherland is remembered. The county is often approached through castles, clearances, crofting history, wild scenery and North Coast routes. Dempster adds another layer: a Sutherland of spoken warnings, fairy households, uncanny children, water encounters, animal marvels and inherited phrases. These are not distractions from history. They show how people made sense of danger, illness, landscape, luck, death and social change.

The best modern use of Dempster is neither blind celebration nor easy dismissal. She should be read as a crucial preserver whose work is also a filter. Through her, Sutherland folklore survived in a form that later readers, scholars and local-history writers could quote, compare and retell. Through her, too, the county’s oral traditions entered a Victorian world that liked to classify, moralise and compare. The result is not silence, but not pure speech either: it is a record, and like all records it asks to be read with gratitude and care.
