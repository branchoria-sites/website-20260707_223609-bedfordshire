## Introduction

[Lancashire]({{ 'where-lancashire-folklore-still-haunts/' | relative_url }}) boggarts were unsettling because they did not stay in one neat category. In the same county tradition, a boggart could be a noisy household pest, a labouring goblin, a death-warning apparition, a horse-shaped farm helper, a headless lane-haunter, or the spirit of a wooded hollow. That slipperiness is the point: boggarts made ordinary places feel unstable. A kitchen, barn, bridge, old road or clough could suddenly become a place where noises had intention and misfortune had a name.



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/Lancashire_07656d_boggarts_dfb6a4-overview.webp" | relative_url }}" alt="Overview image for Boggarts" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

The tradition belongs especially strongly to historic Lancashire, including Manchester and other places now often discussed under Greater Manchester or Merseyside. Historic-county framing matters here because south Lancashire, Manchester, Blackley, Burnley, Poulton-le-Fylde and Furness all sit inside the older Lancashire world in which boggart stories were collected, retold and localised. The Association of British Counties describes historic Lancashire as running from the Mersey to Morecambe Bay, with Furness north of the sands, and lists Manchester among its main towns.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://abcounties.com/counties/county-profiles/lancashire/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: abcounties.com">[Association of British Counties]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">abcounties.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Association of British Counties Lancashire &#124; Association of British Counties</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Association of British Counties Lancashire &#124; Association of British Counties</span></span></span>

## House boggarts made the home unreliable

The most memorable Lancashire house boggart is not just a monster in a room. It is a mechanism for turning domestic routine into anxiety. In John Harland and T. T. Wilkinson’s Victorian collection *Lancashire Folk-lore*, the Rev. William Thornber is cited as distinguishing several sorts of boggart around the Fylde: ghosts of murderers or victims, death-warning family apparitions, horse-boggarts, and “house-boggarts” or mischievous imps haunting dwellings.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/files/41148/41148-h/41148-h.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Project Gutenberg eBook of Lancashire Folk-lore, by John Harland and T. T. Wilkinson...</span></span></span>

That range explains why the Lancashire boggart is so difficult to pin down. Some boggarts were close to ghosts, especially when tied to murder, injustice or death warnings. Others behaved more like brownies or hobs: household spirits that might work, help or interfere. The Hackensall Hall boggart, near the Fylde coast, was said to appear as a huge horse, work industriously if treated kindly, and complain with frightening cries if denied the warmth of a fire.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/files/41148/41148-h/41148-h.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Project Gutenberg eBook of Lancashire Folk-lore, by John Harland and T. T. Wilkinson...</span></span></span>

The frightening part was not always violence. It was intrusion. The boggart acted inside the household’s ordinary systems: sleep, bedding, food, milk, grain, horses and farm work. Harland and Wilkinson record a nineteenth-century report from Burnley Wood in which a boy said a boggart had driven William Clarke from his house because it would not let the family sleep and stripped off the bedclothes. The story is short, but it captures the domestic pattern perfectly: the boggart does not need to appear clearly to make the house unliveable.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/files/41148/41148-h/41148-h.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Project Gutenberg eBook of Lancashire Folk-lore, by John Harland and T. T. Wilkinson...</span></span></span>

This is why boggart stories sit close to what modern readers might call poltergeist stories, while still belonging to an older North West vocabulary. The disturbance is physical enough to be felt, but uncertain enough to remain a rumour. Clothes are pulled away. Objects shift. Sleep is broken. Milk is spoiled. Something is heard in the night, but not necessarily seen. The fear comes from the household no longer behaving like a safe interior.

## The “flitting” story says there may be no escape

One of the most revealing boggart tales is the “Boggart Flitting”. In Lancashire dialect, to flit is to move house. The basic story is simple: a family tries to escape a troublesome household boggart by loading its goods onto a cart, only to discover that the boggart is coming with them. Harland and Wilkinson note that the tale was common to Lancashire and Yorkshire and had parallels across northern Europe.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/files/41148/41148-h/41148-h.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Project Gutenberg eBook of Lancashire Folk-lore, by John Harland and T. T. Wilkinson...</span></span></span>

Its importance is not that it proves one specific Lancashire haunting. It shows how people imagined the boggart’s attachment. A ghost might be tied to a murder site; a water spirit to a pool; a black dog to a road. But a household boggart could belong to the family’s life rather than merely to the building. Moving furniture did not solve the problem, because the trouble moved with the household.

That idea gives the story its bleak humour. The family’s practical solution is defeated by the boggart’s social closeness. It is not a distant demon but an unwanted member of the domestic economy. It understands what the family is doing. It joins the move. It turns the human plan into a joke.

For a Lancashire folklore page, this matters because it shows the boggart as a mechanism rather than a fixed creature. It is a way of explaining why trouble repeats: why a household feels unlucky, why a farm will not run smoothly, why a person cannot simply leave misfortune behind. The boggart makes disturbance portable.

## Boggart Hole Clough turned a hollow into a haunted landscape

Boggart Hole Clough, at Blackley in north Manchester, is the best-known Lancashire boggart place. Today Manchester City Council lists it as a public park on Charlestown Road, with play areas, an athletics track, bowling green, boating lake, fishing area, café and visitor facilities.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.manchester.gov.uk/online-directories/parks-leisure-and-the-arts-directories/parks-playgrounds-allotments-and-open-spaces-directories/parks-and-open-spaces/all-parks-playgrounds-and-open-spaces/boggart-hole-clough" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: manchester.gov.uk">[Manchester City Council]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">manchester.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Manchester City Council Boggart Hole Clough &#124; Manchester City Council</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Manchester City Council Boggart Hole Clough &#124; Manchester City Council</span></span></span> The strangeness of the place is that this ordinary civic park sits on top of a much older legendary landscape.

Ceri Houlbrook’s study of the “suburban boggart” describes Boggart Hole Clough as less than three miles north of Manchester city centre, covering 171 acres and containing playgrounds, an athletics track, a lake, brooks and several deep, wooded cloughs. The article explains that “clough” is a local word for a steep-sided wooded valley, and that the site survived deforestation partly through its former use as a deer park.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://sussexfolktalecentre.org/wp-content/uploads/Suburban-Boggart.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sussexfolktalecentre.org">[sussexfolktalecentre.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sussexfolktalecentre.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Layout 1</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Layout 1</span></span></span>

In the nineteenth century, however, the clough was not simply a municipal leisure space. Houlbrook notes that the place was still rural when John Roby described it in *Traditions of Lancashire* in 1829, and that Roby introduced the famous story of a Blackley farmer, George Cheetham, whose farmhouse was haunted by a mischievous boggart. In that version, the boggart steals children’s bread and butter, throws down milk, makes night noises, yet sometimes churns cream and scours pans.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://sussexfolktalecentre.org/wp-content/uploads/Suburban-Boggart.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sussexfolktalecentre.org">[sussexfolktalecentre.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sussexfolktalecentre.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Layout 1</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Layout 1</span></span></span>

That mixture of nuisance and help is crucial. The Boggart Hole Clough creature is not merely a woodland monster. It connects the house to the hollow. In Roby’s telling, the explanation of the place-name is domestic: a “hole” in a partition near the stairs becomes the boggart’s peeping place. But the wider landscape pulls the story outward into ravines, trees, brooks and night-time fear.

Houlbrook also points out an important caution. Roby’s account may not be a straightforward record of Blackley oral tradition. The story was supplied through Thomas Crofton Croker, and the folklorist Jennifer Westwood argued that there is no clear evidence local people knew that exact George Cheetham tale before Roby printed it. Houlbrook describes it as borrowed folklore used to explain an odd local name.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://sussexfolktalecentre.org/wp-content/uploads/Suburban-Boggart.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sussexfolktalecentre.org">[sussexfolktalecentre.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sussexfolktalecentre.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Layout 1</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Layout 1</span></span></span>

That does not make Boggart Hole Clough unimportant. It makes it more interesting. The clough became a collecting point for traditions: printed legend, local fear, children’s warnings, place-name curiosity, and later urban park identity. Charles Hardwick’s observation, quoted by Houlbrook, is useful here: once a place is called Boggart Hole Clough, especially such a place, neighbouring floating traditions can easily fasten themselves to it.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://sussexfolktalecentre.org/wp-content/uploads/Suburban-Boggart.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sussexfolktalecentre.org">[sussexfolktalecentre.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sussexfolktalecentre.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Layout 1</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Layout 1</span></span></span>

## The stronger local strand may be the haunted clough, not one named monster

If Roby’s farmhouse boggart is partly literary or borrowed, the clough still had older supernatural force. Houlbrook presents Samuel Bamford’s 1844 account as a better reflection of local belief. In Bamford’s story, three local men enter “Boggart” or “Fyrin-Ho” Clough after nightfall to gather St John’s fern seed, a plant associated in folk belief with magical power. What they meet is not a single named boggart but a whole haunted environment: uncanny figures, mournful voices, frightening forms in the thickets, and a crash as if timber were being torn apart.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://sussexfolktalecentre.org/wp-content/uploads/Suburban-Boggart.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sussexfolktalecentre.org">[sussexfolktalecentre.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sussexfolktalecentre.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Layout 1</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Layout 1</span></span></span>

This difference matters. Roby gives readers a tidy origin story: a mischievous house spirit explains the place-name. Bamford gives something more atmospheric and probably more local: the clough itself is a threshold place. Its steep wooded sides, shadows, watercourses and night sounds create the setting in which people expect strange encounters.

Later writers added further variations. Houlbrook notes that George Milner treated the farmhouse boggart as a homely sprite that had gone when the farmhouse ceased to be a home, while Charles Roeder described a more crowded supernatural landscape, including a cow-shaped boggart, a shrieking nut-man among hazels, and an evil spirit in a pit.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://sussexfolktalecentre.org/wp-content/uploads/Suburban-Boggart.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sussexfolktalecentre.org">[sussexfolktalecentre.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sussexfolktalecentre.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Layout 1</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Layout 1</span></span></span>

The result is not one clean legend but a cluster. Boggart Hole Clough is a good example of how Lancashire folklore often works: a place-name, a dramatic landscape, printed antiquarian writing, local memory and children’s warnings all reinforce one another. The boggart is less a single character than a permission for the place to feel haunted.

## Old lanes, bridges and named hollows made fear local

Lancashire boggarts were not restricted to famous parks. The older material repeatedly ties them to lanes, halls, bridges, woods and cloughs. Harland and Wilkinson cite the “headless Boggart of White-gate Lane” as one example from the Fylde, while also mentioning Staining Hall near Blackpool and the Walmsley family of Poulton-le-Fylde, whose boggart appeared with alarming noises before a death.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/files/41148/41148-h/41148-h.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Project Gutenberg eBook of Lancashire Folk-lore, by John Harland and T. T. Wilkinson...</span></span></span>

These examples show how elastic the word was. A boggart could mean a domestic imp, but it could also mean a ghostly warning attached to a family or a lane. In a road tradition, the boggart often works like a black dog, a bogle or a death omen: something met outdoors, especially where the route is lonely, dark or morally charged by an old story of murder, suicide or injustice.

Place-names matter because they preserve fear in the map. Simon Young’s study “What is a Boggart Hole?” argues that boggart place-names had been neglected despite many still being in use, and that by the nineteenth century the word was especially associated with “greater Lancashire”: the County Palatine, the south Pennines and nearby fringes of Cheshire and Derbyshire.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.snsbi.org.uk/Nomina_articles/Nomina_37_Young.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: snsbi.org.uk">[SNSBI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">snsbi.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on snsbi.org.uk.</span></span></span> Young identifies Boggart Holes as a particularly interesting class of name, with fourteen known examples: eight in Lancashire, four in Yorkshire and two in Derbyshire.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.snsbi.org.uk/Nomina_articles/Nomina_37_Young.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: snsbi.org.uk">[SNSBI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">snsbi.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on snsbi.org.uk.</span></span></span>

That distribution fits the landscape. “Hole” in these names need not mean a neat cave. In local usage and folklore it can point to a depression, ravine, hollow, pit, wooded clough or lair-like place where people imagine something going down into the earth. This is why boggart traditions so often cling to cloughs, bridges, pits and lanes rather than to grand monuments.

Burnley gives a useful edge case. Red Rose Collections, Lancashire’s local image archive, records Boggart Bridge pit and dam at Copy Clough, Habergham Eaves, with the colliery open from 1865 to 1949.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://redrosecollections.lancashire.gov.uk/view-item?i=313801" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: redrosecollections.lancashire.gov.uk">[redrosecollections.lancashire.gov.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">redrosecollections.lancashire.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Boggart Bridge Colliery, Burnley</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Boggart Bridge Colliery, Burnley</span></span></span> Modern retellings connect Boggart Bridge with a tribute-demanding boggart under the bridge, but the stronger documented point for this page is the persistence of the name itself in a wooded, industrialised east Lancashire setting. It shows how boggart language could survive into colliery, bridge and clough geography, even when the attached story is thin or variable.

## Noises were the boggart’s strongest evidence

A repeated feature in Lancashire boggart material is sound. The creature is often heard before it is seen, or heard instead of being seen. That makes sense in the settings where these stories live: a farmhouse at night, a barn, a wooded ravine, a bridge, a hollow lane, a pit, a clough in wind and darkness.

In domestic stories, sound turns the house against its occupants. Harland and Wilkinson’s Burnley Wood report focuses on sleeplessness and bedclothes stripped away; the Grislehurst material they cite includes carts overturned at night and goods shifted around the house while people are in bed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/files/41148/41148-h/41148-h.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Project Gutenberg eBook of Lancashire Folk-lore, by John Harland and T. T. Wilkinson...</span></span></span> In the Hackensall Hall tradition, the horse-shaped boggart’s anger is expressed through terrible outcries when the expected fire is neglected.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/files/41148/41148-h/41148-h.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Project Gutenberg eBook of Lancashire Folk-lore, by John Harland and T. T. Wilkinson...</span></span></span>

In Boggart Hole Clough, sound becomes landscape-scale. Bamford’s men hear and experience the clough as if trees are being torn apart, while Roeder’s later account includes shrieks among hazel bushes and the uncanny lowing of a cow-shaped boggart.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://sussexfolktalecentre.org/wp-content/uploads/Suburban-Boggart.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sussexfolktalecentre.org">[sussexfolktalecentre.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sussexfolktalecentre.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Layout 1</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Layout 1</span></span></span> These details are more than decoration. They show how a boggart gives agency to ambiguous noise: wind, animals, water, timber, night movement, settlement sounds and fear itself.

For readers today, this is one of the easiest ways to understand the tradition without reducing it to “people were superstitious”. Boggart stories often begin where explanation is incomplete. A noise is heard. A family is frightened. A hollow has a reputation. A lane is avoided. The boggart is the name that lets the story travel.

## Thin evidence is part of the tradition, not a flaw to hide

Some Lancashire boggart stories are well attested as printed traditions; others are thin, late, borrowed or heavily reshaped. That distinction should not be flattened. Boggart Hole Clough has strong nineteenth-century literary and antiquarian documentation, but the exact George Cheetham farmhouse tale may have been imported into the place-name explanation rather than preserved from Blackley oral tradition.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://sussexfolktalecentre.org/wp-content/uploads/Suburban-Boggart.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sussexfolktalecentre.org">[sussexfolktalecentre.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sussexfolktalecentre.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Layout 1</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Layout 1</span></span></span>

Other named boggarts survive as brief references: White-gate Lane, Staining Hall, Poulton-le-Fylde, Rayscar, Inskip, Hackensall Hall, Towneley and Burnley Wood. Harland and Wilkinson’s collection is invaluable, but it often reports traditions through earlier antiquaries, local clergy, dialect writers or unnamed informants. That is typical of nineteenth-century folklore collecting, where the line between oral memory, literary shaping and antiquarian interpretation can be hard to draw.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/files/41148/41148-h/41148-h.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Project Gutenberg eBook of Lancashire Folk-lore, by John Harland and T. T. Wilkinson...</span></span></span>

This does not make the material useless. It tells us how boggarts functioned in Lancashire imagination. They were not one species with a fixed appearance. They were a local language of disturbance. A place could have “its boggart” in the same way it might have its ghost, omen, curse or dangerous reputation.

Modern folklore has continued that process. Manchester Archives+ describes the Boggart Hole Clough creature through local memory tapes by Paul Graney, including a version in which the boggart lived behind boards in a kitchen on Bell’s farm at Moston Dell and caused chaos with cows, milk and hens’ eggs.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://manchesterarchiveplus.wordpress.com/2016/10/27/manchesters-boggarts/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: manchesterarchiveplus.wordpress.com">[Manchester Archive Plus]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">manchesterarchiveplus.wordpress.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Manchester Archive Plus Manchester’s Boggarts</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Manchester Archive Plus Manchester’s Boggarts</span></span></span> Houlbrook’s study also shows twenty-first-century reuse of the clough’s folklore through visitor-centre material, school activities, community interpretation and commercial adaptation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://sussexfolktalecentre.org/wp-content/uploads/Suburban-Boggart.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sussexfolktalecentre.org">[sussexfolktalecentre.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sussexfolktalecentre.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Layout 1</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Layout 1</span></span></span>

## Why Lancashire boggarts still feel distinctive

Lancashire boggarts matter because they belong to everyday geography. They are not only attached to castles, abbeys or remote moorland. They appear in working houses, farm kitchens, barns, lanes, cloughs, bridges, pits and later public parks. That gives them a close fit with Lancashire’s historic identity: rural and industrial, domestic and public, old-road and urban, printed and oral.

They also connect several neighbouring folklore branches without being swallowed by any of them. Like house fairies or brownies, some boggarts work if properly treated. Like ghosts, some are tied to murder, injustice or death warnings. Like black dogs and bogles, some belong to lanes and night roads. Like fairy-place traditions, Boggart Hole Clough turns a landscape feature into a place where ordinary rules seem thinner.

The most useful way to read Lancashire boggart lore is therefore not to ask, “What did a boggart really look like?” The better question is, “What kind of place did this story make?” In a house, the boggart made rest impossible. In a clough, it made darkness and sound feel inhabited. In an old lane, it made a route memorable and risky. In a place-name, it preserved a warning long after the exact tale had faded.

      button.setAttribute('aria-pressed', active ? 'true' : 'false');
    });
    section.setAttribute('data-ebay-selected-market', marketId);
    if (persist) {
      try { if (window.localStorage) localStorage.setItem('phoenix-ebay-market', marketId); } catch (err) {}
    }
    closeMenu(section);
  }
  sections.forEach(function (section) {
    if (section.getAttribute('data-ebay-localized-init') === '1') return;
    section.setAttribute('data-ebay-localized-init', '1');
    var available = availableMarkets(section);
    var marketId = defaultMarket;
    if (window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation && window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation.inferMarket) {
      marketId = window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation.inferMarket(available, {
        storageKey: 'phoenix-ebay-market',
        defaultMarket: defaultMarket
      });
    } else if (available.indexOf(defaultMarket)< 0) {
      marketId = available[0] || defaultMarket;
    }
    var select = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-select]');
    if (select) {
      select.addEventListener('change', function () {
        applyMarket(section, select.value, true);
      });
    }
    var trigger = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-trigger]');
    var menu = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-menu]');
    if (trigger && menu) {
      trigger.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
        event.stopPropagation();
        var expanded = trigger.getAttribute('aria-expanded') === 'true';
        menu.hidden = expanded;
        trigger.setAttribute('aria-expanded', expanded ? 'false' : 'true');
        var picker = trigger.closest('.fr-ebay-market-picker');
        if (picker) picker.classList.toggle('fr-ebay-market-picker--open', !expanded);
      });
      Array.prototype.slice.call(menu.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-option]')).forEach(function (button) {
        button.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
          event.stopPropagation();
          applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-option'), true);
        });
      });
      document.addEventListener('click', function () { closeMenu(section); });
    }
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      button.addEventListener('click', function () {
        applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button'), true);
      });
    });
    applyMarket(section, marketId, false);
  });
