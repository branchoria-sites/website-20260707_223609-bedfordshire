## Introduction

Stonehenge is one of [Wiltshire]({{ 'wiltshires-legends-written-across-the/' | relative_url }})’s most powerful meeting points between archaeology and legend. The prehistoric monument on Salisbury Plain has inspired stories of giants, the wizard Merlin, King Arthur’s ancestors and mysterious Druids, not because these tales explain how Stonehenge was actually built, but because later generations struggled to understand a structure whose original makers left no written account. The result is a rich layer of folklore built around one of Britain’s oldest landscapes.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.english-heritage.org.uk/siteassets/home/visit/places-to-visit/stonehenge/school-visits/stonehenge-explorer-backpacks.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: english-heritage.org.uk">[English Heritage]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">english-heritage.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">stonehenge explorer backpacks</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">English HeritageSTONEHENGE EXPLORERMay 4, 2016...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 4, 2016</span></span></span>

The oldest surviving legends about Stonehenge appear in medieval writing, especially the twelfth-century works of Geoffrey of Monmouth, while later antiquarians such as John Aubrey and William Stukeley reshaped the monument’s reputation by linking it with Druids and ancient British religion. Archaeology has shown that Stonehenge was built thousands of years before the historical Druids, but these stories remain central to Wiltshire’s cultural identity because they reveal how people have continued to give meaning to an extraordinary place.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stonehenge" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wikipedia.org.</span></span></span>

## Giants, Merlin and the medieval stories

### Why did medieval writers connect Stonehenge with giants?

For medieval people, Stonehenge was already an ancient mystery. Its enormous stones, carefully arranged on Salisbury Plain, belonged to a past that was beyond living memory. Without archaeological explanations, writers created stories that placed the monument within familiar traditions of kings, battles, magic and lost peoples.

The best-known account appears in Historia Regum Britanniae, written in the twelfth century. Geoffrey claimed that Stonehenge was originally a circle of magical healing stones in Ireland called the Giants’ Ring. According to the story, giants had brought the stones from Africa to Ireland, and later the British king Aurelius Ambrosius wanted them moved to Salisbury Plain as a memorial to warriors killed by the Saxons. Merlin used his magic to transport and rebuild the stone circle.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theories_about_Stonehenge" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Theories about Stonehenge</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Theories about Stonehenge</span></span></span>

The legend mixes several themes common in medieval storytelling: giants as the builders of impossible monuments, magical knowledge lost to ordinary people, and ancient sites connected to heroic rulers. The story was not intended as modern archaeology, but it shows how Stonehenge became part of Britain’s legendary past. Variations of Geoffrey’s account spread through later medieval works, helping to secure Merlin’s place in Stonehenge tradition.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stonehenge" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wikipedia.org.</span></span></span>

### Did the Merlin legend contain any historical memory?

The Merlin story is a myth rather than a record of Stonehenge’s construction. Archaeologists know that the monument was created during the Neolithic and Bronze Age, beginning around 3000 BC, long before the medieval period and long before the Druids described by Roman writers.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://time.com/5608296/summer-solstice-stonehenge-history/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: time.com">[Time]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">time.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Here&#x27;s Why Stonehenge Is Connected to the Summer Solstice</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">While the true purpose of Stonehenge remains a topic of speculation, experts like Timothy Darvill and Heather Sebire assert that the monu...</span></span></span>

However, some details of the legend have attracted discussion because they echo real questions about the monument. Geoffrey’s story says the stones came from Ireland, while archaeology has shown that Stonehenge’s bluestones came from western Wales, particularly the Preseli Hills. Modern research into possible earlier stone circles in Wales has renewed interest in the idea that some stones may have been moved from existing ceremonial sites, although this does not confirm the medieval tale.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.theguardian.com/culture/2022/jan/30/how-science-is-uncovering-the-secrets-of-stonehenge-british-museum-nebra-sky-disc-" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theguardian.com">[The Guardian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theguardian.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Central to this narrative is the British Museum&#x27;s 2022 exhibition, &quot;The World of Stonehenge,&quot; which showcases artifacts like intricately...</span></span></span>

This is typical of folklore around ancient monuments: a story may be historically inaccurate while still preserving a memory of something real, such as movement of stones, the importance of ancestors, or the idea that monuments were inherited from an older world.

## Druids and changing interpretations

### How did Stonehenge become known as a Druid monument?

The famous connection between Stonehenge and the Druids is not an ancient belief preserved unchanged. It developed mainly through early modern antiquarian study. John Aubrey examined Stonehenge in the seventeenth century, but the stronger Druid association came from William Stukeley, whose 1740 work *Stonehenge, A Temple Restor’d to the British Druids* presented the monument as a sacred temple of the Druids.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nationaltrustcollections.org.uk/object/220723" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nationaltrustcollections.org.uk">[National Trust Collections]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nationaltrustcollections.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Trust Collections Stukeley&#x27;s Stonehenge (on spine). Stukeley’s ideas were influential because they fitted the eighteenth-century fascination with ancient Britain, mysterious landscapes and imagined connections to pre-Roman traditions. He correctly recognised that Stonehenge had an important relationship with the movements of the Sun, but his belief that Druids built it was later shown to be impossible. The historical Druids belonged to the Iron Age and Roman periods, thousands of years after Stonehenge’s main construction phases.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://time.com/5608296/summer-solstice-stonehenge-history/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: time.com">[Time]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">time.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Here&#x27;s Why Stonehenge Is Connected to the Summer Solstice</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">While the true purpose of Stonehenge remains a topic of speculation, experts like Timothy Darvill and Heather Sebire assert that the monu...</span></span></span>

### Why does the Druid story still matter?

Although archaeology has separated Stonehenge from the Druids of Roman Britain, the association remains a major part of the monument’s modern folklore. Modern Druid and Pagan groups have continued to view Stonehenge as a meaningful ceremonial landscape, particularly around the solstices. The monument’s alignment with the summer solstice sunrise has helped reinforce its image as a place connected with seasonal cycles and ancient wisdom.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://time.com/5608296/summer-solstice-stonehenge-history/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: time.com">[Time]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">time.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Here&#x27;s Why Stonehenge Is Connected to the Summer Solstice</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">While the true purpose of Stonehenge remains a topic of speculation, experts like Timothy Darvill and Heather Sebire assert that the monu...</span></span></span>

The Druid tradition shows how folklore can be created not only by medieval storytellers but also by later generations searching for spiritual and cultural connections with the past. A legend does not have to be archaeologically accurate to become an important part of a place’s identity.

## Modern heritage and folklore

### How are Stonehenge myths retold today?

Today, Stonehenge legends continue through museums, heritage interpretation, tourism and popular culture. Visitors often encounter the stories of Merlin, giants and Druids alongside archaeological explanations of prehistoric engineering and ritual. Rather than replacing archaeology, these stories add another layer to understanding why Stonehenge has remained such a compelling place.

The contrast between evidence and legend is part of Stonehenge’s appeal. Archaeology reveals a monument created by prehistoric communities using enormous organisational skill, transporting stone across long distances and shaping a ceremonial landscape. Folklore explains how later communities imagined those achievements through stories of supernatural builders and forgotten powers.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.theguardian.com/culture/2022/jan/30/how-science-is-uncovering-the-secrets-of-stonehenge-british-museum-nebra-sky-disc-" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theguardian.com">[The Guardian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theguardian.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Central to this narrative is the British Museum&#x27;s 2022 exhibition, &quot;The World of Stonehenge,&quot; which showcases artifacts like intricately...</span></span></span>

### The Heel Stone and smaller traditions around the monument

Not all Stonehenge folklore centres on famous figures such as Merlin. Individual stones have also gathered local stories. The Heel Stone, positioned outside the main circle, has been associated with names such as “Friar’s Heel” and “Sun Stone”. Some traditional explanations connect the name with legends involving the Devil, while the stone’s relationship with the summer solstice sunrise has strengthened its symbolic importance.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stonehenge" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wikipedia.org.</span></span></span>

These smaller traditions show how folklore grows through repeated encounters with a landscape. A single stone, a name, a seasonal event or an unexplained feature can become part of a much larger story about a place.

Stonehenge’s legends remain important within Wiltshire because they demonstrate the continuing conversation between past and present. The monument itself belongs to prehistoric Britain, but the stories of giants, Merlin and Druids belong to the many later generations who have tried to understand, explain and remember this remarkable landscape.
