## Introduction

Galloway fairy places matter because they make the unseen feel stubbornly local. In and around [Kirkcudbrightshire]({{ 'kirkcudbrightshire/' | relative_url }}), fairies and brownies are not only creatures from a distant Celtic mist; they are attached to mounds, mills, burns, loch-sides, farms, byres, old roads and working households. The strongest traditions show a particular Galloway pattern: the landscape is treated as shared ground, and trouble begins when humans forget that a mound, a threshold or a night-time workplace might already belong to someone else.

For this Kirkcudbrightshire page, the centre of gravity is East Galloway, the historic Stewartry of Kirkcudbright, while recognising that some of the best-attested Galloway fairy material crosses into neighbouring Wigtownshire. Kirkcudbrightshire is the eastern part of Galloway on the north shore of the Solway Firth, and older county identity still matters because the tales were collected and retold through parish, estate and regional memory rather than modern council boundaries.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://wikishire.co.uk/wiki/Kirkcudbrightshire" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wikishire.co.uk">[Wikishire]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wikishire.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Kirkcudbrightshire19 Jan 2021 — The County and Stewartry of Kirkcudbright (pronounced Ker-coo-bree), otherwise known as East Gal...</span></span></span>

## Why fairy places cluster around edges

Galloway fairy tradition is strongest where the human world feels unfinished: a mound that may be prehistoric, a ruined castle on an older earthwork, a burn crossed at night, a byre after dark, a mill-lade, a hill path, or a woodland track. These are not random “spooky” locations. They are places where people once worked, travelled, grazed animals, carried water or negotiated access to land.

J. Maxwell Wood’s 1911 collection, *Witchcraft and Superstitious Record in the South-Western District of Scotland*, is the key older printed source for many of these traditions. Wood was not writing modern academic folklore, and he often repeated antiquarian theories of his time, but his book is valuable because it preserves Galloway and Dumfriesshire material on fairies, brownies, witchcraft, wraiths, death customs and haunted places in one place. In his own framing, he was gathering “remnants” of older belief as they survived in local memory.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/files/43966/43966-h/43966-h.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on gutenberg.org.</span></span></span>

One of Wood’s most revealing comments is that fairy and brownie lore often attached itself to places understood as ancient habitation sites. He repeats the old idea that fairies and brownies may have been remembered through stories of small “mound-dwellers”, linking local supernatural belief to prehistoric or early historic earthworks. That theory should not be treated as a simple explanation of fairy belief, but it does show how antiquarians tried to make sense of a striking pattern: in Galloway, old mounds and underground places were natural homes for stories about hidden neighbours.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/files/43966/43966-h/43966-h.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on gutenberg.org.</span></span></span>

This is why Galloway fairy places feel different from generic fairyland. They are not mainly delicate garden scenes. They are legal, agricultural and domestic places: the top of someone’s “house”, the route of a drain, the bank of a loch, the edge of a field, the corner of a barn. The tales ask a practical question beneath the enchantment: who really owns the land, and what happens when humans act as if they are the only occupants?

## Myrton Mound and the Galloway story-world

The most important Galloway example is Myrton Mound, even though it lies just west of Kirkcudbrightshire in the wider Galloway story-world. Wood calls it probably the most characteristic fairy story in the whole south-west of Scotland. That is a strong claim, and it is easy to see why he makes it: the tale combines a green mound, a ruined tower, a loch-side estate, the McCullochs of Myrton, the Maxwells of Monreith, a seventeenth-century execution, and a fairy or brownie king who intervenes in human justice.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/files/43966/43966-h/43966-h.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on gutenberg.org.</span></span></span>

The setting is unusually concrete. Wood places the mound within the policies of Monreith House, in Mochrum parish, on the wooded shore of the White Loch of Myrton. Modern heritage sources confirm that Myrton Castle stands within the Monreith landscape and that the White Loch is a major estate feature; Myrton Castle itself is a scheduled monument, and Monreith’s designed landscape records note the castle’s relationship to the loch and later estate layout.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://portal.historicenvironment.scot/apex/f?p=1505%3A300%3A%3A%3A%3A%3AVIEWTYPE%2CVIEWREF%3Adesignation%2CSM2022" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: portal.historicenvironment.scot">[historicenvironment.scot]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">portal.historicenvironment.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on historicenvironment.scot.</span></span></span>

In the story, Sir Godfrey McCulloch orders a sewer to be cut from Myrton Tower down towards the White Loch. A tiny old man in green appears in anger and announces that he is the king of the brownies, because the drain is being driven through his chamber inside the mound. McCulloch courteously stops the work and redirects the drain. In return, the brownie king promises help when the laird is in greatest need.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/files/43966/43966-h/43966-h.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on gutenberg.org.</span></span></span>

That promise is later fulfilled in spectacular fashion. The historical Sir Godfrey McCulloch was condemned after the shooting of William Gordon and executed in Edinburgh in 1697, but the local tale gives him a fairy escape. At the scaffold, a little white-haired rider on a white horse appears, McCulloch mounts behind him, and the two vanish back across the loch and into the castle rock. The body left behind is explained as a glamour, a fairy deception.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/files/43966/43966-h/43966-h.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on gutenberg.org.</span></span></span>

The tale matters for Kirkcudbrightshire because it shows how the region’s fairy imagination works. This is not a simple story of “nice fairies” or “bad fairies”. The hidden folk have property, rank, anger and memory. They punish disturbance, reward courtesy and can overturn human authority. In a county whose folklore also includes lairds, castles, [witch-trials]({{ 'witch-trials-26a95e/' | relative_url }}), saints’ sites and estate histories, Myrton Mound shows how supernatural tradition could become a second history running beneath the official one.

## Brownies: farm helpers with strict rules

Brownies are often grouped with fairies, but Galloway tradition gives them a more domestic and agricultural role. They work at night, help with farm tasks, handle animals, appear around byres and mills, and expect not wages but a modest acknowledgement such as food. The rules are delicate: a brownie may help if treated with respect, but can leave if paid too openly, clothed, mocked or brought under religious control.

Wood’s chapter moves from fairy mounds into brownie lore without treating them as wholly separate worlds. He presents the brownie as shy, tireless, physically strange and strongly associated with labour. That makes the brownie especially suited to the Stewartry’s farm culture, where supernatural help is imagined in the same terms as seasonal pressure: sheep to gather, crops to bring in, bread to turn, cattle and dairy work to manage.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/files/43966/43966-h/43966-h.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on gutenberg.org.</span></span></span>

The best-known Galloway brownie is Aiken-drum, the Brownie of Blednoch. The poem was written by William Nicholson, known as the Bard of Galloway; the Scottish Poetry Library identifies Nicholson as a native of Borgue in Kirkcudbrightshire and notes that “The Brownie of Blednoch” became his best-known work.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.scottishpoetrylibrary.org.uk/poet/william-nicholson/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: scottishpoetrylibrary.org.uk">[Scottish Poetry Library]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">scottishpoetrylibrary.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Scottish Poetry Library William Nicholson</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Scottish Poetry Library William Nicholson</span></span></span>

Nicholson’s brownie is frightening before he is helpful. He arrives as a strange, misshapen figure asking for work, terrifying the household, the children and even the dog. Once accepted, however, he becomes valuable: he can herd sheep, gather crops by moonlight, churn, bake and tame a wild filly. His requested “wage” is not money or clothing but a simple bowl of brose left between daylight and dark.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/files/43966/43966-h/43966-h.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on gutenberg.org.</span></span></span>

The ending gives the tradition its rule. A young wife leaves him a pair of trousers, and from that day Aiken-drum disappears. Wood’s text preserves the later lines that place him on the Blednoch and Cree, heard again near Threave and Glashnoch Mill, which helps bring a Wigtownshire brownie into the wider East Galloway imaginative map.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/files/43966/43966-h/43966-h.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on gutenberg.org.</span></span></span>

That detail is important. The Brownie of Blednoch is not a neat county-boundary tale; it travels through poetry, dialect, performance and regional memory. For Kirkcudbrightshire readers, its local force comes partly from Nicholson himself, a Borgue-born poet, and partly from the way the tale crosses the Cree and Dee country of Galloway rather than sitting still in one parish.

## What the brownie rules tell us about work

The brownie tradition is memorable because it turns labour into a relationship. A brownie is not a servant in the ordinary sense. He helps, but cannot be hired. He accepts food, but not wages. He may live beside a household, but not be treated as a household member who can be dressed, displayed or controlled.

That boundary is the whole point of the Blednoch story. Aiken-drum’s help is welcome while it remains informal and half-hidden. The gift of trousers changes the relationship. Clothing turns courtesy into payment, or perhaps tries to pull the brownie into human social order. The result is not gratitude but departure.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/files/43966/43966-h/43966-h.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on gutenberg.org.</span></span></span>

Other Scottish brownie traditions repeat the same pattern: they work at night, live close to homes or farms, and are easily offended by payment, clothing or attempts to dominate them. Modern summaries can make this sound cute, but the older Galloway material is sharper. A brownie is useful because he is not fully under human control. He belongs to the household economy, yet remains outside the household hierarchy.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://scotlands-stories.com/scottish-brownie/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: scotlands-stories.com">[Scotland&#x27;s Stories]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">scotlands-stories.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Scotland&#x27;s Stories The Scottish Brownie</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Scotland&#x27;s Stories The Scottish Brownie</span></span></span>

This helps explain why brownies suit working Galloway so well. Their stories belong to places where labour was seasonal, exhausting and often precarious. A farm that suddenly gets its sheep gathered or its harvest helped has received a blessing, but a fragile one. The tale preserves a moral economy: respect unseen labour, do not overreach, and do not assume that every helper can be converted into a paid servant.

## Mills, burns and night roads

Brownies and fairies in Galloway often appear near the practical infrastructure of rural life. Mills, burns, byres and roadways are not background scenery; they are the places where people heard noises, worked before dawn, carried food, met strangers, crossed water or feared being alone.

Wood’s quotation from Nicholson leaves Aiken-drum’s memory at Glashnoch Mill and the “roaring linn” after dark. This is exactly the kind of threshold place where a brownie tradition makes sense: water-power, night sound, labour and fear meet in one setting.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/files/43966/43966-h/43966-h.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on gutenberg.org.</span></span></span>

The same pattern appears in fairy material around water and domestic need. In Wood’s Galloway examples, a fairy woman asks for healing water at the Well of the Co’, and another fairy woman on the Glasserton estate asks a farmer’s wife for milk for an unwell child. These stories are not merely decorative. They make ordinary acts, fetching water or giving milk, into tests of neighbourliness between visible and hidden communities.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/files/43966/43966-h/43966-h.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on gutenberg.org.</span></span></span>

The Glasserton story is especially useful because it shows the gentler side of Galloway fairy belief. The fairy asks for milk; the farmer’s wife gives it; the reward is modest prosperity, including the hint that milk pails and crops will do well. It is a small exchange, but that is what makes it credible as local tradition. The supernatural does not arrive as thunder. It arrives as a tiny request beside a stream.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/files/43966/43966-h/43966-h.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on gutenberg.org.</span></span></span>

## Fairy danger was real in the stories

The modern word “fairy” can mislead readers into expecting sweetness. Galloway fairy lore is often more anxious. Fairies can reward kindness, but they can also curse refusal, steal children, frighten travellers or be linked with illness and death. In Wood’s Portankill and Dunnan story, the man who refuses to fetch healing water for a fairy child is warned that he has been sitting on the fairies’ house-roof; after that, crops fail, cattle die and he eventually dies after returning to the place at night.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/files/43966/43966-h/43966-h.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on gutenberg.org.</span></span></span>

The child-stealing motif appears in Wood’s Kirkmaiden material, where fairies try to seize a newborn child from a herd’s cottage while the father is travelling to Cardoness Castle in the Stewartry of Kirkcudbright. The scene gathers several classic danger signs: Hallowmas, a lonely road, uncanny lights, a fairy procession and a mother alone with a baby.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/files/43966/43966-h/43966-h.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on gutenberg.org.</span></span></span>

These stories are outside the strict core of Kirkcudbrightshire in some of their settings, but they matter because Cardoness and the Stewartry are woven into the narrative route. They also show how Galloway fairy lore moves along roads and obligations. A man’s duty to his laird takes him away from home; the vulnerable household becomes exposed; supernatural danger enters at the threshold.

The useful distinction is this: brownies usually test household manners around labour, while fairies more often test boundaries around land, babies, water, kindness and taboo. The two traditions overlap, especially at mounds, but they do different cultural work.

## From old mounds to woodland faerie trails

Galloway fairy places have not vanished; they have changed form. Older fairy belief was tied to fear, courtesy, land-use and household risk. Modern fairy tourism tends to soften the material into family trails, woodland play and imaginative discovery. That does not make it false folklore, but it does change its function.

The Galloway Faerie Trail at Barstobrick, near Ringford and Castle Douglas, is a good modern example within Kirkcudbrightshire’s orbit. Its own visitor information describes a one-kilometre buggy- and wheelchair-friendly woodland trail where families search for faerie secrets and missing faerie dust. Tourism pages now present it alongside history trails, wildlife trails, horse riding and other countryside activities.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.barstobrick.co.uk/galloway-faerie-trail" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: barstobrick.co.uk">[Barstobrick Holiday Lodges]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">barstobrick.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on barstobrick.co.uk.</span></span></span>

This is a very different fairy world from Myrton Mound or the Brownie of Blednoch. Nobody is being warned not to cut a drain through a hidden palace, and no one is likely to lose a farm helper by giving him trousers. Yet the trail still works because it draws on the same regional instinct: woodland paths and small hidden dwellings make the landscape feel inhabited.

The shift from feared neighbour to family-friendly faerie is part of modern folklore, not merely a loss of it. In older tales, fairies explained misfortune, enforced courtesy and gave ordinary places a second ownership. In modern retellings, they invite children and visitors to look more closely at woods, tracks and small details. The emotional register has changed from caution to wonder, but the mechanism is recognisably Galloway: the land becomes readable as a place with hidden residents.

## How old and how well-attested are these traditions?

The evidence is uneven but valuable. The strongest older printed witness for this page is Wood’s 1911 book, which gathers earlier oral, literary and antiquarian material. Wood himself drew on sources such as *The Hereditary Sheriffs of Galloway*, Allan Cunningham, James F. Cannon and older local recollection. That means some tales were already layered by the time he printed them: part oral tradition, part antiquarian shaping, part literary performance.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/files/43966/43966-h/43966-h.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on gutenberg.org.</span></span></span>

William Nicholson’s “The Brownie of Blednoch” is a literary poem, not a verbatim transcript of a fireside tale. But that does not make it irrelevant to folklore. In Galloway, poetry, local speech and oral tradition fed one another. Nicholson’s poem fixed Aiken-drum in a form people could quote, remember and perform, while still drawing on the wider brownie rules familiar across Scotland. The Scottish Poetry Library’s biographical note reinforces Nicholson’s Galloway identity and the poem’s central place in his reputation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.scottishpoetrylibrary.org.uk/poet/william-nicholson/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: scottishpoetrylibrary.org.uk">[Scottish Poetry Library]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">scottishpoetrylibrary.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Scottish Poetry Library William Nicholson</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Scottish Poetry Library William Nicholson</span></span></span>

John Mactaggart’s *Scottish Gallovidian Encyclopedia*, first published in 1824, is another important but tricky source. It is eccentric, comic, sharp-tongued and not always reliable in a modern factual sense, yet it is deeply valuable for Galloway words, characters, sayings and local curiosities. Later commentary and catalogue records recognise it as a major source for Scots lexicography and for the cultural texture of nineteenth-century Galloway.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/stream/scottishgallovi00mactgoog/scottishgallovi00mactgoog_djvu.txt" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on archive.org.</span></span></span>

The fairest reading is that Galloway fairy places are well-attested as traditions, not as events. Myrton Mound, Aiken-drum, Glasserton’s milk-giving fairy and the Dunnan fairy warning all preserve real local story-patterns. Their exact historical truth is not the point. Their value lies in showing what places people marked as uncanny, what forms of behaviour were praised or punished, and how Galloway turned landscape into memory.

## Why these stories still feel local

Galloway fairy places endure because they are specific. A vague fairyland can be moved anywhere; Myrton Mound cannot. A generic helpful elf can belong to any fantasy; Aiken-drum belongs to Blednoch, the Cree, Threave, Glashnoch Mill and the voice of a Borgue-born poet. The stories remember mounds, mills, lochs, burns, farms and byres with the precision of people who worked and feared those places.

For Kirkcudbrightshire, the fairy and brownie traditions also reveal a distinctive county identity. The Stewartry’s folklore is not only coastal romance or ruined-castle gloom. It is a working landscape of bargains: give milk, respect the mound, do not mock the hidden worker, do not treat every gift as a wage, and be careful at thresholds after dark.

That is why modern woodland trails can sit beside old supernatural warnings without feeling entirely disconnected. Both ask the same readerly habit from the visitor: look again at the landscape. The green mound may be more than an earthwork; the mill may be more than a ruin; the farm may have once had a night worker; the woodland path may still invite small acts of attention. In Galloway, fairy places are not escapes from local history. They are one of the ways local history learned to speak.

    var available = availableMarkets(section);
    var marketId = defaultMarket;
    if (window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation && window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation.inferMarket) {
      marketId = window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation.inferMarket(available, {
        storageKey: 'phoenix-ebay-market',
        defaultMarket: defaultMarket
      });
    } else if (available.indexOf(defaultMarket)< 0) {
      marketId = available[0] || defaultMarket;
    }
    var select = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-select]');
    if (select) {
      select.addEventListener('change', function () {
        applyMarket(section, select.value, true);
      });
    }
    var trigger = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-trigger]');
    var menu = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-menu]');
    if (trigger && menu) {
      trigger.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
        event.stopPropagation();
        var expanded = trigger.getAttribute('aria-expanded') === 'true';
        menu.hidden = expanded;
        trigger.setAttribute('aria-expanded', expanded ? 'false' : 'true');
        var picker = trigger.closest('.fr-ebay-market-picker');
        if (picker) picker.classList.toggle('fr-ebay-market-picker--open', !expanded);
      });
      Array.prototype.slice.call(menu.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-option]')).forEach(function (button) {
        button.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
          event.stopPropagation();
          applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-option'), true);
        });
      });
      document.addEventListener('click', function () { closeMenu(section); });
    }
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      button.addEventListener('click', function () {
        applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button'), true);
      });
    });
    applyMarket(section, marketId, false);
  });
})();
