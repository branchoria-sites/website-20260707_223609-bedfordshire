## Introduction

Dumbarton’s witch-trial history is not a cosy Halloween tale. It is part of [Dunbartonshire]({{ 'where-dunbartonshires-legends-gather/' | relative_url }})’s folklore landscape because later generations told stories about “witches”, devil marks, prisons and burning places, but the surviving evidence points first to persecution: real local people accused under a legal system that treated witchcraft as a capital crime. Janet or Jonet Boyd is the best-known Dumbarton case, but newer local research and council discussion have widened remembrance to a larger group of women and men accused in and around West Dunbartonshire during the 17th century. The question today is not whether these people were “really witches”, but how a town should remember neighbours who were imprisoned, examined, named in records and, in many cases, executed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://witches.hca.ed.ac.uk/trial/T/LA/508" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: witches.hca.ed.ac.uk">[ed.ac.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">witches.hca.ed.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">the trial of Janet BoydConfessed to the provost and bailies of Dumbarton. ## Why Dumbarton’s witch trials belong in local folklore

Witch-trial history sits awkwardly beside folklore. The language of the records is full of supernatural belief: pacts with the Devil, renouncing baptism, witch marks, harmful magic and uncanny illness. Those ideas clearly belong to the belief-world of early modern Scotland. Yet the people accused were not legendary beings. They were townspeople, spouses, widows, neighbours and workers caught in a machinery of fear, law and local suspicion.

For Dunbartonshire, the geography matters. Dumbarton was not an isolated village tale but a burgh with civic officers, a tolbooth, local accounts and connections to national justice through the Privy Council and commissions. The University of Edinburgh’s Survey of Scottish Witchcraft records Janet Boyd of Dumbarton in the wider national database of people accused between 1563 and 1736, while West Dunbartonshire Council’s 2025 discussion treated the subject as a local remembrance issue tied to places in the modern town centre.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://datashare.ed.ac.uk/handle/10283/45" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: datashare.ed.ac.uk">[ed.ac.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">datashare.ed.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on ed.ac.uk.</span></span></span>

That is why this page belongs within a folklore project, but with care. The “witch” is a familiar figure in popular storytelling, yet Dumbarton’s record asks readers to look behind the figure: who was accused, who watched them in prison, who paid for fuel and ropes, who wrote the accounts, and how a community later chooses to name or forget them.

## Janet Boyd and the local trial record

Janet Boyd, also recorded in modern retellings as Jonet Boyd, is the clearest named anchor for Dumbarton’s witch-trial memory. The Survey of Scottish Witchcraft gives her trial record as Dumbarton, with a confession to the provost and bailies; it also notes that a commission previously granted by the Earl of Monteith had to be ratified because of doubts about authority, and that her trial and execution were later deemed legal on 13 January 1629.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://witches.hca.ed.ac.uk/trial/T/LA/508" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: witches.hca.ed.ac.uk">[witches.hca.ed.ac.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">witches.hca.ed.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">the trial of Janet BoydConfessed to the provost and bailies of Dumbarton. In the second commission... no recorded named witches. Mention...</span></span></span>

The wording of Boyd’s confession, as preserved through later summaries of Privy Council material, follows the grim pattern of Scottish witch-trial accusation: she was said to have entered into covenant with the Devil, received his mark, renounced her baptism, had sexual dealings with him, and caused diseases by his power. Those details should not be read as neutral biography. They are the standard demonological script that investigators often wanted to hear, especially where confession was treated as central evidence.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.dailyrecord.co.uk/scotland-now/look-back-scotlands-sinister-witch-35762982" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: dailyrecord.co.uk">[Daily Record]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">dailyrecord.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">look back scotlands sinister witch 35762982</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">look back scotlands sinister witch 35762982</span></span></span>

Boyd’s case also shows how local and national authority overlapped. The story is not simply “a mob believed in witches”. It involved burgh officers, legal commissions, the Privy Council and retrospective approval of proceedings. That matters because remembrance has to include the institutions as well as the superstition. Fear may have travelled through neighbourly quarrels and rumour, but punishment required official power.

The spellings themselves are a warning about the records. Janet, Jonet, Boyd, Neil, Neill, Weill and other names may appear differently across databases, council minutes, older transcripts and local research. This is normal for 17th-century Scottish material, but it makes careful remembrance harder: one person can become hard to trace, while another may be duplicated or lost under variant forms.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://witches.hca.ed.ac.uk/quick/?casefrom=&amp;caseto=&amp;firstname=&amp;lastname=&amp;maritalstatus=&amp;page=206&amp;residence=&amp;sex=Female&amp;socioeconomicstatus=&amp;sort=-res_county" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: witches.hca.ed.ac.uk">[witches.hca.ed.ac.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">witches.hca.ed.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on ed.ac.uk.</span></span></span>

## What the Dumbarton records show beyond one case

Modern local work has pushed the Dumbarton story beyond Janet Boyd alone. In 2025, Louise Wilson of Remembering the Accused Witches of Scotland described research in Dumbarton Library using the Dumbarton Common Good Accounts 1614–1660 and Dumbarton Burgh Records 1627–1746. She reported finding payments connected with watching accused people in the tolbooth and buying peat, coal, timber and tar for burnings.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.raws.scot/post/the-forgotten-names-of-dumbarton" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: raws.scot">[Scottish Witches]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">raws.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Scottish Witches The forgotten names of Dumbarton</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Scottish Witches The forgotten names of Dumbarton</span></span></span>

That detail is stark because it makes the violence administrative. The accounts do not merely tell a dramatic story; they record costs. Local remembrance often begins with exactly this kind of evidence: small payments that reveal imprisonment, surveillance, transport, fuel, binding materials and execution as part of ordinary town business.

Wilson’s research also claimed six previously undocumented names, including a 1624 group: Marion MacLintock, Marion Fallisdaill, Jonet MacKinley and John Taylor. Her account says the two Marions are specifically recorded as burnt after conviction, while Jonet MacKinley appears again in relation to ropes for binding the witches; John Taylor’s outcome is not recorded.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.raws.scot/post/the-forgotten-names-of-dumbarton" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: raws.scot">[Scottish Witches]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">raws.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Scottish Witches The forgotten names of Dumbarton</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Scottish Witches The forgotten names of Dumbarton</span></span></span>

A later example from 1643, Bessie Bargillie, is especially important for the tone of remembrance. Wilson reports that the accounts record belongings sold to meet the charges of her trial and execution, and refer to her being strangled and then burnt. No fuller accusation narrative survives in that account, so the human being is visible mainly through the cost of killing her and the sale of what she left behind.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.raws.scot/post/the-forgotten-names-of-dumbarton" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: raws.scot">[Scottish Witches]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">raws.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Scottish Witches The forgotten names of Dumbarton</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Scottish Witches The forgotten names of Dumbarton</span></span></span>

West Dunbartonshire Council’s 2025 minutes give a broader memorial list of 31 accused witches of West Dunbartonshire, 27 women and 4 men, ranging from 1624 to 1697. The list includes Jonet Boyd, Janet Donald, Jonet Neill, Bessie Bargillie, John McWilliam and others, while marking some outcomes as unknown. The council agreed a motion recognising and commemorating those accused and persecuted in the 17th century.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://wdccmis.west-dunbarton.gov.uk/cmis5/Document.ashx?FgPlIEJYlotS%2BYGoBi5olA%3D%3D=NHdURQburHA%3D&amp;WGewmoAfeNQ16B2MHuCpMRKZMwaG1PaO=ctNJFf55vVA%3D&amp;WGewmoAfeNR9xqBux0r1Q8Za60lavYmz=ctNJFf55vVA%3D&amp;czJKcaeAi5tUFL1DTL2UE4zNRBcoShgo=uxCdKrpQN3ow6SnUVoFOGRMpAEfvC1nZ6gT6eZSv%2FK2w3dr%2BRuhoQQ%3D%3D&amp;d9Qjj0ag1Pd993jsyOJqFvmyB7X0CSQK=ctNJFf55vVA%3D&amp;kCx1AnS9%2FpWZQ40DXFvdEw%3D%3D=hFflUdN3100%3D&amp;mCTIbCubSFfXsDGW9IXnlg%3D%3D=hFflUdN3100%3D&amp;rUzwRPf%2BZ3zd4E7Ikn8Lyw%3D%3D=pwRE6AGJFLDNlh225F5QMaQWCtPHwdhUfCZ%2FLUQzgA2uL5jNRG4jdQ%3D%3D&amp;uJovDxwdjMPoYv%2BAJvYtyA%3D%3D=ctNJFf55vVA%3D" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wdccmis.west-dunbarton.gov.uk">[wdccmis.west-dunbarton.gov.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wdccmis.west-dunbarton.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Item 06</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Item 06</span></span></span>

## Witchcraft law, fear and punishment

The legal background was severe. The Scottish Witchcraft Act of 1563 made witchcraft a capital offence and remained in force until repeal in the 18th century. Historic Environment Scotland summarises its impact as a century and a half of witch hunts in which thousands died, with most accused people being ordinary people rather than powerful figures.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://blog.historicenvironment.scot/2022/06/the-witchcraft-act-and-its-impact-in-scotland/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: blog.historicenvironment.scot">[Historic Environment Scotland Blog]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">blog.historicenvironment.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on historicenvironment.scot.</span></span></span>

The most disturbing point for a modern reader is that confessions cannot be treated as free admissions of guilt. Historic Environment Scotland notes that investigators usually sought confessions proving interaction with the Devil and that torture, often sleep deprivation and sometimes physical torture, was routinely used to obtain them. This helps explain why confessions in cases like Janet Boyd’s contain the expected themes of Devil pact, mark and renounced baptism.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://blog.historicenvironment.scot/2022/06/the-witchcraft-act-and-its-impact-in-scotland/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: blog.historicenvironment.scot">[Historic Environment Scotland Blog]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">blog.historicenvironment.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on historicenvironment.scot.</span></span></span>

Punishment is also often misremembered. The popular image is burning alive, but Scottish practice was commonly strangling first and then burning the body. Historic Environment Scotland states that about two-thirds of accused witches were executed according to the Survey’s estimate, and that most were strangled before their bodies were burned; West Dunbartonshire Council’s 2025 motion uses the same basic pattern when describing local accused people tied to a stake, strangled and burned, with no grave marker.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://blog.historicenvironment.scot/2022/06/the-witchcraft-act-and-its-impact-in-scotland/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: blog.historicenvironment.scot">[Historic Environment Scotland Blog]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">blog.historicenvironment.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on historicenvironment.scot.</span></span></span>

The local setting makes this less abstract. Council minutes place the 17th-century Dumbarton tolbooth near the entrance to the Artizan Centre, directly opposite Quay Street, and say the execution site is thought to have been behind Church Street in the present council office car park, in an area once occupied by Dumbarton Prison. These claims are presented as part of a memorial motion rather than as archaeological certainty, but they show how remembrance attaches persecution to everyday town-centre space.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://wdccmis.west-dunbarton.gov.uk/cmis5/Document.ashx?FgPlIEJYlotS%2BYGoBi5olA%3D%3D=NHdURQburHA%3D&amp;WGewmoAfeNQ16B2MHuCpMRKZMwaG1PaO=ctNJFf55vVA%3D&amp;WGewmoAfeNR9xqBux0r1Q8Za60lavYmz=ctNJFf55vVA%3D&amp;czJKcaeAi5tUFL1DTL2UE4zNRBcoShgo=uxCdKrpQN3ow6SnUVoFOGRMpAEfvC1nZ6gT6eZSv%2FK2w3dr%2BRuhoQQ%3D%3D&amp;d9Qjj0ag1Pd993jsyOJqFvmyB7X0CSQK=ctNJFf55vVA%3D&amp;kCx1AnS9%2FpWZQ40DXFvdEw%3D%3D=hFflUdN3100%3D&amp;mCTIbCubSFfXsDGW9IXnlg%3D%3D=hFflUdN3100%3D&amp;rUzwRPf%2BZ3zd4E7Ikn8Lyw%3D%3D=pwRE6AGJFLDNlh225F5QMaQWCtPHwdhUfCZ%2FLUQzgA2uL5jNRG4jdQ%3D%3D&amp;uJovDxwdjMPoYv%2BAJvYtyA%3D%3D=ctNJFf55vVA%3D" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wdccmis.west-dunbarton.gov.uk">[wdccmis.west-dunbarton.gov.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wdccmis.west-dunbarton.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Item 06</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Item 06</span></span></span>

## From spooky tale to public remembrance

Modern remembrance is changing the meaning of Dumbarton’s witch stories. A conventional spooky retelling might focus on “witches in the tolbooth” or the horror of burnings. A more responsible local-history approach asks who was harmed, how the accusations worked, and why the victims were so easily turned into monsters in the first place.

This shift is visible in West Dunbartonshire’s own cultural programming. The council’s 2023–24 Crime & Punishment exhibition at Dumbarton Library’s Heritage Centre included the 17th-century witchcraft trials alongside Dumbarton Castle prisoners of war and Dumbarton Prison, placing the subject within the history of local justice and penalties rather than treating it as a supernatural curiosity.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.west-dunbarton.gov.uk/leisure-parks-events/museums-and-galleries/exhibitions/crime-punishment-exhibition" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: west-dunbarton.gov.uk">[West Dunbartonshire Council]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">west-dunbarton.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Crime &amp; Punishment Exhibition &#124; West Dunbartonshire Council...</span></span></span>

It is also visible in campaigning and memorial work. Remembering the Accused Witches of Scotland frames its work around promoting and honouring the memory of accused individuals, while the 2025 council motion explicitly linked remembrance with schools, libraries and the possibility of a memorial. The same motion noted the 2022 apologies issued by the Scottish Government and the Church of Scotland, reflecting a national move from folklore spectacle towards acknowledgement of injustice.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://wdccmis.west-dunbarton.gov.uk/cmis5/Document.ashx?FgPlIEJYlotS%2BYGoBi5olA%3D%3D=NHdURQburHA%3D&amp;WGewmoAfeNQ16B2MHuCpMRKZMwaG1PaO=ctNJFf55vVA%3D&amp;WGewmoAfeNR9xqBux0r1Q8Za60lavYmz=ctNJFf55vVA%3D&amp;czJKcaeAi5tUFL1DTL2UE4zNRBcoShgo=uxCdKrpQN3ow6SnUVoFOGRMpAEfvC1nZ6gT6eZSv%2FK2w3dr%2BRuhoQQ%3D%3D&amp;d9Qjj0ag1Pd993jsyOJqFvmyB7X0CSQK=ctNJFf55vVA%3D&amp;kCx1AnS9%2FpWZQ40DXFvdEw%3D%3D=hFflUdN3100%3D&amp;mCTIbCubSFfXsDGW9IXnlg%3D%3D=hFflUdN3100%3D&amp;rUzwRPf%2BZ3zd4E7Ikn8Lyw%3D%3D=pwRE6AGJFLDNlh225F5QMaQWCtPHwdhUfCZ%2FLUQzgA2uL5jNRG4jdQ%3D%3D&amp;uJovDxwdjMPoYv%2BAJvYtyA%3D%3D=ctNJFf55vVA%3D" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wdccmis.west-dunbarton.gov.uk">[wdccmis.west-dunbarton.gov.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wdccmis.west-dunbarton.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Item 06</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Item 06</span></span></span>

This does not mean stripping the subject of folklore value. It means reading folklore with moral attention. The beliefs that powered accusations — witch marks, harmful magic, Devil pacts, uncanny illness and fear of the spiritually suspect neighbour — are part of Scotland’s supernatural tradition. But in Dumbarton, those beliefs left traces in prison records, account books and executions. The folklore cannot be separated from the harm done in its name.

## How should Dumbarton remember them?

A good local remembrance of Dumbarton’s witch trials should do three things at once: name the accused where records allow, explain the belief system without endorsing it, and mark the places where civic authority turned fear into punishment. That is more useful than either sensationalising the accused as “real witches” or flattening the whole subject into a vague statement that people were superstitious in the past.

The strongest approach is careful and human-sized. Janet Boyd can stand as the best-documented Dumbarton case, but she should not become the only remembered person. The wider list of accused people, including those whose outcomes are unknown, shows that local memory is still being reconstructed from scattered records. Where the evidence is uncertain, the uncertainty should be visible rather than smoothed away.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://witches.hca.ed.ac.uk/trial/T/LA/508" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: witches.hca.ed.ac.uk">[ed.ac.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">witches.hca.ed.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">the trial of Janet BoydConfessed to the provost and bailies of Dumbarton. In the second commission... no recorded named witches. Mention...</span></span></span>

Dumbarton also has a chance to connect remembrance to place. A tolbooth near the Artizan Centre, an execution site thought to lie behind Church Street, the library and archives where records survive, and the council buildings of the modern town all create a tight geography of memory. For visitors and residents, this is more powerful than a generic “witches of Scotland” story: it says that persecution happened in recognisable streets, through recognisable institutions, to people whose names can still be spoken.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://wdccmis.west-dunbarton.gov.uk/cmis5/Document.ashx?FgPlIEJYlotS%2BYGoBi5olA%3D%3D=NHdURQburHA%3D&amp;WGewmoAfeNQ16B2MHuCpMRKZMwaG1PaO=ctNJFf55vVA%3D&amp;WGewmoAfeNR9xqBux0r1Q8Za60lavYmz=ctNJFf55vVA%3D&amp;czJKcaeAi5tUFL1DTL2UE4zNRBcoShgo=uxCdKrpQN3ow6SnUVoFOGRMpAEfvC1nZ6gT6eZSv%2FK2w3dr%2BRuhoQQ%3D%3D&amp;d9Qjj0ag1Pd993jsyOJqFvmyB7X0CSQK=ctNJFf55vVA%3D&amp;kCx1AnS9%2FpWZQ40DXFvdEw%3D%3D=hFflUdN3100%3D&amp;mCTIbCubSFfXsDGW9IXnlg%3D%3D=hFflUdN3100%3D&amp;rUzwRPf%2BZ3zd4E7Ikn8Lyw%3D%3D=pwRE6AGJFLDNlh225F5QMaQWCtPHwdhUfCZ%2FLUQzgA2uL5jNRG4jdQ%3D%3D&amp;uJovDxwdjMPoYv%2BAJvYtyA%3D%3D=ctNJFf55vVA%3D" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wdccmis.west-dunbarton.gov.uk">[wdccmis.west-dunbarton.gov.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wdccmis.west-dunbarton.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Item 06</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Item 06</span></span></span>

The most honest conclusion is therefore a cautious one. Dumbarton’s witch trials matter within Dunbartonshire folklore not because they prove supernatural happenings, but because they show how supernatural belief, law, gender, fear and neighbourly conflict could combine into lethal public action. Modern remembrance should keep the mystery of the old belief-world in view, while refusing to turn its victims back into monsters.
