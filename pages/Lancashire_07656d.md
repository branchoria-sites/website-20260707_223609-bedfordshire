## Introduction

For this project, “Lancashire” means the historic county. That matters because historic Lancashire included places now commonly thought of as Greater Manchester, Merseyside and parts of Cumbria, including Furness. ## What counts as Lancashire folklore?

Lancashire folklore is a mixture of recorded oral tradition, court history, antiquarian collecting, seasonal performance and later literary retelling. Some stories, such as the [Pendle witches]({{ 'pendle-witches/' | relative_url }}), are anchored in legal documents and named historical people. Others, such as boggarts or fairy caves, survive through dialect writing, local memory, place-names and Victorian collections. A third group has been reshaped by tourism, museums, novels, sculpture trails and local festivals.

This makes Lancashire especially rich, but it also means readers need to ask what sort of evidence they are looking at. A witch trial record is not the same as a folk tale. A Victorian antiquarian anecdote may preserve genuine local belief, but it may also tidy it into a literary form. A modern heritage trail may keep a story alive while changing its emphasis for visitors. The strongest Lancashire folklore pages should therefore separate five useful categories:

**Old belief and oral tradition:** boggarts, charms, death omens, fairy warnings, household spirits and water bogies.

**Historical events turned into legend:** above all, the 1612 Pendle witch trials and later Lancashire witch scares.

**Customs and performances:** pace-egging, rushbearing, Riding the Black Lad and local wakes.

**Legendary landscape:** holy wells, caves, moorland stones, cloughs, meres and ancient monuments.

**Modern retellings:** novels, museums, walking trails, sculptures, ghost tours and local media.

The county’s folklore has never been sealed inside modern council lines. Boggart stories spread across the North West and Pennines; pace-egg plays belong to Lancashire and the West Riding; the old county included Manchester and Liverpool as well as rural Pendle and Furness. Historic-county framing is therefore useful, but folklore often follows dialect, parish, trade, migration and landscape rather than tidy administrative borders.

## Pendle is Lancashire’s most famous witch landscape

The Pendle witch trials of 1612 are the most internationally recognised Lancashire legend, but they were not just a legend. They were real trials, held mainly at Lancaster Castle in August 1612, with ten people executed after being accused of witchcraft. Lancaster Castle’s account identifies Judge Bromley, Judge Altham, prosecutor Roger Nowell of Read Hall and court clerk Thomas Potts as central figures in the proceedings.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.lancastercastle.com/history-heritage/a-dark-history/lancashire-witch-trials/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: lancastercastle.com">[Lancaster Castle]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">lancastercastle.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on lancastercastle.com.</span></span></span>

The case is unusually well known because Potts published *The Wonderfull Discoverie of Witches in the Countie of Lancaster* in 1613. That printed account helped fix the names, speeches and accusations in public memory, but it should not be read as a neutral transcript. Later historical scholarship treats Potts’s book as an important but shaped legal and political document, written under official supervision and reflecting the fears of early Stuart England.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.jstor.org/stable/j.ctt155jb7v" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: jstor.org">[JSTOR]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">jstor.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on jstor.org.</span></span></span>

Pendle’s power as folklore comes from the collision of ordinary local life with terrifying public accusation. The accused included members of poor families around Pendle Hill, notably the Device and Whittle families, and the case involved quarrels, begging, healing, charms, suspected familiars, illness and testimony by the child witness Jennet Device. Modern summaries often remember it as a simple story of “witches”, but the deeper Lancashire story is about poverty, reputation, religion, neighbourly hostility and a legal system willing to turn fear into executions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pendle_witches" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wikipedia.org.</span></span></span>

Pendle also shows how folklore changes. In the nineteenth century, William Harrison Ainsworth’s novel *The Lancashire Witches* helped transform the trial into Gothic historical romance, making the Pendle witches part of a wider literary imagination. In the twenty-first century, Pendle is also a tourism and memory landscape, with walking routes, interpretation, sculpture, Halloween events and local identity built around the story. A recent academic discussion of Pendle’s rural landscape notes that versions of the witch history circulate locally as folk legend and are strongly tied to place identity.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.revenantjournal.com/contents/on-the-hill-representations-and-retellings-of-the-pendle-witches-history-within-the-rural-landscape-of-lancashire-england/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: revenantjournal.com">[Revenant Journal]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">revenantjournal.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on revenantjournal.com.</span></span></span>

The most important thing for readers is not to treat the executed people as proven witches. They were accused, tried and killed under the beliefs and laws of their time. The folklore is real as tradition; the supernatural claims remain claims within that tradition.

## Boggarts: Lancashire’s unruly household and landscape spirits

If Pendle gives Lancashire its most famous witch story, the boggart gives the county one of its most characteristic supernatural beings. A boggart is not one fixed creature. In northern English folklore it can be a house spirit, a frightening outdoor presence, a shape-changer, a poltergeist-like nuisance or a local bogey attached to a clough, field, hole or farm. Modern folklore historian Simon Young’s work treats “boggart” as a broad northern category of solitary supernatural being, with especially strong links to Lancashire and the North West.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boggart" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wikipedia.org.</span></span></span>

Harland and Wilkinson’s *Lancashire Folk-lore* gives boggarts a prominent place and distinguishes house boggarts from other kinds. Their Lancashire boggarts are rarely cosy. They frighten sleepers, disturb households, haunt places and resist easy control. One repeated lesson in boggart lore is that naming or mocking the being can make matters worse: these are not fairies to be domesticated but presences to be avoided, appeased or fled.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/files/41148/41148-h/41148-h.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">41148 h</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Source details in endnotes.</span></span></span>

Boggart Hole Clough, now in Manchester, is one of the best-known boggart landscapes in the historic county. Modern Manchester lies outside today’s administrative Lancashire, but it was historically part of Lancashire, making the site important for any historic-county folklore map. Local and scholarly retellings describe a “boggart hole” as the supposed lair of a boggart, often a downward place in the landscape such as a ravine, cave, hollow or clough.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.manchestereveningnews.co.uk/news/greater-manchester-news/what-exactly-boggart-creepy-history-23434917" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: manchestereveningnews.co.uk">[Manchester Evening News]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">manchestereveningnews.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">what exactly boggart creepy history 23434917</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">what exactly boggart creepy history 23434917</span></span></span>

Lancashire boggart lore also overlaps with other noisy or frightening beings. “Clapcans”, reported in the Manchester and Bury area, appears in dialect and folklore writing as a clanking or prankish bogie. Young’s discussion warns that such minor beings can be distorted by repetition in folklore dictionaries, which is a useful reminder: some names are well-attested locally, while others survive only through thin or second-hand references.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.academia.edu/74758187/Young_Clapcans_A_Greater_Manchester_Bogie" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: academia.edu">[Academia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">academia.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Young Clapcans A Greater Manchester Bogie</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Young Clapcans A Greater Manchester Bogie</span></span></span>

For readers, the boggart matters because it links Lancashire folklore to everyday fear. These stories are not set in distant fairy palaces. They belong to bedsides, barns, lanes, milkmaids, cloughs and working landscapes. They are part ghost, part goblin, part warning, part explanation for strange noises and bad luck.

## Fairy places and the problem of modern names

Lancashire has fairy traditions, but they often appear in scattered place-names, local anecdotes and antiquarian fragments rather than in long, polished fairy tales. Harland and Wilkinson’s collection records a county full of charms, omens and supernatural beings, while later folklore writing points to fairy-related names such as the Fairy Caves between Blackburn and Accrington.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/files/41148/41148-h/41148-h.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">41148 h</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Source details in endnotes.</span></span></span>

The “Fairy Caves” near Oswaldtwistle are a good example of how folklore and industrial archaeology can sit on top of each other. The structures are locally known as Fairy Caves, but modern local heritage material identifies them as the remains of nineteenth-century beehive-shaped coke ovens beside the Leeds and Liverpool Canal. Lancashire Live reported that the 24 structures are locally called the “fairy caves”, while the Prospects Foundation’s leaflet explains that children in Oswaldtwistle’s West End found them exciting places to play even though they are industrial remains.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.lancs.live/whats-on/food-drink-news/iconic-fairy-caves-permanent-reminder-28319888" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: lancs.live">[Lancashire Live]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">lancs.live</span><span class="citation-popover-title">iconic fairy caves permanent reminder 28319888</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">iconic fairy caves permanent reminder 28319888</span></span></span>

This does not make the fairy name worthless. It shows how folklore often attaches itself to ruins, holes and half-understood structures. A child’s name for a strange place can become a local tradition; an industrial feature can acquire a mythic atmosphere once its original use fades. In Lancashire, where canals, kilns, mills and quarries changed the landscape dramatically, folklore often survives by re-enchanting the remains of industry.

Other fairy-like stories attach to caves and limestone landscapes around the Ribble Valley and Bowland edges. Accounts of the Fairy Hole Caves at Whitewell include a familiar British fairy motif: a human woman taken to assist fairy childbirth and given ointment that lets her see the fairy world. Such tales are widespread, but their local setting gives them Lancashire texture: the story belongs not to an abstract fairyland but to caves, market towns and the upland edge of the county.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://northwestnatureandhistory.co.uk/2023/10/29/unnatural-histories-the-fairy-hole-caves-at-whitewell/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: northwestnatureandhistory.co.uk">[Northwest Nature and History]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">northwestnatureandhistory.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">unnatural histories the fairy hole caves at whitewell</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">unnatural histories the fairy hole caves at whitewell</span></span></span>

The useful distinction is this: some Lancashire fairy lore is old oral tradition, some is place-name memory, and some is modern enchantment of older ruins. All three matter, but they should not be treated as the same kind of evidence.

## Water spirits, meres and holy wells

Lancashire’s folklore is strongly shaped by water: mosses, meres, wells, springs, rivers, canal corridors and coastal sands. Water traditions often mix danger, healing and holiness.

Martin Mere, near Burscough, is one of the county’s strongest watery folklore landscapes. Once a large lowland mere, later drained and transformed, it attracted stories of mermaids and strange water beings. Modern retellings of the Martin Mere mermaid describe a child-stealing water woman in the reign of Charles II, associated with John Roby’s nineteenth-century narrative *The Mermaid of Martin Meer*.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.bigissuenorth.com/features/2019/04/mermaids-and-where-to-find-them/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: bigissuenorth.com">[Big Issue North]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">bigissuenorth.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">mermaids and where to find them</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">mermaids and where to find them</span></span></span>

These inland “mermaids” are not simply sea-maids misplaced. In British folklore, mere-maids and freshwater spirits belong to pools, lakes and dangerous waters. They warn children away from drowning places, explain uncanny waters and give local meres a personality. Lancashire’s version sits naturally beside boggart and Jenny Greenteeth traditions: water is useful and sacred, but also treacherous.

Holy wells show the gentler and more devotional side of water lore. St Anne’s Well, near Sutton and Rainhill in the historic Lancashire area, is protected by Historic England as a medieval holy well: a shallow square sandstone basin with steps, formerly associated with a conduit, stone basin and burial ground.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://historicengland.org.uk/listing/the-list/list-entry/1019449" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: historicengland.org.uk">[Historic England]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">historicengland.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">list entry</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">list entry</span></span></span> A Merseyside Archaeological Society paper notes that its isolation and reputation for healing waters gave rise to legends connecting it with a medieval monastic house and burial ground.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.merseysidearchsoc.com/uploads/2/7/2/9/2729758/jmas_3_paper_4.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: merseysidearchsoc.com">[Merseyside Archaeological Society]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">merseysidearchsoc.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">jmas 3 paper 4</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">jmas 3 paper 4</span></span></span>

St Helen’s Well at Whittle-le-Woods shows how holy wells can become both devotional and folkloric. Local history accounts describe it as a site visited for hundreds of years, where people collected spring water and deposited metal pins as offerings. The physical well was destroyed in 1968, but the place is now marked by a cross and plaque, turning a lost water site into a memory site.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://lancashirepast.com/2014/10/24/st-helens-well-and-cross-whittle-le-woods/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: lancashirepast.com">[Lancashire Past]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">lancashirepast.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">st helens well and cross whittle le woods</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">st helens well and cross whittle le woods</span></span></span>

Lancashire’s well lore is not just about miracle stories. It records how people used water places for healing, offerings, seasonal visits and local identity. A well could be Christian, pre-Christian in reputation, practical, medicinal, social and magical all at once.

## Stones, hills and ancient places with later legends

Lancashire is less famous for stone circles than Cumbria, but it has several ancient and legendary sites that matter for folklore. Lancaster City Museums notes that the only stone circle of note near Lancaster is Summerhouse Hill near Leighton Hall, while evidence also exists for a ceremonial timber circle at Bleasdale in the Forest of Bowland.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.lancaster.gov.uk/sites/museums/explore-online/local-history/stone-circles" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: lancaster.gov.uk">[Lancaster City Council]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">lancaster.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on lancaster.gov.uk.</span></span></span>

Pike Stones on Anglezarke Moor is especially important because it is not merely a picturesque ruin. Historic England identifies Pike Stones as the only known chambered long cairn in Lancashire and an unusual outlier compared with the better-known regional groupings of such monuments elsewhere in Britain.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://historicengland.org.uk/listing/the-list/list-entry/1009120" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: historicengland.org.uk">[Historic England]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">historicengland.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">list entry</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">list entry</span></span></span> Ancient monuments often attract legends after their original purpose is forgotten; even when a specific folk tale is thin, the site’s strangeness and antiquity make it part of the county’s mythic landscape.

Moorland rocks also gather legendary names. Robin Hood’s Bed on Blackstone Edge, on the Lancashire/Yorkshire borderland, is a natural rock formation associated in local tradition with the outlaw sleeping there. Northern Antiquarian records the place-name in nineteenth-century boundary material and notes later folklore about Robin Hood resting on the high moor.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.thenorthernantiquarian.org/2011/03/25/robin-hoods-bed/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: thenorthernantiquarian.org">[The Northern Antiquarian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">thenorthernantiquarian.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">robin hoods bed</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">robin hoods bed</span></span></span>

Robin Hood wells are another repeated Lancashire pattern. At Helmshore, local history writing asks why the name is attached to the well and notes that several Robin Hood’s Wells occur in the region, even though there is no strong evidence that the outlaw figure visited Lancashire.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://lancashirepast.com/2018/05/17/robin-hoods-well-helmshore-near-bury/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: lancashirepast.com">[Lancashire Past]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">lancashirepast.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">robin hoods well helmshore near bury</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">robin hoods well helmshore near bury</span></span></span> This is a classic folklore process: a famous national figure becomes a label for wells, stones and landscape features, replacing or absorbing older local meanings.

The reader’s takeaway should be cautious but appreciative. Not every “Robin Hood” stone proves a medieval outlaw legend. Not every ancient monument has a surviving prehistoric myth. But these places show how Lancashire people have repeatedly used story to make sense of striking features in the land.

## Dragons, black dogs and other darker beings

Lancashire does not have one dragon legend as famous as the Lambton Worm in County Durham, but dragon stories do appear in the county’s nineteenth-century legend collections. *Lancashire Legends, Traditions, Pageants, Sports, &c.* includes “Unsworth and its Dragons”, placing one of Lancashire’s noted dragon traditions at Unsworth, near Bury.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Lancashire_Legends%2C_Traditions%2C_Pageants%2C_Sports%2C_%26c./Part_1/Unsworth_and_its_Dragons" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: en.wikisource.org">[Wikisource]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">en.wikisource.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wikisource.org.</span></span></span>

Such dragon tales often belong to the wider northern “worm” tradition: monstrous serpents or dragons tied to a locality, a hero, a danger to livestock or people, and a landscape feature. In Lancashire, they are less central to county identity than Pendle witches or boggarts, but they are still useful because they connect the county to a broader English and northern pattern of dragon-slaying legend.

Black dogs appear most memorably through the Pendle story of familiars. In modern Pendle sculpture and retelling, the black dog Dandy is associated with James Device and the witch-trial narrative. A Ribble Valley arts page describes the Dandy story as an encounter with a large black dog in the night, and recent local heritage writing discusses the Clitheroe sculpture inspired by Lancashire history and folklore.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://rvarts.co.uk/dandy/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: rvarts.co.uk">[RVArts]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">rvarts.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on rvarts.co.uk.</span></span></span>

It is important not to flatten all black dogs into one creature. British black dogs can be death omens, guardians, devils, familiars or place-haunting apparitions depending on region and story. In Lancashire, the Pendle black dog is best understood through witch-trial testimony and later retelling, while other boggart-like or spectral animals belong to the county’s broader supernatural landscape.

## Customs that kept folklore public

Lancashire folklore is not only about beings and hauntings. Some of its strongest traditions are performed customs: seasonal acts that made belief, identity and memory visible in the street.

Pace-egging is one of the clearest examples. Lancashire Museums describes pace-eggers as once common in Lancashire villages: dressed mummers who processed at Easter, sang, danced, performed themes of death and resurrection, and collected money or decorated eggs.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://lancashiremuseumsstories.wordpress.com/2021/04/02/easter-pace-egging/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: lancashiremuseumsstories.wordpress.com">[Stories from Lancashire Museums]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">lancashiremuseumsstories.wordpress.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">easter pace egging</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">easter pace egging</span></span></span> A specialist account of the Lancashire Pace-Egg Play calls it largely a Lancashire phenomenon with an extension into the Pennine West Riding, usually involving stock characters such as St George, Slasher, a Doctor, fools and comic or exotic figures.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://hebdenbridge.co.uk/features/docs/pace-egging.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: hebdenbridge.co.uk">[Hebden Bridge]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">hebdenbridge.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on hebdenbridge.co.uk.</span></span></span>

Rushbearing belongs to a different rhythm of the year. The custom involved bringing rushes to church, originally to cover floors, but it became a festive procession with carts, garlands, music and dancing. The tradition was especially elaborate in north-west England, and historical summaries note that Lancashire towns such as Rochdale could once assemble several rushcarts from surrounding villages.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rushbearing" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wikipedia.org.</span></span></span> Newchurch-in-Pendle is one modern survival or revival point, showing how a once-common custom can become a treasured local marker.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://calendarcustoms.com/articles/newchurch-in-pendle-rushbearing/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: calendarcustoms.com">[Calendar Customs]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">calendarcustoms.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on calendarcustoms.com.</span></span></span>

Riding the Black Lad at Ashton-under-Lyne is stranger and more local. Tameside’s own history page says the custom was unique to Ashton and connected it with Sir Ralph de Assheton, the so-called Black Knight, a figure associated with Richard III; by the twentieth century the custom had changed into a carnival-like Black Knight Pageant.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.tameside.gov.uk/towns/ashton-under-lyne-menu" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: tameside.gov.uk">[Tameside Metropolitan Borough Council]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">tameside.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on tameside.gov.uk.</span></span></span> An older folklore article in *Folk-Lore* discussed “Riding the Black Lad” as an “immemorial custom” still remembered in the 1890s.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.jstor.org/stable/1253739" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: jstor.org">[JSTOR]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">jstor.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on jstor.org.</span></span></span>

These customs matter because they show folklore as social action. People did not only tell Lancashire folklore by the fireside. They carried it in processions, sang it in streets, performed it in disguise and attached it to church calendars, Easter gatherings and local civic pride.

## Collectors, antiquarians and modern retellers

Much of what readers now call Lancashire folklore survives because nineteenth-century collectors wrote it down. John Harland and T. T. Wilkinson’s *Lancashire Folk-lore* is central because it deliberately gathered beliefs, charms, customs, sayings and seasonal practices at a time when many were thought to be fading. The editors themselves said they did not claim to exhaust the field, but to preserve the more striking features of local superstition and custom.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/files/41148/41148-h/41148-h.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">41148 h</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Source details in endnotes.</span></span></span>

John Roby’s *Traditions of Lancashire* and later legend collections helped shape the county’s storytelling voice. These works are valuable, but they are literary-antiquarian rather than simple recordings of oral speech. Roby and his contemporaries often turned local material into dramatic prose, which means modern readers should enjoy them while asking where the underlying tradition ends and the author’s romance begins.

Modern scholarship has added a sharper lens. Work on the Lancashire witches distinguishes trial record, political context, gendered representation, fiction and commemoration. Recent research on boggarts and fairies similarly asks how dialect, place-name, oral memory and printed folklore interacted.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.jstor.org/stable/j.ctt155jb7v" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: jstor.org">[JSTOR]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">jstor.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on jstor.org.</span></span></span>

Tourism and local media now play their own part. Pendle witch trails, Dandy the black dog, ghost walks, museum blogs, local-history websites and community festivals keep Lancashire folklore visible. They can simplify stories, but they also keep them alive for new audiences. The best modern retellings do not pretend every legend is literally true; they show why people cared enough to keep telling it.

## Why Lancashire folklore still feels distinctive

Lancashire folklore has a particular atmosphere because it joins three landscapes that are often treated separately: the wild upland county of Pendle, Bowland and the Pennine edge; the wet county of meres, mosses, wells and sands; and the industrial county of mills, canals, towns and cloughs. Few counties make that combination so visible.

The Pendle witches give the county a tragic historical centre. Boggarts give it a rough northern supernatural vocabulary. Fairy caves and canal ruins show how industrial remains become enchanted. Holy wells preserve older habits of healing and offering. Pace-egging and rushbearing show how custom made folklore public. Robin Hood stones and wells reveal how national legends settle into local ground.

Lancashire’s folklore is therefore not a museum of quaint survivals. It is a record of how communities explained danger, illness, injustice, water, ruins, seasonal change, frightening places and memorable people. Its stories are strongest when read with both sympathy and caution: sympathy for the fears and hopes that produced them, and caution about the difference between belief, accusation, performance, literature and evidence.
