## Introduction

Kinder Scout’s Mermaid’s Pool is one of [Derbyshire]({{ 'where-derbyshire-turns-landscape-into/' | relative_url }})’s strangest water legends: a mermaid, nymph or mountain water-woman is said to haunt a lonely pool below Kinder Downfall, far from the sea, and to grant immortality to the person who sees her at the right enchanted moment. The core tradition is well attested by the late nineteenth century, especially in Robert Charles Hope’s 1893 collection of English holy-well lore, which records a Hayfield tradition of a beautiful nymph bathing in the pool and giving immortality to the man lucky enough to see her.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/stream/legendaryloreofh00hope/legendaryloreofh00hope_djvu.txt" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on archive.org.</span></span></span>



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/Derbyshire_ebae69_kinder_mermaid_water_b38d07-overview.webp" | relative_url }}" alt="Overview image for Kinder Mermaid" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

The story matters because it makes Derbyshire’s upland water feel both beautiful and dangerous. Unlike the county’s [well dressings]({{ 'well-dressing/' | relative_url }}), which turn water into public thanksgiving, the Kinder mermaid belongs to the lonely moor: peat, gritstone, mist, falling water and old paths from Hayfield and Edale. Modern retellings often make her more threatening, but the older recorded version is subtler. She is not simply a sea-maid stranded inland; she is a moorland water spirit, attached to a place where water appears in surprising, powerful forms.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://visitpeakdistrict.com/business-directory/kinder-scout" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: visitpeakdistrict.com">[Visit Peak District &amp; Derbyshire]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">visitpeakdistrict.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Visit Peak District &amp; DerbyshireKinder ScoutFrom Hayfield head up William Clough, working your way to Sandy Heys and Kinder Downfall - th...</span></span></span>

## The Mermaid’s Pool story

Mermaid’s Pool lies near Kinder Downfall on Kinder Scout, within the Dark Peak part of Derbyshire’s Peak District. Kinder Scout itself is a high moorland plateau and National Nature Reserve; at 636 metres, it is the highest point in the Peak District, Derbyshire and the East Midlands.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kinder_Scout" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Kinder Scout</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Kinder Scout</span></span></span> The pool is usually described as a small, dark upland tarn rather than a grand lake, which is part of the legend’s odd charm: a creature normally associated with the sea is placed in a remote moorland hollow.

The best-known form of the legend says that the mermaid appears at Easter, most often on Easter Eve, Easter Sunday, midnight or sunrise depending on the version. Whoever sees her may receive the gift of immortality or unusually long life. Hope’s 1893 account gives the simplest and most important form: near the Downfall, not far from Hayfield, “a beautiful nymph” is said to live in the side of the Scout, bathe daily in Mermaid’s Pool, and make immortal the man who has the good fortune to see her.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/stream/legendaryloreofh00hope/legendaryloreofh00hope_djvu.txt" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on archive.org.</span></span></span>

Later retellings add more danger. Some say the mermaid may drag an unlucky watcher into the water; others say the gift of long life is double-edged, because the sight of her beauty leaves the witness unable to love anyone else. Derbyshire Folklore’s modern summary includes the familiar Easter appearance, the promise of long life or immortality, and the darker possibility of drowning, while Tales of Britain and Ireland notes that the older Kinder figure is often more peaceable than the later tourist versions suggest.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.derbyshirefolklore.org/mermaids-in-the-peak.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: derbyshirefolklore.org">[derbyshirefolklore.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">derbyshirefolklore.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on derbyshirefolklore.org.</span></span></span>

That difference is important. In older source-trails, the Kinder figure is often called a nymph or mountain nymph rather than a fish-tailed mermaid. She lives in the side of the Scout, leads a favoured mortal to a cavern, and belongs to the pool as a supernatural bathing woman. The word “mermaid” may have helped later audiences imagine her in familiar storybook terms, but the early Derbyshire material points just as strongly towards a local water-maiden or upland spirit.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/stream/legendaryloreofh00hope/legendaryloreofh00hope_djvu.txt" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on archive.org.</span></span></span>

## Why there is a mermaid so far from the sea

The inland setting is the first thing readers notice. Derbyshire has no coast, and Kinder Scout is not merely inland but high, exposed and moorland. Some modern versions explain the puzzle by saying the pool is salty, or that it is linked by an underground passage to the Atlantic. These details are widely repeated in popular accounts, though they are much less securely evidenced than the nineteenth-century nymph-and-immortality tradition.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.historic-uk.com/CultureUK/The-Mermaids-of-the-Peak-District/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: historic-uk.com">[Historic UK]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">historic-uk.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Mermaids of the Peak District</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Mermaids of the Peak District</span></span></span>

A more useful explanation is that “mermaid” in British folklore does not always mean a literal sea creature. Hope’s own introduction places Hayfield and Chapel-en-le-Frith alongside other English traditions of nymphs, mermaids and water spirits attached to pools, meres, rivers and wells. He treats these figures as part of a broad pattern of water beings inspiring awe among rural communities, not simply as marine monsters accidentally imported into inland places.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/stream/legendaryloreofh00hope/legendaryloreofh00hope_djvu.txt" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on archive.org.</span></span></span>

The landscape strengthens the story. Kinder Downfall is the Peak District’s tallest waterfall, and in strong westerly winds its falling water can be blown back on itself, creating the famous spectacle of water appearing to flow upwards.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kinder_Scout" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Kinder Scout</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Kinder Scout</span></span></span> Near such a place, a legend about unusual water does not feel arbitrary. The moor already makes water behave strangely: it gathers in peat, pours over gritstone, vanishes into cloughs, returns as mist, and can turn from trickle to torrent.

The mermaid’s inlandness also gives the story its Derbyshire character. A sea mermaid would belong to coastal folklore; the Kinder mermaid belongs to a county where water is more often encountered as spring, well, waterfall, bog, reservoir or upland pool. She makes moorland water feel enchanted without needing the ocean.

## The nineteenth-century source trail

The strongest written evidence for the Kinder mermaid comes from the nineteenth century. Hope’s 1893 *Legendary Lore of the Holy Wells of England* places “Hayfield: Mermaid’s Pool or Well” under Derbyshire and gives a short but vivid account: the pool is near the Downfall and Old Oak Wood, a nymph lives in the side of the Scout, she bathes in the pool, and the man who sees her bathing becomes immortal.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/stream/legendaryloreofh00hope/legendaryloreofh00hope_djvu.txt" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on archive.org.</span></span></span>

Hope also records a fuller local story. According to “the old folk of Hayfield”, a man from Hayfield crossed the Scout in the previous century, met the mountain nymph, was led by her to a nearby cavern, and received the gift of immortality. This is one of the most valuable features of the tradition because it places the story in local oral memory rather than only in later tourism writing.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/stream/legendaryloreofh00hope/legendaryloreofh00hope_djvu.txt" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on archive.org.</span></span></span>

There are signs that the name itself is older than Hope’s book. A modern folklore discussion drawing on work by Dr Simon Young states that “Mermaid Pool” appears on an 1840 Ordnance Survey map, making the place-name part of the early written trail even before the fullest printed folklore accounts became common.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://talesofbritainandireland.com/episode-56-peak-mermaid/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: talesofbritainandireland.com">[Tales of Britain and Ireland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">talesofbritainandireland.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">episode 56 peak mermaid</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">episode 56 peak mermaid</span></span></span> That does not prove the whole legend existed in its later form by 1840, but it does show that the mermaid association was not a purely modern invention.

A second Derbyshire reference in Hope complicates the map. He lists another “Mermaid’s Pool” at Mill Hill, Chapel-en-le-Frith, where looking into the pool at midnight on Easter Eve reveals a mermaid.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/stream/legendaryloreofh00hope/legendaryloreofh00hope_djvu.txt" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on archive.org.</span></span></span> This may be a separate local tradition, a confusion, or part of a wider High Peak cluster of Easter water-sightings. Either way, it shows that the Kinder story was not floating alone: mermaid-pool belief attached itself to the High Peak’s upland water landscape more than once.

## Aaron Ashton and the long-life tale

One of the most memorable modern additions to the Kinder mermaid story is Aaron Ashton of Hayfield, a long-lived former soldier who died in 1835 aged 104. A Hayfield genealogy page preserves a Derby Mercury death notice reporting his age, while later folklore retellings connect him with Easter visits to Mermaid’s Pool in the hope of seeing the mermaid.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.genuki.org.uk/big/eng/DBY/Hayfield" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: genuki.org.uk">[GenUKI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">genuki.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on genuki.org.uk.</span></span></span>

The appeal of the story is obvious. If the mermaid grants long life, and a Hayfield man associated with the legend lived to 104, the connection almost writes itself. Derbyshire Folklore presents Ashton as a retired soldier and true believer who regularly visited the pool on Easter Eve, while noting that it is not said whether he actually saw her.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.derbyshirefolklore.org/mermaids-in-the-peak.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: derbyshirefolklore.org">[derbyshirefolklore.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">derbyshirefolklore.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on derbyshirefolklore.org.</span></span></span>

The evidence needs careful handling. A recent detailed discussion of Ashton notes that the claim linking him to Easter visits appears in John Benjamin Firth’s *Highways and Byways in Derbyshire* in 1905, decades after Ashton’s death, and that there is a gap between the 1835 death record and the later folklore association.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://britishmythology.substack.com/p/aaron-the-nymph-and-nudity-in-nineteenth" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: britishmythology.substack.com">[britishmythology.substack.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">britishmythology.substack.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">aaron the nymph and nudity in nineteenth</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">aaron the nymph and nudity in nineteenth</span></span></span> That does not make the story worthless, but it changes what it can prove. Ashton is best read as a strong local retelling attached to the older immortality motif, not as firm evidence that one named man personally practised the custom in the eighteenth century.

Even with that caution, the Ashton tale reveals how the legend worked socially. The mermaid’s gift was not an abstract magical prize; it could be used to explain a real person’s exceptional age. Folklore often does this: it fastens a supernatural pattern onto a memorable local life, making both the person and the place easier to remember.

## Gift, danger and the Easter threshold

The Kinder mermaid is unusual because her main gift is not treasure, marriage or prophecy, but immortality. In Hope’s version the reward is straightforward: the man who sees her bathing becomes immortal.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/stream/legendaryloreofh00hope/legendaryloreofh00hope_djvu.txt" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on archive.org.</span></span></span> In later versions the gift often becomes long life rather than literal immortality, perhaps because long life is easier to fit into local anecdote.

Easter gives the story its ritual charge. Several versions place the sighting at Easter Eve, Easter Sunday, midnight or sunrise. Hope’s Chapel-en-le-Frith note specifically names midnight as Easter Day is coming in, while the Kinder tradition is often retold as an Easter appearance.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/stream/legendaryloreofh00hope/legendaryloreofh00hope_djvu.txt" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on archive.org.</span></span></span> The timing matters because Easter sits at a seasonal threshold: darkness to dawn, winter to spring, death to renewal, ordinary time to holy time.

The danger appears more strongly in later accounts. Popular summaries warn that the mermaid may drown those she dislikes, while older Kinder material is more concerned with forbidden sight, favour and supernatural reward.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.historic-uk.com/CultureUK/The-Mermaids-of-the-Peak-District/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: historic-uk.com">[Historic UK]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">historic-uk.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Mermaids of the Peak District</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Mermaids of the Peak District</span></span></span> This shift may reflect a broader tendency in modern folklore retelling to sharpen ambiguous water spirits into more dramatic threats.

The pool itself encourages that darker turn. Moorland water can look still but be cold, peaty, deep-edged, muddy or difficult to judge. Modern walking comments often describe the area around Mermaid’s Pool as boggy or stagnant rather than a safe bathing place.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.komoot.com/highlight/5724741" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: komoot.com">[komoot]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">komoot.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on komoot.com.</span></span></span> A legend about being pulled into the water may therefore express a practical landscape warning as much as a supernatural belief.

## Moorland water legends around Kinder

The Kinder mermaid belongs within a wider Derbyshire and Peak District pattern in which upland water becomes mysterious, healing, dangerous or morally charged. Hope’s Derbyshire section places Mermaid’s Pool alongside other wells and springs, including Skinner’s Well at Ashford-in-the-Water, where he records an Easter morning custom of drinking the water with sugar.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/stream/legendaryloreofh00hope/legendaryloreofh00hope_djvu.txt" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on archive.org.</span></span></span> That nearby detail is small, but it reinforces the link between Easter, water and local custom.

Across the wider Peak, mermaid and water-spirit stories cluster around isolated pools. Black Mere or Blake Mere, just over the historic Derbyshire focus in the Staffordshire Peak, has its own mermaid tradition and darker tales of warning, murder or drowning; Doxey Pool is associated in modern retellings with Jenny Greenteeth, a more threatening water figure.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.historic-uk.com/CultureUK/The-Mermaids-of-the-Peak-District/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: historic-uk.com">[Historic UK]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">historic-uk.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Mermaids of the Peak District</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Mermaids of the Peak District</span></span></span> These comparisons should not pull the Kinder page out of Derbyshire, but they help explain why the motif feels at home in moorland: lonely upland pools invite stories about hidden depth, taboo and beings below the surface.

Kinder’s own water landscape is more than one pool. Kinder Downfall, the River Kinder, Kinder Reservoir and the surrounding peat moor create a chain from wild upland source to managed water supply. The National Trust notes that Kinder’s moorland has suffered from peat erosion caused by historical land management, high visitor numbers and climate change, while restoration work is helping transform the National Nature Reserve.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nationaltrust.org.uk/visit/peak-district-derbyshire/kinder-edale-and-the-high-peak/our-work-at-kinder-edale-and-the-high-peak" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nationaltrust.org.uk">[National Trust]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nationaltrust.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nationaltrust.org.uk.</span></span></span> Peak District nature recovery reporting adds that revegetating bare peat can reduce erosion, slow water flow from the moors and improve water quality.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://reports.peakdistrict.gov.uk/naturerecovery/docs/themes/moorland/case-studies/kinder-scout.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: reports.peakdistrict.gov.uk">[Peak District Reports]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">reports.peakdistrict.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">kinder scout</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">kinder scout</span></span></span>

That modern conservation context does not “explain away” the mermaid, but it changes how the place reads today. The old legend says water can give life, danger and enchantment; the modern landscape story says upland water also depends on peat, vegetation, access and care. Both make the pool more than scenery.

## What is old, what is retold, and what is uncertain

The most secure core of the tradition is this: by the late nineteenth century, Mermaid’s Pool near Hayfield and Kinder Downfall was associated with a female water being, described as a nymph or mermaid, whose sight could grant immortality. Hope’s 1893 account is the key printed source, and the 1840 map reference strengthens the case that the name was already established earlier in the century.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/stream/legendaryloreofh00hope/legendaryloreofh00hope_djvu.txt" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on archive.org.</span></span></span>

Less secure are the details that often make modern versions more spectacular: salty water, a tunnel to the Atlantic, automatic drowning, ancient Celtic rites, and precise rules about midnight or sunrise at Kinder specifically. Some of these may be old motifs, some may be transferred from nearby pools, and some may be later embellishment. Historic UK, for example, repeats the salinity and water-worship explanation, but those claims should be treated as popular interpretation rather than proof of ancient practice.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.historic-uk.com/CultureUK/The-Mermaids-of-the-Peak-District/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: historic-uk.com">[Historic UK]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">historic-uk.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Mermaids of the Peak District</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Mermaids of the Peak District</span></span></span>

The “mermaid” label itself is also uncertain. Early evidence allows for a nymph, water-woman or supernatural bathing figure rather than a fish-tailed sea creature. Tales of Britain and Ireland makes this point directly, noting that several sources suggest she is not necessarily a mermaid in the modern sense but a woman or spirit connected with the hill and pool.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://talesofbritainandireland.com/episode-56-peak-mermaid/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: talesofbritainandireland.com">[Tales of Britain and Ireland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">talesofbritainandireland.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">episode 56 peak mermaid</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">episode 56 peak mermaid</span></span></span>

This uncertainty is not a weakness in the story. It is the story’s texture. Kinder’s mermaid survives because she can be read in several ways: as a remnant of water-spirit belief, as a local Easter legend, as a romantic nineteenth-century moorland tale, as a warning about dangerous water, and as a modern walking-route mystery.

## Why the Kinder mermaid still matters

The Kinder Scout mermaid remains one of Derbyshire’s most memorable legends because she puts wonder in the wrong place. A mermaid should belong to the coast, yet here she is among peat, gritstone and hill mist. That dislocation makes the story stick. It turns a small pool into a question: why would a sea-like being live on a moor?

The answer is that Derbyshire folklore is often about thresholds. Well dressings turn springs into public gratitude; standing-stone legends turn prehistoric monuments into moral tales; black-dog stories make lonely roads feel watched. The Kinder mermaid does the same for upland water. She makes a moorland pool into a meeting-place between beauty and danger, gift and loss, landscape and imagination.

Today, walkers may visit Mermaid’s Pool as part of a Kinder route from Hayfield or near Kinder Downfall, while local-history and folklore sites continue to retell the Easter immortality story.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://visitpeakdistrict.com/business-directory/kinder-scout" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: visitpeakdistrict.com">[Visit Peak District &amp; Derbyshire]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">visitpeakdistrict.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Visit Peak District &amp; DerbyshireKinder ScoutFrom Hayfield head up William Clough, working your way to Sandy Heys and Kinder Downfall - th...</span></span></span> The legend’s survival does not depend on proving that a mermaid lives there. It depends on something more durable: the way Derbyshire’s high moorland makes water feel alive, secretive and powerful enough to deserve a story.

      flag.className = flagClass(marketId);
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-trigger-market-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = selectedText;
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-domain-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = domainLabel(marketId);
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-option]')).forEach(function (button) {
      button.setAttribute('aria-selected', button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-option') === marketId ? 'true' : 'false');
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      var active = button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button') === marketId;
      button.setAttribute('aria-pressed', active ? 'true' : 'false');
    });
    section.setAttribute('data-ebay-selected-market', marketId);
    if (persist) {
      try { if (window.localStorage) localStorage.setItem('phoenix-ebay-market', marketId); } catch (err) {}
    }
    closeMenu(section);
  }
  sections.forEach(function (section) {
    if (section.getAttribute('data-ebay-localized-init') === '1') return;
    section.setAttribute('data-ebay-localized-init', '1');
    var available = availableMarkets(section);
    var marketId = defaultMarket;
    if (window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation && window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation.inferMarket) {
      marketId = window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation.inferMarket(available, {
        storageKey: 'phoenix-ebay-market',
        defaultMarket: defaultMarket
      });
    } else if (available.indexOf(defaultMarket)< 0) {
      marketId = available[0] || defaultMarket;
    }
    var select = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-select]');
    if (select) {
      select.addEventListener('change', function () {
        applyMarket(section, select.value, true);
      });
    }
    var trigger = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-trigger]');
    var menu = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-menu]');
    if (trigger && menu) {
      trigger.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
        event.stopPropagation();
        var expanded = trigger.getAttribute('aria-expanded') === 'true';
        menu.hidden = expanded;
        trigger.setAttribute('aria-expanded', expanded ? 'false' : 'true');
        var picker = trigger.closest('.fr-ebay-market-picker');
        if (picker) picker.classList.toggle('fr-ebay-market-picker--open', !expanded);
      });
      Array.prototype.slice.call(menu.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-option]')).forEach(function (button) {
        button.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
          event.stopPropagation();
          applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-option'), true);
        });
      });
      document.addEventListener('click', function () { closeMenu(section); });
    }
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      button.addEventListener('click', function () {
        applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button'), true);
      });
    });
    applyMarket(section, marketId, false);
  });
})();
