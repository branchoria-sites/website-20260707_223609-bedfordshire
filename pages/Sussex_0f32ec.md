## Introduction

For this project, “Sussex” means the historic county on England’s south coast, bordered by Hampshire, Surrey, Kent and the English Channel, rather than only the modern administrative split between East Sussex, West Sussex and Brighton and Hove. Wikishire’s historic-county mapping treats Sussex as one traditional county, and the Historic Counties Trust frames the UK’s 92 historic counties as cultural and geographical units whose traditions and identities remain meaningful even where modern councils differ.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://wikishire.co.uk/wiki/Sussex" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wikishire.co.uk">[Wikishire]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wikishire.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wikishire.co.uk.</span></span></span>

## Why Sussex folklore feels so tied to the land

Sussex legends often begin with a feature you can still find on a map: a hill ring, a pond, a churchyard, a sea-edge ruin, a lane-name or a clump of trees. This matters because the county’s folklore is not mainly a set of grand national myths dropped into Sussex. It is local explanation. Why is this pool said to be bottomless? Why does that hillfort feel uncanny? Why do bells ring from under the sea? Why is a tree, mound or bridge treated with caution? These questions are the engine of much Sussex story.

The South Downs are especially important. The National Park Authority’s own folklore writing highlights dragons, witches and [fairies]({{ 'fairies-d53826/' | relative_url }}) as part of the Downs’ inherited story-world, while its 2015 coverage of the folklore mapping project noted how densely myths and legends cluster within the protected landscape.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.southdowns.gov.uk/south-downs-folklore-celebrating-sussex-day/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: southdowns.gov.uk">[South Downs National Park]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">southdowns.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">South Downs National Park South Downs folklore: Celebrating Sussex Day</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">South Downs National Park South Downs folklore: Celebrating Sussex Day</span></span></span> The University of Chichester map makes the same point visually: West Sussex alone is crowded with marked sites for dragons, fairies, witches, black dogs, treasure, Devil stories, gallows, bells and seasonal customs.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.chi.ac.uk/app/uploads/2022/07/Folklore-Map-Interactive-compressed.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: chi.ac.uk">[University of Chichester]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">chi.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">University of Chichester</span></span></span>

The county’s divided terrain also helps explain its variety. The coast gives Sussex tales of drowned bells and lost places; the Downs give it fairy hills, barrows, hillforts and Devil sites; the Weald contributes deep woods, iron-working memories, witchcraft and smuggling stories. The result is a folklore landscape that feels both old and practical: stories cluster where travel was difficult, where parish boundaries mattered, where old religious sites lingered, or where strange natural features asked to be explained.

## Dragons in the water: the Sussex knucker

The knucker is Sussex’s most distinctive legendary creature. A knucker is a water-dragon associated with a “knucker hole”, a deep pool or watery pit, and the best-known story belongs to Lyminster near Arundel. The South Downs National Park describes Lyminster’s pool near the church as the home of the Knucker Dragon and notes the usual derivation from Old English *nicor*, meaning a water monster.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.southdowns.gov.uk/south-downs-folklore-celebrating-sussex-day/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: southdowns.gov.uk">[South Downs National Park]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">southdowns.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">South Downs National Park South Downs folklore: Celebrating Sussex Day</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">South Downs National Park South Downs folklore: Celebrating Sussex Day</span></span></span>

The common Lyminster tale has two main versions. In one, a wandering knight kills the dragon and is rewarded by a royal marriage. In the more local and more comic version, a farmer’s boy, often called Jim Pulk or Jim Puttock, bakes a huge poisoned pie for the beast. The dragon eats the pie, the cart and sometimes the horse, then dies; the boy later dies too after accidentally taking in some of the poison. The University of Chichester folklore map records both versions and connects the story to the “slayer’s slab” inside Lyminster church, where faint marks were interpreted as a sword laid across dragon ribs.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.chi.ac.uk/app/uploads/2022/07/Folklore-Map-Interactive-compressed.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: chi.ac.uk">[University of Chichester]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">chi.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">University of Chichester</span></span></span>

What makes the knucker tradition especially Sussex-like is that it is not only one monster story. Sussex Archaeological Society’s folklore pages describe knuckers as part of a wider county pattern of dragons and serpents, and later retellings place knucker holes at Lyminster, Binsted, Lancing, Shoreham and Worthing.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sussexarch.org.uk/saaf/dragon.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sussexarch.org.uk">[sussexarch.org.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sussexarch.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on sussexarch.org.uk.</span></span></span> This is a good example of folklore spreading through a shared landscape type. A deep, dark, hard-to-measure pool becomes “bottomless”; a bottomless pool needs a creature; the creature becomes a dragon; the dragon demands a local hero.

The knucker also shows how Sussex folklore moves between old dialect, place-name explanation and tourism. Modern visitors can still read the tale as a dragon story, but its deeper value is as a memory of watery danger, local wit and parish identity. It is less a dragon “belief system” than a county habit of making strange water speak.

## Fairies, Pharisees and the last fairy places

Sussex fairy lore is notable for its dialect. Local fairies were often called “Pharisees”, a form usually explained as a Sussex rendering or double plural of “fairies” rather than a claim about the biblical Pharisees. Sussex Archaeological Society’s fairy-lore page records the county’s variant fairy names, and the folklore map uses “Pharisees” for Sussex fairy traditions at places such as Washington and Burlow Castle.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sussexarch.org.uk/saaf/fairies.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sussexarch.org.uk">[sussexarch.org.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sussexarch.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on sussexarch.org.uk.</span></span></span>

The stories themselves are often domestic and moral rather than glittering. At Burlow Castle, the “Pharisees” secretly work for a farmer by night, but when they are overheard complaining and the farmer laughs, they are offended and his luck turns bad.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.chi.ac.uk/app/uploads/2022/07/Folklore-Map-Interactive-compressed.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: chi.ac.uk">[University of Chichester]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">chi.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">University of Chichester</span></span></span> This kind of tale is common in British fairy tradition: the hidden people may help, but they demand respect, discretion and proper behaviour. The point is not that the fairies are cute. It is that unseen labour, neighbourly obligation and rural luck are fragile.

Sussex also has “last fairy” landscapes. The University of Chichester map marks Harrow Hill near Patching as “the last home of the fairies in England”, while the South Downs’ fairy-and-ghost mapping project treats fairy sites as especially concentrated in the Downs.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.chi.ac.uk/app/uploads/2022/07/Folklore-Map-Interactive-compressed.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: chi.ac.uk">[University of Chichester]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">chi.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">University of Chichester</span></span></span> Such claims should be read carefully: “last home” is folklore and literary memory, not a historical census of belief. But it tells us something real about Sussex identity. By the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, fairy lore was being reimagined as vanishing rural enchantment, especially by writers, artists and antiquarians.

Rudyard Kipling is central to that literary Sussex. *Puck of Pook’s Hill* is set near Burwash in the High Weald, around the country of Kipling’s home at Bateman’s, and uses Puck to connect children with layers of English history.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Puck_of_Pook%27s_Hill" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Puck of Pook&#x27;s Hill</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Puck of Pook&#x27;s Hill</span></span></span> Kipling’s Sussex fairies are therefore not simply oral tradition written down. They are literary reworkings of local landscape, old belief and imperial-era historical imagination. Hilaire Belloc’s *The Four Men*, a walking book across Sussex first published in 1911, also helped turn the county into a mythic and emotional landscape, though in a more elegiac and patriotic mode.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://standardebooks.org/ebooks/hilaire-belloc/the-four-men" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: standardebooks.org">[Standard Ebooks]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">standardebooks.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Standard Ebooks The Four Men, by Hilaire Belloc</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Standard Ebooks The Four Men, by Hilaire Belloc</span></span></span>

## Black dogs, white dogs and haunted roads

Spectral dogs are among the most widespread traditions in British folklore, and Sussex has its own forms. Sussex Archaeological Society notes that Sussex black-dog ghosts were commonly called Wish Hounds or Witch Hounds, and that the terminology itself blurs ghost, omen and witchcraft associations.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sussexarch.org.uk/saaf/blackdog.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sussexarch.org.uk">[sussexarch.org.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sussexarch.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on sussexarch.org.uk.</span></span></span> These hounds belong to a wider British pattern of black dogs haunting roads, crossroads, barrows, execution sites and lonely places, but Sussex gives them local addresses.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_dog_%28folklore%29" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Black dog (folklore</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Black dog (folklore</span></span></span>

Black Dog Hill, between Ditchling and Westmeston, is one of the clearest Sussex examples. The University of Chichester map records it as haunted by a black dog, citing Simpson’s Sussex collection.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.chi.ac.uk/app/uploads/2022/07/Folklore-Map-Interactive-compressed.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: chi.ac.uk">[University of Chichester]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">chi.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">University of Chichester</span></span></span> Nearby routes, Downs paths and old lanes matter here: a black dog story is often a way of giving a dangerous or uncanny stretch of road a memorable warning.

Sussex dog apparitions are not always black. The map records a ghostly white dog on the road from Alfriston to Seaford, said to appear on Midsummer’s Eve every seven years and to bring death to anyone who sees it.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.chi.ac.uk/app/uploads/2022/07/Folklore-Map-Interactive-compressed.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: chi.ac.uk">[University of Chichester]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">chi.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">University of Chichester</span></span></span> That detail is typical of folklore’s calendar logic. The haunting is not random; it has a rhythm, a place and a condition. These conditions make the tale easy to transmit and easy to test in imagination, even when no one is treating it as literal evidence.

## The Devil on the Downs

Sussex has a strong run of Devil legends, especially around hillforts, rings and prominent earthworks. Chanctonbury Ring, the famous prehistoric hilltop site on the South Downs, is the county’s best-known example. It is a late Bronze Age or early Iron Age hillfort with later Romano-British religious activity, and it became visually iconic after beech trees were planted around its perimeter in the eighteenth century.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chanctonbury_Ring" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Chanctonbury Ring</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Chanctonbury Ring</span></span></span>

The folklore is wonderfully variable. One version says that if a person runs round the Ring seven times anti-clockwise, the Devil appears and offers soup, milk or porridge, sometimes in exchange for the runner’s soul. Other versions alter the number of circuits, the time of night or the food. The British Mountaineering Council, local folklore sites and modern summaries all preserve versions of this motif, while earlier print evidence is often traced to Arthur Beckett’s *The Spirit of the Downs* in 1909.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://thebmc.co.uk/en/chanctonbury-ring" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: thebmc.co.uk">[thebmc.co.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">thebmc.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on thebmc.co.uk.</span></span></span>

Chanctonbury matters because it shows how archaeology and folklore overlap without being the same thing. The hillfort and Romano-British temples are historically real; the Devil-summoning rite is a local legend attached to the site’s age, height and atmosphere. A careful folklore reading does not need to prove the Devil story. It asks why this particular hill, ringed by trees and visible across the Downs, became a place where people imagined bargains, taboo circuits and supernatural testing.

Other Sussex Devil stories follow similar logic. The folklore map records Devil-summoning or Devil-associated tales at Kingston by Sea, Broadwater churchyard and other sites, often involving running round a tree or tomb, buried bodies, uncanny graves or churchyard boundaries.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.chi.ac.uk/app/uploads/2022/07/Folklore-Map-Interactive-compressed.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: chi.ac.uk">[University of Chichester]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">chi.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">University of Chichester</span></span></span> These are not random spooky decorations. They turn old sacred or marginal places into moral theatres: do not mock the dead, do not disturb treasure, do not cross the wrong boundary at the wrong time.

## Witches, cunning folk and the Sussex reputation

Sussex witchcraft traditions are unusually visible because they appear in both older local folklore and modern pagan writing. Doreen Valiente’s *Where Witchcraft Lives*, first published in 1962, specifically examines witchcraft in Sussex, including folk rites, hares, the moon and the county’s place in the modern witchcraft revival.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/details/wherewitchcraftl0000vali" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on archive.org.</span></span></span> Valiente’s work is not the same kind of source as an antiquarian collection; it is part folklore study, part insider modern witchcraft writing. That distinction matters, because Sussex witchcraft today is shaped by both inherited stories and twentieth-century pagan reinterpretation.

Older Sussex witch tales often involve practical harm and counter-magic. At Lancing, the folklore map records a witch near the Sussex Pad on Old Shoreham Road who could stop carters’ wagons by invisibly clutching their wheels. Carters were said to run a knife round the iron wheel to force her to cry out and release it.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.chi.ac.uk/app/uploads/2022/07/Folklore-Map-Interactive-compressed.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: chi.ac.uk">[University of Chichester]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">chi.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">University of Chichester</span></span></span> This is classic rural witchcraft belief: the witch interferes with ordinary work, and the counter-charm turns the invisible attack back on the attacker.

At Hurstpierpoint, the map records Nanny Smart, a witch who could not die until she had sold her life’s secrets; at Liphook, a witch who could turn into a hare is identified after a mauled hare and an injured woman appear to match.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.chi.ac.uk/app/uploads/2022/07/Folklore-Map-Interactive-compressed.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: chi.ac.uk">[University of Chichester]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">chi.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">University of Chichester</span></span></span> Hare transformation is a widespread witch motif, but in Sussex it is anchored in named places and practical anxieties: sickness, disability, livestock, roads, wheels, neighbours and reputation.

The important point is that “witch” in Sussex folklore does not always mean the same thing. It may mean a feared neighbour, a cunning person with useful knowledge, a stock figure in a cautionary tale, a modern religious identity, or a tourist-friendly shorthand for the uncanny Weald. A good Sussex folklore page should keep those layers separate rather than flattening them into one dramatic category.

## Giants, ogres and local villains made huge

Sussex giant stories often begin with a historical person and grow into grotesque legend. Sir Goddard Oxenbridge of Brede is the clearest case. The folklore map records the tale that he was a giant who ate a child every day for supper, until the children of Sussex lured him with beer and sawed him in half with a wooden saw, with East Sussex children on one side and West Sussex children on the other. His severed trunk is then said to haunt the house and bridge.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.chi.ac.uk/app/uploads/2022/07/Folklore-Map-Interactive-compressed.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: chi.ac.uk">[University of Chichester]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">chi.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">University of Chichester</span></span></span>

That story is striking because it makes county geography part of the action: East and West Sussex children literally cooperate from opposite sides of the saw. It is grotesque, comic and communal. It also turns a powerful local figure into a monster who can be defeated by children acting together. As with many giant tales, the legend may preserve social feeling more than biographical fact.

Sussex also has other giant and “ogre” traces in place-names and hill-country stories, but Brede is the memorable centrepiece because it links a named family, a church monument, a bridge, a child-eating ogre and a county-wide revenge fantasy. It is folklore as social levelling: the great man becomes too large, too hungry and too dangerous, so the community cuts him down to size.

## Lost bells, drowned places and holy water

Sussex coastal folklore often listens for bells. The University of Chichester map records several drowned-bell traditions, including Kingston Gorse near Ferring, where the bells of an old church and village are said still to ring under the sea, and Bulverhythe, where old church bells are likewise heard beneath the water.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.chi.ac.uk/app/uploads/2022/07/Folklore-Map-Interactive-compressed.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: chi.ac.uk">[University of Chichester]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">chi.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">University of Chichester</span></span></span> At Arlington, the church bell is hidden underground in Bell Hole, a deep pool in the Cuckmere River, and can only be raised by six white oxen.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.chi.ac.uk/app/uploads/2022/07/Folklore-Map-Interactive-compressed.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: chi.ac.uk">[University of Chichester]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">chi.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">University of Chichester</span></span></span>

These bell stories connect Sussex to a wider British and European pattern of lost churches, drowned lands and bells heard on holy days or at low tide. In Sussex, they make particular sense because the coast is visibly changeable: erosion, shingle, marsh, river-mouths and submerged remains all invite stories about places swallowed or hidden. A bell is an ideal folklore object because it is both sacred and audible. Even when lost, it can still call.

Holy wells and saints’ waters form a quieter but important part of the county’s sacred landscape. Sussex holy-well listings include sites such as St Ann’s Well at Hove, St Dunstan’s Well at Mayfield, St Helen’s Well at Hastings and other surviving or lost wells across the historic county.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://britishholywells.co.uk/england/sussex/sussexlist.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: britishholywells.co.uk">[britishholywells.co.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">britishholywells.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on britishholywells.co.uk.</span></span></span> St Richard of Chichester, St Lewinna, St Cuthman and St Dunstan all appear in Sussex religious memory, though the boundary between saintly history, medieval devotion and later local legend varies case by case.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://sussexexclusive.com/silly-sussex/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sussexexclusive.com">[Sussex Exclusive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sussexexclusive.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Sussex Exclusive Strange Tales of Old Sussex: Silly Sussex</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Sussex Exclusive Strange Tales of Old Sussex: Silly Sussex</span></span></span>

The well tradition matters because it shows another side of Sussex folklore: not only monsters and ghosts, but healing, pilgrimage, local holiness and Christianised landscape. A pond may hold a dragon; another spring may cure, bless or remember a saint. Both are ways of treating water as more than water.

## Seasonal customs and the fire calendar

Sussex folklore is not only stories about the past. Some of its strongest traditions are performed. Lewes Bonfire is the best-known example: Visit Lewes describes torch-lit processions through the town, six individual Bonfire Societies and around 3,000 people taking part in processions and firesite events.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.visitlewes.co.uk/information/lewes-bonfire" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: visitlewes.co.uk">[Visit Lewes]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">visitlewes.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on visitlewes.co.uk.</span></span></span> The Sussex Traditions archive also treats Lewes Bonfire as part of the county’s living customary culture, not merely a visitor spectacle.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://sussextraditions.org/sussex-topics-2/customs/lewes-bonfire/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sussextraditions.org">[sussextraditions.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sussextraditions.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on sussextraditions.org.</span></span></span>

Bonfire in Sussex combines national commemoration of the 1605 Gunpowder Plot with local memory, especially the Protestant martyrs burned at Lewes under Mary I in the 1550s. Local society accounts and visitor information both emphasise that the Lewes event is organised through independent bonfire societies, with processions, costumes, banners, fire, effigies and remembrance.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.visitlewes.co.uk/information/lewes-bonfire" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: visitlewes.co.uk">[Visit Lewes]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">visitlewes.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on visitlewes.co.uk.</span></span></span>

This is folklore in motion. It is renewed each year, argued over, adapted to current politics and controlled by local custom rather than preserved behind glass. For readers interested in Sussex identity, bonfire is as important as dragons or fairies because it shows how tradition survives by being performed, not just collected.

## Who preserved Sussex folklore?

The single most important named collector for modern readers is Jacqueline Simpson. Her *Folklore of Sussex* was first published in 1973, with later editions, and publishers describe it as the first book entirely devoted to Sussex folklore. It covers landscape tales, ancient churches, lost bells, buried treasure, dragons, fairies, the Devil, ghosts, graves, gibbets, witches, customs, cures and beliefs around birth, marriage and death.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://thehistorypress.co.uk/publication/folklore-of-sussex/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: thehistorypress.co.uk">[thehistorypress.co.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">thehistorypress.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The History PressFolklore of SussexAmong many topics explored here are tales linked to landscape features and ancient churches which invo...</span></span></span>

Simpson is also important because later public projects lean on her work. The University of Chichester’s folklore map is explicitly based on *Folklore of Sussex*, and its entries repeatedly cite Simpson alongside Jennifer Westwood and Simpson’s broader *Lore of the Land*.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sussexfolktalecentre.org/2015/06/16/the-folklore-map-of-sussex-and-the-south-downs/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sussexfolktalecentre.org">[Chichester Fantasy Centre]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sussexfolktalecentre.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">the folklore map of sussex and the south downs</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">the folklore map of sussex and the south downs</span></span></span> This gives Sussex an unusually accessible bridge between scholarly collection and public walking culture.

Older antiquarian sources matter too. The Internet Archive holds Simpson’s 1973 volume bibliographically and older Sussex Archaeological Collections include witchcraft and giant material, showing that county folklore was being noticed through antiquarian and local-history channels long before modern tourism discovered it.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/details/folkloreofsussex0000simp" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on archive.org.</span></span></span> Modern institutional and community sources — the South Downs National Park, Sussex Archaeological Society, Sussex Traditions, local museums and heritage centres — now keep these stories visible for walkers, schools, storytellers and visitors.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.southdowns.gov.uk/ghosts-and-fairies-in-the-south-downs/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: southdowns.gov.uk">[South Downs National Park]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">southdowns.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ghosts and fairies in the south downs</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ghosts and fairies in the south downs</span></span></span>

## Old oral tradition, literary Sussex and modern retelling

A useful Sussex folklore reader needs one final distinction: not every famous Sussex “legend” has the same kind of evidence behind it. Some tales come from oral tradition recorded by collectors. Some are antiquarian interpretations of place. Some are literary inventions rooted in Sussex soil. Some are modern retellings made for maps, walks, festivals, blogs, museum pages and tourism.

Kipling’s Puck stories, for example, are not simple transcripts of peasant fairy belief. They are literary historical fantasy set in the Sussex Weald, using a fairy figure to make English history feel present in the land.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Puck_of_Pook%27s_Hill" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Puck of Pook&#x27;s Hill</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Puck of Pook&#x27;s Hill</span></span></span> Belloc’s *The Four Men* is likewise a literary pilgrimage across Sussex, not a folklore collection in the strict sense, but it helped shape the emotional myth of Sussex as a loved and almost sacred county.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://standardebooks.org/ebooks/hilaire-belloc/the-four-men" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: standardebooks.org">[Standard Ebooks]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">standardebooks.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Standard Ebooks The Four Men, by Hilaire Belloc</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Standard Ebooks The Four Men, by Hilaire Belloc</span></span></span>

Modern folklore mapping and tourism add another layer. When a map marks a dragon, black dog or fairy hill, it does not freeze a belief exactly as it was once held. It creates a new public route into the story. That can be valuable, provided the difference is clear. Sussex folklore today is therefore best understood as a layered county tradition: old stories tied to parish and landscape, antiquarian preservation, twentieth-century literary romance, modern pagan reinterpretation, and living customs such as bonfire all occupying the same historic county map.
