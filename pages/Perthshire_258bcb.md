## Introduction

For readers, the key is to separate three things: older oral tradition, especially Gaelic storytelling; antiquarian and folklore collecting from the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries; and today’s retellings in tourism, walking routes, podcasts, museums and local websites. Perthshire’s folklore is vivid, but it is not best read as a catalogue of “proofs”. It is a map of how people made sense of mountains, danger, illness, death, seasonal change, disputed power and belonging.

## Where folklore sits in historic Perthshire

Historic Perthshire is a broad inland county across central Scotland, stretching from Strathmore and the Tay country into Highland districts such as Breadalbane and Rannoch. Wikishire describes it as reaching from Strathmore in the east to the Pass of Drumochter in the north, Rannoch Moor and Ben Lui in the west, and Aberfoyle in the south; it is also bounded by an unusually large number of neighbouring counties, which helps explain why its traditions often blend Highland, Lowland and route-way influences.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://wikishire.co.uk/wiki/Perthshire" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wikishire.co.uk">[Wikishire]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wikishire.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wikishire.co.uk.</span></span></span>

This matters for folklore because Perthshire is a borderland in more than one sense. It straddles the Highland-Lowland divide, with Gaelic-speaking upland districts historically set beside more Anglicised farming and burgh communities to the south and east. The county’s story-world therefore includes Gaelic fairy hills, saintly miracle traditions, cattle-protection customs, Lowland witch-trial memories, Pictish and prehistoric stones, and later literary or tourist layers around Ossian, castles and “haunted Perthshire”.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perthshire" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wikipedia.org.</span></span></span>

Modern administrative geography can mislead a folklore reader. Perthshire ceased to be a local-government county in 1975, and much of it now lies in Perth and Kinross, but west Perthshire was split into other council areas, including Stirling. Scotland’s People notes that Perth county, also known as Perthshire, had its boundaries altered in 1891 and that counties as local-government areas were abolished in Scotland in 1975. The historic county remains useful for folklore because stories follow older parishes, estates, roads, glens and identities rather than modern council lines.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.scotlandspeople.gov.uk/content/perth-county" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: scotlandspeople.gov.uk">[Scotland&#x27;s People]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">scotlandspeople.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on scotlandspeople.gov.uk.</span></span></span>

## Fairy Perthshire: hills, caves and dangerous neighbours

The most famous fairy landscape in Perthshire is Schiehallion, the isolated mountain north of Loch Tummel and Loch Rannoch. The John Muir Trust, which cares for part of the mountain, gives the Gaelic name as meaning “Fairy Hill of the Caledonians” and presents it as one of Scotland’s best-known fairy mountains.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.johnmuirtrust.org/whats-new/news/1594-schiehallion-fairies" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: johnmuirtrust.org">[johnmuirtrust.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">johnmuirtrust.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">1594 schiehallion fairies</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">1594 schiehallion fairies</span></span></span>

Schiehallion’s fairy reputation is powerful because the mountain looks the part: a solitary, recognisable peak with a commanding place in the county’s western landscape. Recent writing has also shown how older folklore, conservation, walking culture and modern imaginative retelling now meet there. A Financial Times feature described Schiehallion as a mountain layered with scientific history and folklore, noting tales of a hollow mountain, fairy realm, witch with frosted hair and healing well, while also connecting the hill to the famous eighteenth-century gravity experiment and the development of contour mapping.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ft.com/content/d23aa2e3-db1c-4dab-aa2d-71131192b6c7" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ft.com">[Financial Times]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ft.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Financial Times Scotland&#x27;s magic mountain</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Financial Times Scotland&#x27;s magic mountain</span></span></span>

This mixture is typical of Perthshire. Fairy places are rarely just “fairy places”; they are route markers, shieling grounds, grazing landscapes, dangerous weather zones, old ritual sites or places where later visitors went looking for mystery. Around Schiehallion, the modern walker may meet the fairy tradition through conservation stories, guidebooks and local storytelling rather than through an unbroken fireside tale. That does not make the tradition worthless, but it changes how we should read it: as a living blend of place-name, oral memory, romantic mountain writing and tourism.

Perthshire’s fairy lore also connects naturally with Scottish traditions of the fairy mound and the fairy hound. The Scottish spectral dog known as the Cù Sìth is often linked with fairy places, hills and death omens; a recent Atlas Obscura article specifically notes reported ghostly black-dog traditions around Schiehallion and explains that the name ties the hound to fairy mounds and the fairy realm.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.atlasobscura.com/articles/cu-sith-scottish-folklore-black-dog" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: atlasobscura.com">[Atlas Obscura]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">atlasobscura.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Atlas Obscura Death as a Dog: The Cù Sith of Scotland</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Atlas Obscura Death as a Dog: The Cù Sith of Scotland</span></span></span>

## Gaelic tale-collecting and the voices behind the stories

One of Perthshire’s strongest claims in British folklore is the survival of collected Gaelic oral material from Highland Perthshire. Lady Evelyn Stewart Murray, born at Blair Castle, collected hundreds of Gaelic folk tales and songs from people in and around the Atholl estate in the late nineteenth century. The National Library of Scotland describes her papers as including 240 Gaelic folk tales collected in Perthshire, with related notebooks of songs and other Gaelic material.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://manuscripts.nls.uk/repositories/2/resources/21979" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: manuscripts.nls.uk">[manuscripts.nls.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">manuscripts.nls.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nls.uk.</span></span></span>

This is important because it anchors Perthshire folklore in named collecting practice, not just vague “local legend”. The Scottish Gaelic Texts Society published *Tales from Highland Perthshire*, collected by Lady Evelyn Stewart Murray and translated and edited by Sylvia Robertson and Tony Dilworth, in 2009; the University of Aberdeen records Thomas McKean’s 2011 review of that volume under keywords including Gaelic language, folklore, folktale and traditional narrative.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sgts.org.uk/en/publications/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sgts.org.uk">[sgts.org.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sgts.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on sgts.org.uk.</span></span></span>

The value of this material is partly linguistic. Perthshire Gaelic is now much reduced, but nineteenth-century collecting captured stories from a dialect area that had already been changing rapidly. The county’s linguistic history helps explain why folklore here can feel double-layered: some traditions come through Gaelic tale types, saint lore and Highland seasonal customs, while others survive through English-language antiquarian books, parish histories and later popular retellings.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perthshire" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wikipedia.org.</span></span></span>

For a modern reader, this means that a Perthshire tale is worth asking: who recorded it, in what language, and from whom? A story gathered from an Atholl Gaelic speaker in the 1890s is different evidence from a Victorian tourist anecdote, which is different again from a modern ghost-tour version. All three may matter, but they do not carry the same weight.

## St Fillan, healing stones and holy water

St Fillan is one of Perthshire’s most important saintly figures, especially around Killin, Glen Dochart, Strathfillan and Upper Strathearn. Local heritage accounts describe [St Fillans]({{ 'st-fillan/' | relative_url }}) village as named for a Celtic missionary associated with Dundurn, while Clan Donnachaidh material notes a strong cult of St Fillan in west Perthshire by the ninth century, particularly in Glen Dochart and Upper Strathearn.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://stfillans.scot/heritage/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: stfillans.scot">[stfillans.scot]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">stfillans.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on stfillans.scot.</span></span></span>

The saint’s folklore is not just devotional; it is practical and bodily. St Fillan’s Healing Stones at Killin are a set of eight stones, traditionally shaped like parts of the body and used by rubbing the relevant stone on the afflicted area. Discover Highlands and Islands says the stones are kept at the Old Mill beside the Falls of Dochart and are said to have been given healing powers by St Fillan.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://discoverhighlandsandislands.scot/en/story/heartlands-highland-perthshire-healing-stones" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: discoverhighlandsandislands.scot">[Discover Highlands and Islands]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">discoverhighlandsandislands.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Discover Highlands and Islands Heartlands of Highland Perthshire</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Discover Highlands and Islands Heartlands of Highland Perthshire</span></span></span>

Tobar an Dualchais, the major online archive of Scottish oral recordings, preserves a 1964 account of the healing stones lying in a niche in the mill on a bed of river weed, with the bed changed annually on Christmas Eve. That detail matters because it shows folklore as practice, not just story: the stones were not merely narrated, but tended, displayed, renewed and visited.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.tobarandualchais.co.uk/track/18060?l=en" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: tobarandualchais.co.uk">[Tobar an Dualchais]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">tobarandualchais.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on tobarandualchais.co.uk.</span></span></span>

St Fillan’s holy water traditions are darker to modern eyes. Accounts of St Fillan’s Holy Pool in Strathfillan describe rituals for people believed to be mentally ill, involving immersion, stones, circling, and a night at the nearby church or priory with St Fillan’s bell. These accounts should be handled carefully: they belong to a pre-modern world where illness, faith, coercion and hope were entangled, and they should not be romanticised as gentle “healing”.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.megalithic.co.uk/article.php?sid=15319" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: megalithic.co.uk">[Megalithic Portal]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">megalithic.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on megalithic.co.uk.</span></span></span>

The wider folklore pattern is clear. Perthshire’s saint traditions often Christianise older-looking sacred places: wells, pools, stones, bells, bodily charms and circuit rituals. The names are saintly, but the actions often involve older folk logic: water taken at the right time, movement in the right direction, stones standing in for body parts, and repeated ritual numbers.

## Witches, trials and remembered fear

Perthshire has both documented witchcraft history and later witch folklore, and the two should not be blurred. A detailed study, “Feart Maidens: the witches of Perthshire”, found that tales and records do not always match neatly: some places preserve witch stories where records are thin, while archival work identifies cases that were not prominent in popular memory. The study notes Abernethy’s witches’ walk and witches’ cave, and refers to local tales linking witches to the building of the round tower, while also identifying more than a hundred cases from databases and further names from literature and documents.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.tafac.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2021/11/V27-p57-70-HallyburtonGordon.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: tafac.org.uk">[tafac.org.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">tafac.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Feart Maidens&#x27;: the witches of Perthshire</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Feart Maidens&#x27;: the witches of Perthshire</span></span></span>

This is a good example of how folklore works after trauma. Witchcraft accusations were legal, social and religious events, but later communities often remembered them through paths, caves, hills, crosses and cautionary stories. At Dunning, local memory links a cross with the burning of a witch in 1657, while Abernethy’s witch road turns prosecution into landscape.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.villagecrier.co.uk/witches_road.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: villagecrier.co.uk">[villagecrier.co.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">villagecrier.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Witches Road</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Witches Road</span></span></span>

Modern readers should resist two easy mistakes. One is to treat every “witch” story as proof of a hidden pagan survival. The other is to strip away the folklore so completely that nothing remains but court paperwork. Perthshire’s witch traditions show how real prosecutions, fear of harmful magic, local rivalries, Presbyterian moral order, and later storytelling all combined to produce remembered places.

National Trust for Scotland’s general explanation of Scottish witch belief is useful here: early modern authorities often framed witchcraft as demonic pact and harmful magic, while popular belief could also include healing, cursing, second sight and everyday protective practices. Perthshire’s material sits in that wider Scottish pattern, but with its own local geography of caves, roads, towers and execution memories.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nts.org.uk/stories/the-myths-of-witches" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nts.org.uk">[National Trust for Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nts.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nts.org.uk.</span></span></span>

## Stones, circles and charm objects

Perthshire is rich in prehistoric and early historic stone landscapes, and not every stone has a secure folklore attached to it. That distinction matters. A stone circle may be archaeologically important without having a surviving old legend; a later “Druid” label may tell us more about eighteenth- or nineteenth-century imagination than about Neolithic belief.

Croft Moraig stone circle, near Aberfeldy and Kenmore, is one of the county’s most striking ancient sites. Archaeological writing describes it as a complex multi-phase monument, with timber posts, standing stones, a stone bank, cup-marked stone and later circle phases. Stuart Piggott’s excavation report, published in the *Proceedings of the Prehistoric Society*, noted that coach drivers once called it “the Druid Stones” to passengers, a telling example of how archaeology becomes popular folklore through tour language.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/proceedings-of-the-prehistoric-society/article/excavation-of-a-stone-circle-at-croft-moraig-perthshire-scotland/DFA0C35DB67EC254A22705CC7D480DF9" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridge.org">[Cambridge University Press &amp; Assessment]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cambridge.org.</span></span></span>

Charm-stones give a different kind of evidence. The Clach na Bratach, or Stone of the Standard, is associated with Clan Donnachaidh and is now housed at the Clan Donnachaidh Museum in Pitlochry. Tradition says it was found by Duncan Ard on the way to Bannockburn, and it was believed to have healing and prophetic properties, including clouding over before misfortune in battle.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clach_na_Bratach" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Clach na Bratach</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Clach na Bratach</span></span></span>

These objects show Perthshire folklore working through material things. A stone can be a prehistoric monument, a clan relic, a healing instrument, a boundary marker, a tourist stop, or a focus for local pride. The safest reading is not “all stones were magical”, but “stones made memory durable”. They allowed stories to stay attached to a visible, visitable thing.

## Ghosts, black dogs and haunted routes

Perthshire’s ghost traditions are less unified than its fairy and saint lore, but they are widespread in popular retelling. Modern tours in Perth and Dunkeld explicitly sell the county’s “dark” and spooky past, including ghosts, witches, grave robbers and haunted locations. VisitScotland lists a History and Horror Tour of Perth that presents the “Fair City” through dark, sometimes spooky stories, showing how older motifs are now part of public heritage entertainment.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.visitscotland.com/info/tours/history-and-horror-tour-of-perth-95e1ec8c" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: visitscotland.com">[VisitScotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">visitscotland.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on visitscotland.com.</span></span></span>

Spectral dogs form one of the more intriguing links between Perthshire and wider British folklore. The general black-dog motif is common across Britain, often appearing as a death omen, guardian, devilish form or road-haunting creature. In Scottish material, the Cù Sìth and related black-dog traditions connect dogs with fairies, death and wild places; Schiehallion’s fairy-mountain reputation gives Perthshire a natural place in that wider map.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_dog_%28folklore%29" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Black dog (folklore</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Black dog (folklore</span></span></span>

A more local strand appears in accounts of the Ghost o’ Mause, an early eighteenth-century Perthshire tale in which a black dog is linked to a restless dead man and an unburied victim. The story’s moral structure is important: the dog is not simply a monster, but a sign that a wrong must be uncovered and the dead properly buried.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://uncoveringscotland.wordpress.com/2011/08/01/the-black-dog-emissary-of-the-devil/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: uncoveringscotland.wordpress.com">[Uncovering Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">uncoveringscotland.wordpress.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Uncovering Scotland The Black Dog-emissary of the Devil</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Uncovering Scotland The Black Dog-emissary of the Devil</span></span></span>

Castle ghosts belong to a different register. Huntingtower Castle near Perth is associated in modern ghost lore with “Lady Greensleeves”, usually told as a tragic or protective female apparition. Such stories are often thinly evidenced compared with archival folklore collections, but they matter because they show how Perthshire’s built heritage has been folded into the wider Scottish “Green Lady” ghost tradition.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.spookyisles.com/scottish-green-lady-stories/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: spookyisles.com">[Spooky Isles]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">spookyisles.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Spooky Isles5 Scottish Green Lady Ghost Stories, And 1 From Wales</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Spooky Isles5 Scottish Green Lady Ghost Stories, And 1 From Wales</span></span></span>

## Dragons, saints and modern roadside creatures

Perthshire is not primarily a dragon county in the way some regions are, but it has modern and saintly creature traditions worth noting. At St Fillans, the village heritage frame links the settlement to the saint, Dundurn and Loch Earn, while modern visitor accounts also point to the St Fillans Dragon and Toad beside the A85.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://stfillans.scot/heritage/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: stfillans.scot">[stfillans.scot]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">stfillans.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on stfillans.scot.</span></span></span>

The St Fillans Dragon is especially interesting because it sits on the boundary between folklore and folk art. It is not best treated as an ancient dragon legend on the same level as a medieval saint’s life or an oral wonder tale. Rather, it is a modern landscape feature that has acquired local affection, roadside recognition and a place in the village’s story-world. That is still folklore in the broad sense: people name it, photograph it, tell visitors about it, and fold it into the identity of the place.

This distinction helps keep the county page honest. Perthshire’s older supernatural ecology is stronger in fairies, saints, healing stones, witches, spectral dogs and seasonal rites than in dragons. Where dragons appear, they often belong to modern retelling, roadside art, or saintly wonder rather than a deep, heavily attested medieval beast tradition.

## Seasonal customs: fire, guising and the turning year

Perthshire’s seasonal folklore is strongest where it connects to livestock, fire, wells and community performance. Beltane is especially relevant because eighteenth-century observers recorded Perthshire May Day fire customs. Accounts of Beltane traditions describe bonfires, protective rites for livestock, a mixture of eggs, butter, oatmeal and milk, and a Beltane bannock with pieces offered symbolically to protect animals from predators and harm.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beltane" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wikipedia.org.</span></span></span>

That kind of custom makes sense in a county of upland grazing, shielings and seasonal movement. Beltane was not just a picturesque fire festival; it marked the risky start of the pastoral summer, when cattle and people moved into a landscape imagined as physically and supernaturally dangerous. Fire, food and offering worked as protection, social bonding and seasonal announcement.

Halloween traditions also have Perthshire examples. Tobar an Dualchais preserves a recording on Halloween customs in Perthshire, including bonfires, dancing, tricks, tying doors, moving animals and blocking chimneys. This is the practical, mischievous side of seasonal folklore: not a polished myth, but a night when normal order could be inverted.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.tobarandualchais.co.uk/track/1554?l=en" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: tobarandualchais.co.uk">[Tobar an Dualchais]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">tobarandualchais.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on tobarandualchais.co.uk.</span></span></span>

Killin’s local traditions show how distinctive calendars could survive. Accounts of Killin describe Halloween being kept on 11 November into the period before the First World War, with bonfires, dancing, guisers performing poems, jokes or songs, and children playing pranks; the same material notes Hogmanay as a major local celebration.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Killin" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wikipedia.org.</span></span></span>

## Ossian, Fingal and the problem of romantic invention

Perthshire has an important place in the Ossianic imagination, but this is where careful wording matters. Ossian is associated with James Macpherson’s enormously influential eighteenth-century poems, which he presented as translations from ancient Gaelic tradition. Modern scholarship generally treats them as literary works drawing on Gaelic material rather than straightforward ancient epics.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ossian" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wikipedia.org.</span></span></span>

Perthshire enters this world through places such as Glenalmond and “Ossian’s Grave”, and through the wider Highland tour culture that sought physical locations for Fingal, Ossian and heroic memory. Academic work on “Fingalian topographies” notes attempts to explain the Perthshire location of Ossian’s Grave by placing Ossian’s army near the Roman frontier after Fingal’s death.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://eprints.gla.ac.uk/115002/1/115002.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: eprints.gla.ac.uk">[Enlighten Publications]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">eprints.gla.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on gla.ac.uk.</span></span></span>

For the county’s folklore identity, Ossian is both valuable and troublesome. Valuable, because it shows how Perthshire became part of the Romantic Highland imagination. Troublesome, because visitors can mistake literary mapping for ancient local tradition. The best reading is layered: Perthshire had Gaelic oral culture; Macpherson and later writers transformed Gaelic heroic material into European literary fashion; tourists then looked for those poems in the landscape.

## What is old, what is collected, and what is modern?

Perthshire folklore is strongest when read as a set of layers rather than a single time capsule.

**Old oral and customary tradition** includes Gaelic tales from Atholl and Highland Perthshire, seasonal fire customs, healing rites around St Fillan, fairy-hill beliefs, and local accounts of water spirits, fairies, second sight and ominous beings. These are often best approached through collections, oral archives and parish or estate memory.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://manuscripts.nls.uk/repositories/2/resources/21979" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: manuscripts.nls.uk">[nls.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">manuscripts.nls.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nls.uk.</span></span></span>

**Antiquarian and early modern evidence** includes witchcraft records, parish descriptions, nineteenth-century folklore collecting and archaeological reports that preserve how people named or explained stones, wells and roads. These sources are invaluable, but they also carry the assumptions of ministers, landowners, collectors, judges and educated visitors.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.tafac.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2021/11/V27-p57-70-HallyburtonGordon.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: tafac.org.uk">[tafac.org.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">tafac.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Feart Maidens&#x27;: the witches of Perthshire</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Feart Maidens&#x27;: the witches of Perthshire</span></span></span>

**Modern folklore and retelling** includes ghost tours, conservation storytelling at Schiehallion, roadside creatures at St Fillans, local heritage panels, podcasts, blogs and tourism pages. These should not be dismissed simply because they are modern. Folklore is still being made when a community keeps rebedding healing stones, tells visitors about a fairy mountain, names a roadside dragon, or turns a dark city walk into shared performance.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.johnmuirtrust.org/whats-new/news/1594-schiehallion-fairies" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: johnmuirtrust.org">[johnmuirtrust.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">johnmuirtrust.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">1594 schiehallion fairies</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">1594 schiehallion fairies</span></span></span>

## Why Perthshire matters in UK folklore

Perthshire matters because it compresses several major British folklore themes into one county landscape. Fairy hills, saintly healing, witch persecution, standing stones, spectral hounds, seasonal fire customs and Romantic literary reinvention are all present, but none of them can be understood properly without the county’s geography.

The Tay, Earn, Loch Tay, Loch Earn, Rannoch, Breadalbane, Atholl, Strathearn, Killin, Aberfeldy, Dunkeld, Perth and Schiehallion each carry different kinds of memory. Some are Gaelic and upland; some are burghal and Lowland; some are saintly; some are archaeological; some are shaped by tourism. The historic county frame helps hold them together without pretending that Perthshire has one single folklore voice.

The most memorable Perthshire traditions are those where story and landscape still meet: Schiehallion as fairy mountain; St Fillan’s stones at Killin; Croft Moraig’s “Druid” afterlife beside its real prehistoric complexity; Abernethy’s witch road and cave; Perth’s ghost-tour streets; and Halloween bonfires remembered not as spectacle, but as local practice. Together they make Perthshire one of Scotland’s richest county-level folklore regions: not because every tale is ancient or certain, but because the county preserves so many visible places where belief, fear, healing, performance and identity have gathered.
