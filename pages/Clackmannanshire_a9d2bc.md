## Introduction

The strongest Clackmannanshire traditions are those tied to named places and records: the [Stone of Mannan]({{ 'mannan-stone/' | relative_url }}) beside Clackmannan Tolbooth, the Alloa witch cases of the 1650s, the Castle Campbell landscape above Dollar, and the later Sauchie Poltergeist. Some are old, some are antiquarian retellings, and some are modern paranormal folklore. The useful way to read them is not as proof of the supernatural, but as a map of how local memory attaches itself to stones, wells, burns, towers and roads.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://portal.historicenvironment.scot/apex/f?p=1505%3A300%3A%3A%3A%3A%3AVIEWTYPE%2CVIEWREF%3Adesignation%2CLB1947" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: portal.historicenvironment.scot">[historicenvironment.scot]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">portal.historicenvironment.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on historicenvironment.scot.</span></span></span>

## Where the folklore belongs

For this project, Clackmannanshire is best treated as the historic county centred on the north bank of the Forth, with the Ochil Hills rising to the north and Alloa as the principal modern town. Wikishire places it between Perthshire to the north, Fife to the south and Stirlingshire to the west, with Kinross-shire touching it to the east; the county also has boundary quirks, including a detached part near the Forth.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://wikishire.co.uk/wiki/Clackmannanshire" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wikishire.co.uk">[Wikishire]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wikishire.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wikishire.co.uk.</span></span></span>

Modern administration broadly reinforces the same local identity. Clackmannanshire Council describes the area as bordering Falkirk, Perth and Kinross, Fife and Stirling council areas, while Scotland’s People notes that the historic county’s boundaries were altered in 1891 and that Scottish counties as local-government areas were abolished in 1975.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.clacks.gov.uk/visiting/clackmannanshiremap/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: clacks.gov.uk">[Clacks]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">clacks.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on clacks.gov.uk.</span></span></span>

That matters for folklore because Clackmannanshire’s traditions often sit on edges: the Forth crossing and old ports to the south, the Hillfoots villages below the Ochils, and routes north through [Dollar Glen]({{ 'dollar-glen/' | relative_url }}) and Glendevon. Fairy stories, witch legends and holy-well traditions do not always obey neat county lines, but Clackmannanshire’s own landmarks give the county a clear centre of gravity.

## The Stone of Mannan: the county’s founding object

The most important folklore object in Clackmannanshire is the Stone of Mannan in Clackmannan itself. Historic Environment Scotland describes the Clackmannan Stone, also known as Clach Manau, Mannan’s Stone or Bruce’s Stone, as a very unusual monument with possible prehistoric origins and associations with the foundation of the town and shire. The capstone is thought by some sources to date from the third or second millennium BC, though other accounts are more cautious and call it pre-Christian rather than securely Bronze Age.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://portal.historicenvironment.scot/apex/f?p=1505%3A300%3A%3A%3A%3A%3AVIEWTYPE%2CVIEWREF%3Adesignation%2CLB1947" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: portal.historicenvironment.scot">[Historic Environment Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">portal.historicenvironment.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on historicenvironment.scot.</span></span></span>

Its present appearance is partly nineteenth-century stagecraft. The broken capstone was bound with iron rods and placed on a large whinstone support in 1833, after having reportedly been kept at Clackmannan Tower and before that associated with Lookabootye Brae nearer the Forth. Historic Environment Scotland records both the uncertainty and the significance: whatever its exact date or original function, the stone is bound into the identity of the town and county.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://portal.historicenvironment.scot/apex/f?p=1505%3A300%3A%3A%3A%3A%3AVIEWTYPE%2CVIEWREF%3Adesignation%2CLB1947" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: portal.historicenvironment.scot">[Historic Environment Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">portal.historicenvironment.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on historicenvironment.scot.</span></span></span>

Two different kinds of story cling to it. One links the stone to Mannan or Manau, a name associated with an early people or place-name tradition in this part of Scotland. Clackmannanshire Council’s visitor page also notes a more legendary version involving “the Sea God Mannau”. A second story connects it with Robert the Bruce’s lost glove, turning the stone into a Bruce relic as well as a possible pagan survival.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.clacks.gov.uk/visiting/clackmannan/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: clacks.gov.uk">[Clacks]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">clacks.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on clacks.gov.uk.</span></span></span>

The Stone of Mannan is therefore a good example of how local folklore layers itself. It may preserve a very old territorial memory, but later civic display, Bruce legend and nineteenth-century antiquarian interpretation have all reshaped how visitors understand it. Its power is less in any single provable origin than in the fact that Clackmannanshire can point to a named stone and say: this is where the county’s story begins.

## Castle Gloom and the sad names above Dollar

Castle Campbell, above Dollar, is Clackmannanshire’s most dramatic legendary landscape. Historic Environment Scotland describes the castle as standing on a narrow ridge in the Ochils, with steep ravines on either side carrying the Burn of Care and the Burn of Sorrow. The castle was originally known as Castle Glume or Castle Gloom and dates from the early 1400s; it later became a major Lowland seat of the Campbells of Argyll.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.historicenvironment.scot/visit/all/castle-campbell/history-and-stories/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: historicenvironment.scot">[Historic Environment Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">historicenvironment.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on historicenvironment.scot.</span></span></span>

The names almost invite folklore. A widely retold legend says a king’s daughter, imprisoned at Castle Gloom for loving beneath her station, gave melancholy names to the castle, the burns and the surrounding glen. Discover Britain gives the familiar form: the unhappy maiden’s sorrow explains Castle Gloom, Burn of Care and Burn of Sorrow. Local retellings on Clackmannanshire.scot connect the same story-world with the Maiden’s Well near the old route through the Glen of Care.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.discoverbritain.com/heritage/mythology/castle-gloom-legend/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: discoverbritain.com">[https://www.discoverbritain.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">discoverbritain.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on discoverbritain.com.</span></span></span>

There is a useful caution here. The name “Gloom” may not simply mean sadness. Some modern historical guides suggest that the older name may relate to a word for a chasm, which would fit the castle’s gorge-side setting. That does not make the maiden legend worthless; it shows how folklore often turns a hard or obscure place-name into a human story.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://sistersofthebruce.wordpress.com/2015/09/12/dollar-glen-and-castle-gloom/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sistersofthebruce.wordpress.com">[Sisters of The Bruce]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sistersofthebruce.wordpress.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Sisters of The Bruce Dollar Glen and Castle Gloom</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Sisters of The Bruce Dollar Glen and Castle Gloom</span></span></span>

For a visitor, the value of the tale is immediate. The landscape already feels theatrical: burns below, wooded ravines, castle walls above. The legend gives that scenery an emotional script. Even when read as later storytelling rather than medieval fact, it explains why Dollar Glen has become one of the county’s strongest folklore settings.

## Fairy hills, fairy labour and the Ochil imagination

Clackmannanshire’s fairy traditions gather especially around the Ochil edge above Dollar. The strongest example is the Butter Well tradition recorded by The Northern Antiquarian from an earlier account in the Scottish Journal of Topography. In that story, the fairies help manage the hay at Craiginnan, coming from places including Blackford, Gleneagles and Buckieburn to work among the cut grass. The farmer repays them at sheep-shearing with good fleeces, and his prosperity depends on maintaining friendship with the “gude neebors”.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.thenorthernantiquarian.org/2018/11/23/butter-well-dollar/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: thenorthernantiquarian.org">[thenorthernantiquarian.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">thenorthernantiquarian.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Butter Well, Dollar, Clackmannanshire</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Butter Well, Dollar, Clackmannanshire</span></span></span>

This is a classic Scottish fairy bargain, but it is unusually localised. It is not just “fairies helped a farmer”; it is fairies at Craiginnan, linked to haymaking, fleeces, named routes and the hills above Castle Campbell. That gives the story a practical rural feel. The fairies are not decorative woodland sprites. They are powerful neighbours whose labour, goodwill and resentment matter to farm life.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.thenorthernantiquarian.org/2018/11/23/butter-well-dollar/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: thenorthernantiquarian.org">[thenorthernantiquarian.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">thenorthernantiquarian.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Butter Well, Dollar, Clackmannanshire</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Butter Well, Dollar, Clackmannanshire</span></span></span>

Another borderland tradition concerns Maiden Castle near the upper glens. The Northern Antiquarian places the rounded hill at the meeting of Glenquey and the Glen of Care, while British Folklore describes it as a low round hill near Dollar and Glendevon traditionally believed to be a fairy hill. Because Glendevon lies close to the Perthshire boundary, this is exactly the sort of tale that reminds readers that folklore follows glens and old routes more readily than modern lines on a map.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.thenorthernantiquarian.org/2013/04/02/maiden-castle/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: thenorthernantiquarian.org">[thenorthernantiquarian.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">thenorthernantiquarian.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Maiden Castle, Glendevon, Perthshire</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Maiden Castle, Glendevon, Perthshire</span></span></span>

The Ochil fairy material matters because it gives Clackmannanshire more than castle ghosts and witch trials. It preserves an older rural imagination in which hills, wells, hayfields and burns are socially alive. The fairies are part of the working landscape.

## Witches in Alloa and Dollar: records, fear and later confusion

Clackmannanshire has one of the better documented local witch-trial clusters in Scotland: the Alloa cases of the 1650s. The University of Edinburgh’s Survey of Scottish Witchcraft is the key modern database for these records, describing itself as a database of nearly 4,000 people known to have been accused of witchcraft in early modern Scotland, with information on accusation, trial, fate and themes such as fairies.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://witches.hca.ed.ac.uk/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: witches.hca.ed.ac.uk">[witches.hca.ed.ac.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">witches.hca.ed.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on ed.ac.uk.</span></span></span>

Margaret Duchill is the best-known Alloa name. The Survey’s trial record places her confession at Alloa on 11 May 1658, with pre-trial notes stating that the presbytery investigated accusations, took confessions and ordered imprisonment. A linked case record notes meeting places including Alloa Cross and records folk-culture elements such as Elphame or Fairyland, shape-changing, and animal forms including cat and black dog.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://witches.hca.ed.ac.uk/trial/T/JO/1471" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: witches.hca.ed.ac.uk">[witches.hca.ed.ac.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">witches.hca.ed.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on ed.ac.uk.</span></span></span>

That detail is important for folklore readers. The Alloa witch material is not simply a legal history of persecution, though it is certainly that. It also preserves the kinds of supernatural language that authorities extracted, shaped and used: meetings with the Devil, fairyland, midnight gatherings and transformation. These records must be read carefully because they were produced under pressure in a judicial and religious system, not gathered as neutral oral tradition.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://witches.hca.ed.ac.uk/trial/T/JO/1471" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: witches.hca.ed.ac.uk">[witches.hca.ed.ac.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">witches.hca.ed.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on ed.ac.uk.</span></span></span>

There is also a Dollar witch tradition attached to the Wizard’s Stone. The Northern Antiquarian records a local claim that the stone marked the place where the last Dollar witch, named Forrester, was burned, while also warning that this is probably folk confusion with Thomas Forrest, the vicar of Dollar burned for heresy in 1539. The value of the story lies in that confusion: later memory has folded Reformation martyrdom, witch-burning and a named landscape stone into one haunting local landmark.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.thenorthernantiquarian.org/2013/04/13/wizards-stone-dollar/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: thenorthernantiquarian.org">[thenorthernantiquarian.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">thenorthernantiquarian.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Wizard&#x27;s Stone, Dollar, Clackmannanshire</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wizard&#x27;s Stone, Dollar, Clackmannanshire</span></span></span>

## Ghosts, curses and tower-house memory

Clackmannanshire’s tower houses give the county a strong haunted-history feel, even where the evidence is modern or touristic rather than old oral tradition. Alloa Tower is the main example. The National Trust for Scotland describes it as a bold medieval keep dating back to the 14th century, built to guard a strategically important ferry crossing on the River Forth. Clackmannanshire Council adds that it was completed by 1497, with later alterations, and highlights features that naturally feed ghost stories: medieval vaulting, a pit dungeon and an internal well.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nts.org.uk/visit/places/alloa-tower" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nts.org.uk">[National Trust for Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nts.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nts.org.uk.</span></span></span>

Local paranormal retellings attach apparitions to the tower’s dungeon and upper rooms. Clackmannanshire.scot records stories of an old man chained in the dungeon and a dark figure watching over a cradle, the latter interpreted in the article as a possible nursemaid of James VI. These are best treated as modern ghost lore rather than securely ancient tradition, but they show how a building’s real architecture — dungeon, cradle, thick walls, royal associations — gives later supernatural storytelling somewhere to settle.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://clackmannanshire.scot/index.php/community/paranormal/ghosts-of-alloa-tower" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: clackmannanshire.scot">[clackmannanshire.scot]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">clackmannanshire.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Ghosts of Alloa Tower</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Ghosts of Alloa Tower</span></span></span>

A separate Alloa Tower tradition is the curse story: the idea that the Erskines were cursed after stones from Cambuskenneth Abbey were sold after the Reformation, with later misfortunes read back as fulfilment. Great Castles retells the version in which strangers take the lands, fire consumes the house, the tower is used as a stable, a weaver sits in the laird’s chamber and a sapling grows from the roof before the curse is spent. This is colourful but should be handled as legend, not as documentary explanation for the family’s history.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://great-castles.com/alloaghost.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: great-castles.com">[Great Castles]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">great-castles.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on great-castles.com.</span></span></span>

Other towers matter more as atmosphere and identity than as famous ghost sites. Sauchie Tower was built between 1430 and 1440 by Sir James Schaw, according to Clackmannanshire Council, while Menstrie Castle preserves links with the Baronets of Nova Scotia and Sir William Alexander. Together with Clackmannan Tower, they make the county unusually rich in compact tower-house heritage.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.clacks.gov.uk/visiting/sauchietower/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: clacks.gov.uk">[Clacks]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">clacks.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on clacks.gov.uk.</span></span></span>

## The Sauchie Poltergeist and modern folklore

Not all Clackmannanshire folklore is ancient. The Sauchie Poltergeist is a modern example of how a twentieth-century case can become part of a county’s supernatural identity. Clackmannanshire.scot places the story in Park Crescent, Sauchie, and says many people know it in passing as one of the county’s best-known paranormal tales.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://clackmannanshire.scot/index.php/community/paranormal/sauchie-poltergeist" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: clackmannanshire.scot">[clackmannanshire.scot]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">clackmannanshire.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Sauchie Poltergeist</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Sauchie Poltergeist</span></span></span>

The broad modern version centres on strange disturbances in 1960 connected with Virginia Campbell, an 11-year-old girl in Sauchie. Later paranormal summaries describe knocking sounds, moving furniture and investigation by observers, although the evidential quality varies depending on the source. A Sunday Post article from 2020 shows that the story was still being actively retold 60 years later, with Park Crescent described in popular terms as one of Scotland’s most haunted neighbourhoods.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://creators.spotify.com/pod/profile/hauntedchronicles/episodes/The-Sauchie-Poltergeist-Haunting-With-Malcolm-Robinson-e2nhdvk" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: creators.spotify.com">[Spotify for Creators]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">creators.spotify.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on spotify.com.</span></span></span>

For a public folklore page, the Sauchie case is useful precisely because it is modern. It shows that folklore is not only something collected by antiquarians from vanished rural communities. New legends form around council streets, newspapers, podcasts and local memory. The story’s survival depends less on proving a poltergeist and more on how residents, writers and investigators have kept returning to the same place and the same uneasy question: what happened in Sauchie?

## Wells, stones and the small sacred landscape

Clackmannanshire’s quieter folklore often survives around wells and stones. The Lady’s Well and other wells near Clackmannan and Dollar appear in local-history and antiquarian sources, though some are poorly preserved or hard to locate today. The Northern Antiquarian records a Chapel Well south of Clackmannan village from the Ordnance Survey Name Book of 1861, described as a constant spring near a former chapel site whose precise remains could no longer be pointed out by local people.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.thenorthernantiquarian.org/tag/clackmannan/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: thenorthernantiquarian.org">[thenorthernantiquarian.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">thenorthernantiquarian.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on thenorthernantiquarian.org.</span></span></span>

At Dollar, the Butter Well and other hill wells sit in a wider Scottish pattern where springs could be healing places, boundary markers, fairy places or simply memorable points on old routes. Not every well has a dramatic legend attached, and that is part of the point. Many local sacred sites survive in half-recorded form: a name on an old map, an Ordnance Survey note, a local explanation, a lost chapel, a spring under vegetation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.thenorthernantiquarian.org/2018/11/23/butter-well-dollar/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: thenorthernantiquarian.org">[thenorthernantiquarian.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">thenorthernantiquarian.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Butter Well, Dollar, Clackmannanshire</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Butter Well, Dollar, Clackmannanshire</span></span></span>

The Deil’s Cradle and Wizard’s Stone traditions north of Dollar add a darker tone. The Modern Antiquarian and Northern Antiquarian preserve folklore of a “Devil’s Cradle” near the Wizard’s Stone, while the Wizard’s Stone itself has become tied to a supposed witch-burning site. These are not strongly evidenced medieval traditions, but they are revealing examples of how unusual rocks and glens attract names involving the Devil, witches and dangerous memory.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.themodernantiquarian.com/post/121407/folklore/wizards-stone" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: themodernantiquarian.com">[The Modern Antiquarian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">themodernantiquarian.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Modern Antiquarian Wizard&#x27;s Stone (Natural Rock Feature</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Modern Antiquarian Wizard&#x27;s Stone (Natural Rock Feature</span></span></span>

## What is old, what is collected, and what is modern?

Clackmannanshire’s folklore is easiest to understand if the traditions are sorted by evidence type rather than by spookiness.

**Best-attested historic material:** the Stone of Mannan’s civic and antiquarian importance, the Alloa witch cases, and the documented histories of Alloa Tower, Castle Campbell, Sauchie Tower and Menstrie Castle. These have institutional or archival anchors, even when the legends around them are more fluid.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://portal.historicenvironment.scot/apex/f?p=1505%3A300%3A%3A%3A%3A%3AVIEWTYPE%2CVIEWREF%3Adesignation%2CLB1947" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: portal.historicenvironment.scot">[historicenvironment.scot]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">portal.historicenvironment.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on historicenvironment.scot.</span></span></span>

**Antiquarian and local oral tradition:** the fairy labour at Craiginnan and the Butter Well, the Maiden’s Well traditions around Castle Gloom, the Wizard’s Stone story and the fairy-hill associations near Dollar and Glendevon. These often come through nineteenth- and twentieth-century collectors, local-history writers, old journals or later folklore websites.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.thenorthernantiquarian.org/2018/11/23/butter-well-dollar/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: thenorthernantiquarian.org">[thenorthernantiquarian.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">thenorthernantiquarian.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Butter Well, Dollar, Clackmannanshire</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Butter Well, Dollar, Clackmannanshire</span></span></span>

**Modern paranormal folklore:** the Sauchie Poltergeist, Alloa Tower ghost investigations and recent haunted-place retellings. These belong to living popular culture. They can be meaningful to the county’s identity without being treated as historical proof.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://clackmannanshire.scot/index.php/community/paranormal/sauchie-poltergeist" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: clackmannanshire.scot">[clackmannanshire.scot]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">clackmannanshire.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Sauchie Poltergeist</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Sauchie Poltergeist</span></span></span>

This distinction helps avoid two common mistakes. One is flattening everything into “ancient Celtic myth”, which overstates the evidence. The other is dismissing modern ghost stories as worthless because they are recent. Clackmannanshire’s folklore is a mixture of deep place-memory, early modern fear, antiquarian reconstruction and contemporary storytelling.

## Why Clackmannanshire’s folklore matters

Clackmannanshire’s legends matter because they make the county legible through place. The Stone of Mannan turns the county name into an object you can stand beside. Castle Gloom turns Dollar Glen’s ravines into a story of sorrow and imprisonment. The Alloa witch records show how official fear transformed local quarrels and supernatural language into prosecution. The Sauchie Poltergeist shows how a modern street can become legendary within living memory.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.clacks.gov.uk/visiting/clackmannan/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: clacks.gov.uk">[clacks.gov.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">clacks.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on clacks.gov.uk.</span></span></span>

The county’s smallness strengthens these connections. In a larger region, such traditions might feel scattered. In Clackmannanshire, they form a compact folklore map: stone, tower, glen, well, burn, cross, street. That makes the Wee County especially suited to a place-based folklore approach, where the reader can see how supernatural tradition, local history and landscape memory keep reinforcing one another.

For today’s visitors and local readers, the richest route is not to ask whether every ghost, fairy or curse is literally true. It is to ask why these stories gathered where they did. In Clackmannanshire, the answer is usually visible in the landscape: a strange stone in a civic square, a castle above two ravines, a spring on an old hill road, a tower with a dungeon, or a quiet street that became famous for unexplained knocks.
