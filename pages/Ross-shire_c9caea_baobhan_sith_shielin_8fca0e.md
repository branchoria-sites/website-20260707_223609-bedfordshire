## Introduction

The Baobhan Sith shieling tale is [Ross-shire]({{ 'where-ross-shires-strange-stories-live/' | relative_url }})’s sharpest local version of a wider Highland fear: the beautiful fairy woman who enters a remote night shelter, dances with young men, and leaves them bloodless by morning. Its strongest evidence is Rev. C. M. Robertson’s west Ross-shire collection, published in the *Transactions of the Gaelic Society of Inverness*, where the story appears as “The Hunters and the Maidens”. Robertson’s material was gathered between 1900 and 1904, chiefly around Lochcarron, Kenlochewe, Torridon and Little Lochbroom, so this is not merely a floating Scottish vampire legend but a tale recorded inside the Ross-shire oral landscape.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://electricscotland.com/history/articles/folklore_ross.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: electricscotland.com">[Electric Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">electricscotland.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Electric Scotland Transactions</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Electric Scotland Transactions</span></span></span>



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/Ross-shire_c9caea_baobhan_sith_shielin_8fca0e-overview.webp" | relative_url }}" alt="Overview image for Baobhan Sith" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

What makes the tale memorable is its blend of ordinary Highland setting and sudden supernatural predation. A shieling hut, a night of hunting, song, dancing, a careless wish for partners, and a fold of iron-shod horses become the ingredients of a frightening story about desire, danger, and fairy protection in the wild ground of Wester Ross.

## The Ross-shire tale in its own landscape

Robertson’s version places the action among four men from Strathmore who are hunting in the hills and spend the night in a shieling at Airigh nan Guthach, between Loch Droma and Braemore. That location matters. This is west Ross-shire terrain of hill tracks, seasonal shelters and exposed upland travel, not a castle chamber or a Gothic crypt. The men are away from settlement, after dark, in a temporary hut — exactly the sort of place where Highland fairy stories often place human beings at their most vulnerable.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://electricscotland.com/history/articles/folklore_ross.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: electricscotland.com">[Electric Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">electricscotland.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Electric Scotland Transactions</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Electric Scotland Transactions</span></span></span>

A shieling was not originally a horror setting. In Highland rural life it was tied to transhumance: the seasonal movement of people and livestock to upland pasture, with small huts used as summer shelters while animals grazed away from the main settlement. Archaeological and heritage summaries stress how widespread these shieling sites were across mainland Scotland and the Western Isles, and how they could include small huts, dairies and other working structures.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://scarf.scot/national/scarf-modern-panel-report/modern-case-studies/case-study-transhumance-and-shielings/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: scarf.scot">[Scarf]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">scarf.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on scarf.scot.</span></span></span> That everyday background gives the Ross-shire tale its force. The story turns a familiar seasonal shelter into a threshold place, where the normal rules of hospitality, music and courtship become dangerously unstable.

Ross-shire is a good county frame for this tale because its historic geography includes both the mountainous west and the older collecting districts named by Robertson. Wikishire describes Ross-shire as a Highland county reaching from the Atlantic to the North Sea, divided conventionally into Wester Ross, Easter Ross and the Isle of Lewis; the Gazetteer likewise emphasises the contrast between Easter Ross and the mountain and sea-loch landscape of Wester Ross.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://wikishire.co.uk/wiki/Ross-shire" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wikishire.co.uk">[Wikishire]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wikishire.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Ross shire</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Ross shire</span></span></span> The Baobhan Sith shieling story belongs firmly to that western, upland side of the county imagination.

## The hunters, the dance and the fatal wish

Robertson’s tale begins almost casually. Four hunters take shelter for the night. To pass the time, one supplies mouth-music while the others dance. One of the dancers then speaks the dangerous wish: that they had partners. Almost at once, four young women arrive. After a little conversation, partners are paired off, one woman sits beside the musician, and the dancing resumes with increasing vigour.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://electricscotland.com/history/articles/folklore_ross.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: electricscotland.com">[Electric Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">electricscotland.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Electric Scotland Transactions</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Electric Scotland Transactions</span></span></span>

The horror comes not through a dramatic monster reveal but through a small observation. One man notices drops of blood falling from one of his companions. He hides his alarm and asks to step outside. His partner tries to stop him, then agrees only when he offers to let her hold one end of his plaid. Once outside, he pins the free end of the plaid to the turf wall and runs.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://electricscotland.com/history/articles/folklore_ross.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: electricscotland.com">[Electric Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">electricscotland.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Electric Scotland Transactions</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Electric Scotland Transactions</span></span></span>

This is a classic folktale escape: the survivor wins because he keeps his head. He does not fight the women; he deceives them just long enough to reach protection. When his absence is discovered, the woman pursuing him is urged on by her companions. Before she can catch him, he reaches a horse fold at Fasa-grianach. There, among the horses, she becomes powerless. At daylight, he raises the alarm, and neighbours find the other hunters dead in the shieling, drained of blood.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://electricscotland.com/history/articles/folklore_ross.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: electricscotland.com">[Electric Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">electricscotland.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Electric Scotland Transactions</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Electric Scotland Transactions</span></span></span>

The tale’s structure is simple, but each step carries a warning:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **The wish is risky.** The women appear immediately after the men voice a desire for partners.
* **The dance is a lure.** Music and sociability make the danger feel welcome before it becomes deadly.
* **The survivor survives by restraint.** He notices, conceals his fear, and escapes rather than boasting or fighting.
* **Daylight restores the human world.** The bodies can be found and the story can be told only after morning.

</div>

That pattern helps explain why the story travelled so well. It is not only a monster anecdote. It is a night-travel warning, a hunting tale, a fairy tale about spoken desire, and a local place-story attached to a named shieling landscape.

## Hoofs, iron-shod horses and fairy protection

Robertson records variants as well as the main telling, and those variants are crucial. In one version, there are three men rather than four, and they are on their way home over the Dirrie Mor to Lochbroom when a storm drives them into the hut. In another, one of the dancers or the musician looks down and sees that the women have hoofs. The musician stops playing in alarm, and the other men fall dead.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://electricscotland.com/history/articles/folklore_ross.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: electricscotland.com">[Electric Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">electricscotland.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Electric Scotland Transactions</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Electric Scotland Transactions</span></span></span>

The hoof detail shifts the story from “beautiful strangers” to unmistakably non-human beings. It also links the Baobhan Sith to a wider Highland pattern in which fairy women, glaistigs and other dangerous female beings may appear alluring but betray themselves through animal feet, deer associations or hidden bodily signs. Later summaries of Baobhan Sith lore often foreground the green dress and concealed hooves, but Robertson’s Ross-shire text is more valuable because it shows the motif as a variant inside a collected oral case, not as a fixed costume description.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://electricscotland.com/history/articles/folklore_ross.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: electricscotland.com">[Electric Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">electricscotland.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Electric Scotland Transactions</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Electric Scotland Transactions</span></span></span>

The horse fold is just as important. In Robertson’s main version, the fugitive reaches the fold at Fasa-grianach and the woman can no longer harm him. Later explanations often make the protective logic explicit: the horses are iron-shod, and iron is one of the standard substances feared or avoided by fairies in Scottish and wider British fairy belief. The popular modern retellings keep this detail because it gives the survivor a clear rule: reach iron, reach safety.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://electricscotland.com/history/articles/folklore_ross.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: electricscotland.com">[Electric Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">electricscotland.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Electric Scotland Transactions</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Electric Scotland Transactions</span></span></span>

There is also a practical texture here. The protection is not a magic sword or a priestly relic. It is the iron on working animals. In a Highland hunting and shieling setting, horses are part of the ordinary world of travel and labour. The tale makes the everyday equipment of upland life into a boundary marker between human order and fairy predation.

## The strange word at the heart of the blood-drinking

One of the most interesting details in Robertson’s account is the repeated word “dubhach”. In the chase, the pursuing woman cries that she has lost hers, while another has eaten hers. Robertson says the word was obsolete and that its meaning could not be firmly established, but he argues from the context that it refers to the blood or bodily substance sucked from the victims.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://electricscotland.com/history/articles/folklore_ross.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: electricscotland.com">[Electric Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">electricscotland.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Electric Scotland Transactions</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Electric Scotland Transactions</span></span></span>

This is a good example of why the Ross-shire evidence matters. Modern versions often translate the Baobhan Sith directly into “Scottish vampire fairy”, which is useful for readers but smooths away the older texture of the tale. Robertson’s note shows a collector trying to preserve local wording even when its precise meaning was already uncertain. The story was not simply filed under a familiar vampire label; it was recorded with a difficult word, a disputed shieling name, and alternative explanations from local telling.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://electricscotland.com/history/articles/folklore_ross.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: electricscotland.com">[Electric Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">electricscotland.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Electric Scotland Transactions</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Electric Scotland Transactions</span></span></span>

The place-name issue deepens the point. Robertson notes that a guide to Ullapool and Lochcarron had given the shieling name as Airigh mo Dhubhach and interpreted it as “shieling of my sorrow”, supposedly from the mothers’ lament for the dead men. Robertson says the name as he heard it was Airigh nan Guthach, and treats the “dubhach” explanation with caution.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://electricscotland.com/history/articles/folklore_ross.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: electricscotland.com">[Electric Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">electricscotland.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Electric Scotland Transactions</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Electric Scotland Transactions</span></span></span>

For a folklore reader, that caution is part of the evidence. The tale is well attested as a west Ross-shire story, but not every later gloss around it is equally secure. Its power comes from the collected narrative and its variants, not from pretending that every place-name explanation can be nailed down.

## From Ross-shire oral tale to modern vampire fairy

The Baobhan Sith is now often introduced as a Scottish vampire fairy: a beautiful female supernatural being who seduces or enchants men before drinking their blood. That description is not wrong, but it is a modern shorthand for a cluster of older Highland tale features. In the Ross-shire shieling story, the vampire quality is plain because the dead hunters are found with their blood sucked from their bodies; the fairy quality appears through the sudden wish-granting arrival, the dance, the hoof variant, the powerlessness before iron, and the daylight boundary.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://electricscotland.com/history/articles/folklore_ross.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: electricscotland.com">[Electric Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">electricscotland.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Electric Scotland Transactions</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Electric Scotland Transactions</span></span></span>

The story also entered broader folklore reference through later writers. Modern summaries commonly trace the shieling narrative through Robertson’s “The Hunters and the Maidens”, then to Donald Mackenzie’s *Scottish Folk-Lore and Folk Life* and Katharine Briggs’s fairy reference works. Storytelling sites and Scottish folklore pages now retell the episode for contemporary audiences, usually heightening the “vampire” angle, the green-dressed women, and the iron horseshoes.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.houseoflegends.me/footnotes" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: houseoflegends.me">[houseoflegends.me]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">houseoflegends.me</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on houseoflegends.me.</span></span></span>

That modern afterlife changes the emphasis. In older collection, the story is one item in a Ross-shire body of fairy and supernatural belief. In present-day retellings, the Baobhan Sith often becomes a named monster, suitable for Halloween lists, fantasy bestiaries and dark-fairy fiction. House of Legends notes that the tale was recorded by Robertson and later picked up by Mackenzie and Briggs, while popular Scottish storytelling pages frame it directly as “the Scottish Baobhan Sith” or “Scottish vampire” material.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.houseoflegends.me/footnotes" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: houseoflegends.me">[House of Legends]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">houseoflegends.me</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on houseoflegends.me.</span></span></span>

The safest way to read that development is not as corruption, but as refocusing. Modern audiences recognise “vampire” instantly; older Highland audiences may have heard a warning about fairy women, night wishes, music, blood, hoofs and iron. The Ross-shire case sits at the hinge between those two ways of telling.

## Why this tale matters for Ross-shire folklore

The Baobhan Sith shieling tale matters because it shows Ross-shire folklore at close range. It is not a vague “Highland legend” with no roots. It has a named collector, a publication trail, a west Ross-shire collecting area, a named shieling, a route variant over the Dirrie Mor to Lochbroom, and detailed oral variants preserved beside the main tale. Robertson himself makes clear that many of the items he collected were not necessarily unique to the district, but that they proved these beliefs belonged within the west Ross-shire area of circulation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://electricscotland.com/history/articles/folklore_ross.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: electricscotland.com">[Electric Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">electricscotland.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Electric Scotland Transactions</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Electric Scotland Transactions</span></span></span>

It also captures a particular Ross-shire mood: the mingling of beauty and danger in remote ground. The shieling is practical, the music sociable, the women attractive, and the horses ordinary. Yet the story turns all of them into signs in a supernatural system. A spoken wish can summon the wrong answer. A dance can become a feeding. A plaid can become a trick. A horse fold can become sanctuary.

For readers exploring Ross-shire legends, this tale belongs alongside [fairy hills]({{ 'fairy-hills-1ce0fd/' | relative_url }}), second sight, uncanny roads, witchcraft stories and haunted burns because it shows how local tradition made the landscape morally charged. Wester Ross was not imagined merely as empty mountain country. In tales like this, its huts, tracks, corries and folds were alive with rules: be careful what you wish for after dark, watch the feet of strangers, keep near iron, and do not assume that beauty at the door is safe.

## What is old, what is later, and what should be treated carefully

The oldest secure anchor for this Ross-shire case is Robertson’s early twentieth-century publication of material collected in the first years of the 1900s. That does not mean the story began then; oral tales are often older than their first printed record. It does mean that claims about great antiquity, pagan survival or exact medieval origin should be treated cautiously unless tied to stronger evidence.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://electricscotland.com/history/articles/folklore_ross.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: electricscotland.com">[Electric Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">electricscotland.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Electric Scotland Transactions</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Electric Scotland Transactions</span></span></span>

The blood-drinking element is firmly in the Ross-shire text. The women’s hoofs are also present, but as a variant rather than the single fixed form of the tale. The iron-horse protection is strongly implied by the horse-fold escape in Robertson and made explicit in later interpretation and retelling. The “vampire fairy” label is a useful modern bridge for readers, but it should not erase the older fairy logic of the tale: sudden wish fulfilment, dangerous women from outside the human household, enchantment through music and dance, animal signs, and the protective force of iron.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://electricscotland.com/history/articles/folklore_ross.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: electricscotland.com">[Electric Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">electricscotland.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Electric Scotland Transactions</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Electric Scotland Transactions</span></span></span>

The most responsible retelling, then, is neither dry nor overblown. It should keep the terror of the scene — the lonely shieling, the dance, the blood, the flight to the horses — while making clear that the evidence comes from collected oral tradition, not from a single timeless “myth” with one official form. That is exactly what makes the Ross-shire Baobhan Sith valuable: it is a local tale with enough detail to feel rooted, and enough variation to show that it was alive in telling.
