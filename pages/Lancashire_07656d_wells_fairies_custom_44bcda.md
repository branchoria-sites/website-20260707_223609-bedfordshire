## Introduction

[Lancashire]({{ 'where-lancashire-folklore-still-haunts/' | relative_url }})’s holy wells, fairy places and seasonal customs show how old belief survived in everyday settings: a spring beside a lane, a cave above a river, a village Easter play, or a rushcart taken to church. This is the quieter side of Lancashire folklore, less famous than Pendle but just as revealing. It links Christian devotion, healing water, fairy sight, parish identity and calendar performance to specific places where people gathered, made offerings, told warnings, and kept annual habits alive. In historic-county terms, Lancashire also includes places now commonly placed in Greater Manchester, Merseyside and parts of Cumbria, so its folklore map is wider than the modern county council area. The Association of British Counties describes the historic county as running from the Mersey to Morecambe Bay, with Furness north of the sands, while Wikimedia Commons’ historic-counties map records the older county framework used by this project.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://abcounties.com/counties/county-profiles/lancashire/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: abcounties.com">[Association of British Counties]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">abcounties.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Association of British Counties Lancashire</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Association of British Counties Lancashire</span></span></span>



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/Lancashire_07656d_wells_fairies_custom_44bcda-overview.webp" | relative_url }}" alt="Overview image for Wells &amp; Customs" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

The main pattern is not a single “mythology”, but a practical landscape of local observance. Wells were visited for healing or blessing; fairy places explained strange caves, springs and hilltop remains; Easter and summer customs turned belief into performance. Victorian collectors John Harland and T. T. Wilkinson preserved many of these traditions in *Lancashire Folk-lore*, a work explicitly concerned with “superstitious beliefs and practices” as well as “local customs and usages” in the County Palatine.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/files/41148/41148-h/41148-h.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on gutenberg.org.</span></span></span>

## Healing wells and legendary stones

Holy wells matter in Lancashire because they sit at the meeting-point of religion, medicine, travel and local story. Historic England’s national description of holy wells notes that they are water sources with Christian associations, but also recognises that some may continue older sacred-spring traditions; medieval well cults often involved healing hopes, baptismal use, offerings, coins or objects placed to keep the well’s “sympathy” with the visitor.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://historicengland.org.uk/listing/the-list/list-entry/1009493" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: historicengland.org.uk">[Historic England]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">historicengland.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Historic England Scarisbrick Park holy well, Scarisbrick</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Historic England Scarisbrick Park holy well, Scarisbrick</span></span></span> In Lancashire this broad pattern becomes local and concrete: St Anne’s Well near Rainhill and Sutton, St Helen’s wells at Brindle and Sefton, Peggy’s Well near Waddow Hall, and Scarisbrick Park holy well all show different ways in which water became story-bearing ground.

St Anne’s Well, on the boundary between Rainhill and Sutton St Helens, is one of the clearest examples because it is both a scheduled monument and a legendary healing site. Historic England describes it as a medieval shallow square basin of dressed sandstone, about 1.75 metres wide and 1.2 metres deep, with steps leading down into it. The official record also notes its nineteenth-century reputation for healing eye diseases, a missing medieval-looking stone figure of a woman with a pitcher, and reports of a burial ground nearby.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://historicengland.org.uk/listing/the-list/list-entry/1019449" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: historicengland.org.uk">[Historic England]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">historicengland.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Historic England St Anne&#x27;s Well, Non Civil Parish</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Historic England St Anne&#x27;s Well, Non Civil Parish</span></span></span> A Merseyside Archaeological Society paper gives a more detailed local-history frame: the well is marked on the first edition Ordnance Survey map, appears in tithe-map evidence, and had traditions linking it with a chapel, burial ground, monastic property and ill luck when supposedly sacred stones were reused elsewhere.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.merseysidearchsoc.com/uploads/2/7/2/9/2729758/jmas_3_paper_4.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: merseysidearchsoc.com">[Merseyside Archaeological Society]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">merseysidearchsoc.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Merseyside Archaeological Society</span></span></span>

What makes St Anne’s Well valuable for folklore is the overlap between physical evidence and narrative memory. The masonry, basin and antiquarian descriptions show that this was not merely a vague “old well”. At the same time, the traditions around healing water, eye cures, burial ground, vanished chapel and punished disturbance show how people explained a sacred place after its original use had faded. A modern civic account adds that the well had become buried through farming and was later investigated and repaired with Historic England involvement, reminding readers that survival can depend as much on archaeology and conservation as on uninterrupted public visiting.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://rainhillcivic.org.uk/2024/09/15/st-annes-well/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: rainhillcivic.org.uk">[rainhillcivic.org.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">rainhillcivic.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">st annes well</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">st annes well</span></span></span>

Scarisbrick Park holy well gives a different emphasis: the well was probably useful to travellers. Historic England records it as a scheduled medieval holy well beside Southport Road, near a medieval wayside cross, with a natural spring covered by a partly mutilated oval capping stone bearing a small floriated cross. The entry stresses that it was once accessible from both the road and the cross, “for the benefit of wayfarers”, and calls it a rare Lancashire survival of a holy well associated with a medieval wayside cross.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://historicengland.org.uk/listing/the-list/list-entry/1009493" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: historicengland.org.uk">[Historic England]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">historicengland.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Historic England Scarisbrick Park holy well, Scarisbrick</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Historic England Scarisbrick Park holy well, Scarisbrick</span></span></span> This is not the dramatic folklore of apparitions or curses; it is the quieter belief that water, road and cross could make a stopping-place meaningful.

Harland and Wilkinson’s older collection preserves the more uncanny side of Lancashire wells. At Waddow, near the Ribble, they record Peggy’s Well, where drownings and accidents near Brunckerley stepping-stones were attributed to “Peggy, the evil spirit of the well”. The tradition also involved a mutilated stone figure, later separated from its head, and a household belief that storms, misfortunes and accidents could be blamed on Peggy’s displeasure.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/files/41148/41148-h/41148-h.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on gutenberg.org.</span></span></span> Whether the figure was a displaced religious image, a misunderstood ornament, or something else, the story shows a common folklore process: an old object loses its formal meaning, then gains a new one as a dangerous local presence.

The St Helen’s wells recorded by Harland and Wilkinson sit between devotion and divination. At Brindle, they quote Dr Kuerden’s manuscript description of a clear spring walled square in stone and visited on St Helen’s Day, when local Catholic worshippers were said to throw pins into the water. At Sefton, they cite the observation that the bottom of St Helen’s Well was “almost covered with pins”, apparently used for omens by watching the disturbed water.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/files/41148/41148-h/41148-h.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on gutenberg.org.</span></span></span> Pin-throwing is a small act, but it changes the well from a water source into a place where people tried to read the future, ask favour, or mark a relationship with unseen power.

## Fairy caves and named places

Lancashire fairy tradition is strongly attached to named landscape features rather than to a single county-wide fairy court. Harland and Wilkinson describe Lancashire fairies as “Hill Folk” still believed among rural people, with fairy pipes found in ploughed fields, fairy revels seen at dusk, and special means such as ointment or four-leaved clover sometimes needed to see them. Their account places one sighting on Mellor Moor near Blackburn, where a dwarf-like figure in hunting dress was said to appear near Roman remains; they also record local belief in an underground city beneath the hill, with bells heard on certain days.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/files/41148/41148-h/41148-h.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on gutenberg.org.</span></span></span> The important point is not whether such beings were “real”, but how ruins, old camps, moorland stones and strange sightings were woven into explanations people could remember.

Fairies’ Well between Hardhorn and Staining is especially telling because it links fairy lore to healing water. In Harland and Wilkinson’s version, a poor woman drawing water to bathe her child’s weak eyes is given a box of ointment by a handsome man. She tests it on her own eye and later sees him invisibly stealing corn at Preston; when she reveals how she can see him, he strikes out the enchanted eye.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/files/41148/41148-h/41148-h.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on gutenberg.org.</span></span></span> This is a classic fairy-ointment motif, known beyond Lancashire, but the local setting matters. It turns a cold spring in the Fylde into a place where healing, poverty, secrecy and the risks of second sight all meet.

The Fairy Holes above Whitewell in the Forest of Bowland offer one of Lancashire’s best examples of a place where folklore and archaeology overlap without being the same thing. The site consists of limestone caves above the River Hodder, and archaeological summaries record finds including Bronze Age pottery, animal bones, flint and evidence of early occupation or ritual activity.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.megalithic.co.uk/article.php?sid=4776" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: megalithic.co.uk">[Megalithic Portal]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">megalithic.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on megalithic.co.uk.</span></span></span> Local retellings attach fairy stories to the same caves, including a tale of a Clitheroe woman taken by fairies to act as a midwife and given sight by fairy ointment.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://northwestnatureandhistory.co.uk/2023/10/29/unnatural-histories-the-fairy-hole-caves-at-whitewell/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: northwestnatureandhistory.co.uk">[Northwest Nature and History]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">northwestnatureandhistory.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on northwestnatureandhistory.co.uk.</span></span></span>

That double identity is useful for readers. The archaeology does not prove the fairy story, and the fairy story does not explain the archaeology. Instead, the two show why caves make powerful folklore places. They are difficult to reach, half-hidden in woodland, physically old, and visibly different from ordinary farmland. A cave with ancient bones and pottery is exactly the kind of place later communities might name as a fairy dwelling, especially in a county where “fairy hole” and “fairy well” names already carried meaning.

Warton Crag, near Carnforth, adds another northern Lancashire example. Folklore accounts refer to a Fairy Hole on the east face of the crag, with stories of treasure and fairies washing linen.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://folklorethursday.com/regional-folklore/lancashire-fairies/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: folklorethursday.com">[Folklore Thursday]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">folklorethursday.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">lancashire fairies</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">lancashire fairies</span></span></span> As with Whitewell, the detail is modest but memorable: a cave, hidden wealth, domestic fairy labour, and a limestone landscape that feels older than the surrounding settlement. These stories are not grand epics. Their strength lies in the way they give a specific crag, spring or hollow a second life in local imagination.

## Pace-egging, rushbearing and local wakes

Seasonal customs kept Lancashire folklore visible in public. Unlike a private charm or a whispered fairy sighting, a pace-egg play or rushbearing procession happened in the street, around the church, or at the village wake. These customs turned belief into performance: people dressed up, sang, collected eggs or money, carried greenery, processed through the parish, and renewed the social year.

Pace-egging is the most distinctive Easter custom in this group. The English Folk Dance and Song Society describes Pace Eggers as an Easter form of the mummers’ play, widely performed by young men in Yorkshire and Lancashire, with colourful costumes, comic characters, and the familiar death-and-resurrection pattern of folk drama.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.efdss.org/learning/resources/beginners-guides/48-british-folk-customs-from-plough-monday-to-hocktide/3386-pace-eggers" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: efdss.org">[English Folk Dance and Song Society]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">efdss.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">English Folk Dance and Song Society Pace Eggers</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">English Folk Dance and Song Society Pace Eggers</span></span></span> Lancashire Museums’ account similarly describes pace-eggers as once common in Lancashire villages, processing at Easter in fantastic dress, often with blackened faces, animal skins, ribbons and streamers, while performing songs and comic drama and collecting tribute in money or eggs.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://lancashiremuseumsstories.wordpress.com/2021/04/02/easter-pace-egging/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: lancashiremuseumsstories.wordpress.com">[Stories from Lancashire Museums]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">lancashiremuseumsstories.wordpress.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Stories from Lancashire Museums Easter Pace-Egging</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Stories from Lancashire Museums Easter Pace-Egging</span></span></span>

Harland and Wilkinson preserve how local and variable these Easter customs could be. In the Fylde, children and young people claimed “pace-eggs” as a customary dole, dyed eggs with whin blossom or other colouring materials, and joined in dances and plays. They also describe a local performance called “Ignagning”, later replaced by bands of boys called “Jolly Lads”, who kept the comic fool “Old Toss-pot” even when older St George combat elements were altered into more recent patriotic material.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/files/41148/41148-h/41148-h.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on gutenberg.org.</span></span></span> This matters because it shows tradition adapting, not simply surviving unchanged. The structure of Easter performance remained, while characters and topical references shifted.

A rougher related performance was “Acting with Ball”, sometimes substituted for pace-egging. Harland and Wilkinson describe a horse’s head fixed to a pole, given bottle-bottom eyes, covered with a calfskin and sack, and taken around by a small group; the creature pranced, snapped and frightened the crowd. They note that it had become nearly extinct around Blackburn and Burnley, while also linking it to the wider hobby-horse tradition in seasonal mumming.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/files/41148/41148-h/41148-h.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on gutenberg.org.</span></span></span> For modern readers, this is a reminder that “custom” was not always quaint. It could be noisy, comic, frightening, rough and socially risky.

Rushbearing belongs to a different season and mood. At its simplest, it was the annual carrying of rushes to church, once needed because church floors of stone or beaten earth were strewn with rushes for warmth, cleanliness and scent. The Sowerby Bridge Rushbearing Association explains that the practical task developed into a celebration with music, dancing, revelry and strong ale.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://rushbearing.com/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: rushbearing.com">[Sowerby Bridge Rushbearing]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">rushbearing.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on rushbearing.com.</span></span></span> The Morris Ring’s account of north-western rushbearing likewise places the custom in the practical world of rushes used for floor covering, mats, seats, ropes, wicks and other everyday purposes.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://themorrisring.org/about-morris/north-west" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: themorrisring.org">[The Morris Ring]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">themorrisring.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Morris Ring Rush-Bearing &amp; Rushcarts</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Morris Ring Rush-Bearing &amp; Rushcarts</span></span></span>

In the Lancashire and Pennine borderlands, rushbearing became especially associated with carts and morris dance. Later accounts describe the north-western rushcart as an elaboration of the church-floor custom, with decorated carts taken to the parish church and accompanied by dancers and music. The tradition is now often discussed through revived or continuing events in places such as Saddleworth, Gorton, Littleborough and other north-western communities, some of which sit within or beside the historic Lancashire frame.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rushbearing" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wikipedia.org.</span></span></span> The boundary issue matters here: rushbearing follows old parish, Pennine and industrial-district culture more naturally than modern council lines.

Wakes also form part of this seasonal pattern. A wake was a parish festival, originally linked to a church dedication day, but often expanding into fair, holiday and social gathering. Harland and Wilkinson’s account of Whalley rush-bearing shows how a wake could become morally charged in later religious storytelling: a 1688 episode describes dancing and drinking at the rush-bearing, followed by a possession narrative in which a young man supposedly offered himself to the devil to become the best dancer in Lancashire.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/files/41148/41148-h/41148-h.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on gutenberg.org.</span></span></span> The story should not be treated as evidence of a literal bargain; it is evidence that seasonal festivity, dancing, drink and religious anxiety were tightly connected in local memory.

## What these traditions reveal about Lancashire

The strongest lesson from Lancashire’s wells, fairy places and customs is that folklore often survives through use. People did not need a formal mythology to keep old belief alive. They needed a spring where eyes were washed, a well where pins were thrown, a cave with a fairy name, a cart pulled to church, or a Good Friday play performed by neighbours. These practices gave memory a route into ordinary life.

They also show why Lancashire folklore cannot be reduced to [Pendle witches]({{ 'pendle-witches/' | relative_url }}), despite Pendle’s importance. The county’s quieter traditions are more dispersed and more domestic. They belong to the Fylde, Bowland, Waddow, Brindle, Sefton, Rainhill, Scarisbrick, Mellor Moor, Blackburn, Burnley, Whalley and the Pennine fringe. Some are well attested by official heritage records; some are preserved in antiquarian collections; some survive through revival, local performance or heritage storytelling. The evidence is uneven, but that unevenness is part of the story.

For readers visiting or researching these places today, the key distinction is between three layers. First is the physical site: the well basin, spring, cave, cross or parish route. Second is the recorded tradition: a nineteenth-century antiquarian note, a local-history paper, a scheduled monument entry, or a museum account. Third is modern retelling: festival revival, walking blogs, folklore articles, tourism copy and community memory. A good Lancashire folklore map needs all three, but it should not confuse them. St Anne’s Well is both archaeology and healing legend; Fairy Holes is both prehistoric cave site and fairy place-name; pace-egging is both old seasonal begging custom and revived folk performance.

That is why these wells and customs remain important. They show Lancashire as a county where belief was not only spoken in tales, but built into water, stone, parish time and public performance. The old stories may be fragmentary, but they still mark the landscape with questions a modern reader can recognise: where did people go for help, what places felt enchanted or dangerous, and how did a community know when one season had turned into the next?

    } else if (available.indexOf(defaultMarket)< 0) {
      marketId = available[0] || defaultMarket;
    }
    var select = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-select]');
    if (select) {
      select.addEventListener('change', function () {
        applyMarket(section, select.value, true);
      });
    }
    var trigger = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-trigger]');
    var menu = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-menu]');
    if (trigger && menu) {
      trigger.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
        event.stopPropagation();
        var expanded = trigger.getAttribute('aria-expanded') === 'true';
        menu.hidden = expanded;
        trigger.setAttribute('aria-expanded', expanded ? 'false' : 'true');
        var picker = trigger.closest('.fr-ebay-market-picker');
        if (picker) picker.classList.toggle('fr-ebay-market-picker--open', !expanded);
      });
      Array.prototype.slice.call(menu.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-option]')).forEach(function (button) {
        button.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
          event.stopPropagation();
          applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-option'), true);
        });
      });
      document.addEventListener('click', function () { closeMenu(section); });
    }
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      button.addEventListener('click', function () {
        applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button'), true);
      });
    });
    applyMarket(section, marketId, false);
  });
})();
