## Introduction

The best way to understand Kincardineshire folklore is to treat it as a county of thresholds. Dunnottar stands between land and sea; Stonehaven’s Fireballs mark the turn of the year; [holy wells]({{ 'holy-wells-d2f4bb/' | relative_url }}) connect cure, pilgrimage and saintly presence; witch-pool traditions preserve the memory of early modern fear; and the Fordoun Stone links Christian legend with the older carved-symbol world of the Picts. Some stories are well-attested in antiquarian writing, some are local literary retellings, and some are living customs shaped by tourism and community pride.

## Where the folklore belongs

Kincardineshire is often called the Mearns, a name used for the old province and still important in local identity. As a historic county it is triangular, coastal and varied: North Sea cliffs and fishing towns on one side, fertile lowland in the Howe of the Mearns, and moorland and Grampian uplands inland. That mix matters because the folklore is not evenly spread. Sea-cliff stories gather around Dunnottar and Stonehaven; saintly and ecclesiastical traditions cluster around Fordoun, Fetteresso, Kinneff and Drumlithie; and older routeways over the Mounth give the county a sense of being a passage between north and south Scotland.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://abcounties.com/counties/county-profiles/kincardineshire/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: abcounties.com">[Association of British Counties]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">abcounties.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on abcounties.com.</span></span></span>

Historic county geography also needs a little care. Kincardineshire ceased to be a main administrative county in the twentieth century, and most of the old county is now within the Aberdeenshire council area, with the Kincardine and Mearns name surviving in modern local governance. For folklore, however, the older county remains useful because antiquarian books, parish records, holy-well notices and older local histories often refer to Kincardineshire, the Mearns, or particular parishes rather than modern boundaries.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kincardineshire" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wikipedia.org.</span></span></span>

This also explains why Kincardineshire folklore sometimes appears in “Aberdeenshire” or “north-east Scotland” collections. Modern publishers and tourism bodies often use Aberdeenshire as the public-facing label, while older sources preserve the Mearns or Kincardineshire. A reader tracing a story should therefore search both the county name and the local parish or place-name.

## Dunnottar: history that behaves like legend

Dunnottar Castle is the county’s most dramatic folklore anchor, even when a story attached to it is historical rather than supernatural. The castle is a scheduled monument on a rocky headland south of Stonehaven, with Historic Environment Scotland recording its protected status and associated structures such as the chapel, well, keep, stables and approach tunnels.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://portal.historicenvironment.scot/apex/f?p=1505%3A300%3A%3A%3A%3A%3AVIEWTYPE%2CVIEWREF%3Adesignation%2CSM986" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: portal.historicenvironment.scot">[Historic Environment Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">portal.historicenvironment.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Historic Environment Scotland Dunnottar Castle (SM986</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Historic Environment Scotland Dunnottar Castle (SM986</span></span></span>

Its importance in Scottish historical memory comes partly from the saving of the Honours of Scotland during the Cromwellian period. The castle’s own visitor material presents Dunnottar as a place that “played a crucial role in Scottish history” and is especially famous for sheltering the Scottish Crown Jewels from Cromwell’s army in the 1650s.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.dunnottarcastle.co.uk/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: dunnottarcastle.co.uk">[Dunnottar Castle]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">dunnottarcastle.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on dunnottarcastle.co.uk.</span></span></span> That episode has the shape of a treasure legend: precious objects hidden in extremity, loyal keepers, a sea-girt fortress, and a narrow escape from national loss. It is not “folklore” in the sense of a fairy tale, but it is exactly the kind of historic drama that becomes retold as county legend.

Dunnottar also draws in darker narratives. The nearby Carron Water is associated in nineteenth-century folklore writing with witchcraft ordeal: a pool near Dunnottar Church was said to have served as a place where accused witches were tested by water, a cruel practice in which floating could be taken as guilt and sinking as innocence. The account appears in James M. Mackinlay’s *Folklore of Scottish Lochs and Springs*, which places the Dunnottar pool among wider Scottish and British beliefs about water as a judge, omen and cure.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://electricscotland.com/history/water/chapter9.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: electricscotland.com">[electricscotland.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">electricscotland.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Folklore of Scottish Lochs and Springs</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Folklore of Scottish Lochs and Springs</span></span></span>

For readers, the important point is not that Dunnottar was “haunted” in one neat, famous way. It is that the castle and its waters gathered several layers of belief: saintly association, state violence, covenanting imprisonment, witchcraft memory, hidden regalia and cliff-edge danger. That makes it one of the county’s strongest mythic landscapes.

## Witchcraft memory without turning victims into villains

Kincardineshire’s witchcraft folklore should be handled carefully. Older local tradition sometimes speaks of witches, pools and charms as if they belong to a supernatural world, but the historical record behind Scottish witchcraft is a record of accusation, fear, interrogation and punishment. The University of Edinburgh’s Survey of Scottish Witchcraft brought together archive material on nearly 4,000 people known to have been accused of witchcraft between 1563 and 1736, and later open-data work mapped many recorded residences, detentions, trials and death places.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.heritagefund.org.uk/news/mapping-scotlands-accused-witches-through-open-data" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: heritagefund.org.uk">[The National Lottery Heritage Fund]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">heritagefund.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on heritagefund.org.uk.</span></span></span>

The Dunnottar-area water ordeal is especially valuable because it shows how belief and law could meet brutally. Water was not just symbolic; in ordeal thinking, it was imagined as a moral agent that might reject the guilty. Mackinlay’s account makes clear the trap: a person who floated was treated as guilty, while one who sank might be “innocent” but in grave danger of drowning.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://electricscotland.com/history/water/chapter9.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: electricscotland.com">[electricscotland.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">electricscotland.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Folklore of Scottish Lochs and Springs</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Folklore of Scottish Lochs and Springs</span></span></span>

This is where modern retelling has to differ from old sensational folklore. The county’s witch stories should not be framed as proof that witches existed or that accused people possessed powers. They are evidence of a belief culture in which misfortune, illness, religious anxiety and social tension could be channelled into accusations. The folklore value lies in understanding what people feared, how communities explained harm, and how places such as pools, stones and churchyards became attached to memory.

Joseph Grant’s *Kincardineshire Traditions*, published at Aberdeen in 1830, shows another route by which witchcraft entered county culture: literary ballad retelling. The Internet Archive catalogue describes the work as a 60-page verse publication, while a later biographical account of Grant says its pieces were largely founded on local legends of love, war and witchcraft familiar to him from childhood.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/details/kincardineshiret00gran" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on archive.org.</span></span></span> That places Grant between oral tradition and literary invention: useful as a sign of what early nineteenth-century local readers valued, but not the same as a verbatim record of older oral tales.

## Saints, wells and the sacred geography of the Mearns

Kincardineshire has unusually rich saint-and-well traditions for a small county. These are not ghost stories, but they are central to folklore because holy wells often preserved older habits of healing, pilgrimage, blessing and local identity long after the medieval religious world that sustained them had changed.

Mackinlay records several Kincardineshire wells dedicated to saints. Springs dedicated to St Kieran are noted at Drumlithie in Glenbervie parish and at Stonehaven; St Ninian is linked with a well near Dunnottar Castle; Kenty’s Well at Kinneff is connected with Kentigern; and Paldy’s Well at Fordoun is associated with Palladius, along with Paldy’s or Paddy’s Fair.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/files/56034/56034-h/56034-h.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Project Gutenberg Folklore of Scottish Lochs and Springs</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Project Gutenberg Folklore of Scottish Lochs and Springs</span></span></span>

The Palladius tradition is especially important for Fordoun. Mackinlay notes that Palladius was popularly associated with Kincardineshire, although his personal connection with the district is uncertain; the tradition held that a chapel at Fordoun received his relics through his disciple Terrananus, whose name survived in Banchory-Ternan.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/files/56034/56034-h/56034-h.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Project Gutenberg Folklore of Scottish Lochs and Springs</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Project Gutenberg Folklore of Scottish Lochs and Springs</span></span></span> Folklorically, that uncertainty is part of the interest. Fordoun became a place where saintly memory, local naming and ecclesiastical prestige reinforced each other, whether or not the earliest historical claims can be proved.

Fetteresso adds another layer. A medieval Marian shrine and well west of Stonehaven was described in scholarship on medieval Scottish life as famous for miracles and a pilgrimage destination for “multitudes of the faithful”, with James IV connected to that reputation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://dokumen.pub/a-history-of-everyday-life-in-medieval-scotland-9780748629503.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: dokumen.pub">[dokumen.pub]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">dokumen.pub</span><span class="citation-popover-title">A History of Everyday Life in Medieval Scotland</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">9780748629503 - DOKUMEN.PUB...</span></span></span> This matters because it shows Kincardineshire was not merely a remote container of parish superstitions. It had places of organised devotion, royal attention and public pilgrimage.

A reader visiting these sites today will usually find ruins, names, local memory or archaeological records rather than a continuous medieval ritual. But the pattern is clear: in the Mearns, water was not just practical. It could cure, judge, bless, warn, remember a saint, or anchor a fair.

## Pictish stones and Christian storytelling at Fordoun

The Fordoun Stone, at Auchenblae/Fordoun Parish Church, is one of the county’s strongest links between archaeology and legend. Trove, Historic Environment Scotland’s public discovery service, identifies it as a Pictish cross-slab, Pictish symbol stone and ogham-inscribed stone, also associated with St Palladius’s Chapel; its former county is listed as Kincardineshire.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.trove.scot/place/36458" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: trove.scot">[Trove.scot]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">trove.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on trove.scot.</span></span></span>

Pictish symbol stones are not folklore in themselves, but they often generate folklore because their symbols are memorable and mysterious to later communities. Aberdeenshire Council explains that Pictish stones are found across eastern Scotland, with early examples using incised animal and object symbols and later ones combining Christian crosses with Pictish forms. The council dates the main early symbol-stone tradition chiefly to the sixth and seventh centuries, with later Christianised stones in the seventh to ninth centuries.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.aberdeenshire.gov.uk/leisure-sport-and-culture/archaeology/sites-to-visit/pictish-symbol-stones/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: aberdeenshire.gov.uk">[Aberdeenshire Council]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">aberdeenshire.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Aberdeenshire Council Pictish Symbol Stones</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Aberdeenshire Council Pictish Symbol Stones</span></span></span>

Fordoun is powerful because the stone sits at the meeting point of two story systems. One is archaeological: carved symbols, Christian cross-slab, ogham inscription, early medieval eastern Scotland. The other is legendary and ecclesiastical: Palladius, relics, chapel, holy well and fair. The stone does not prove the Palladius story, but it helps explain why Fordoun could be imagined as an ancient holy centre. A carved stone in a church setting gives local tradition something visible to gather around.

This is a useful rule for Kincardineshire folklore more generally: stones and wells often outlast the explanations attached to them. Later people inherit a striking object, a name, or a ritual habit, then explain it through saints, giants, witches, fairies or heroes according to the beliefs of their own time.

## Fire at New Year: Stonehaven’s living custom

Stonehaven’s Fireballs Ceremony is the county’s best-known living folk custom. It takes place at Hogmanay: as midnight strikes on 31 December, local participants parade along the High Street swinging flaming balls around their heads. The event’s own organisers describe it as Stonehaven’s unique way to welcome the New Year, with around 40 men and women taking part, and a gathering that begins before midnight and is followed by fireworks.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://stonehavenfireballs.com/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: stonehavenfireballs.com">[stonehavenfireballs.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">stonehavenfireballs.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Stonehaven Fireballs</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Stonehaven Fireballs</span></span></span>

This is a good example of modern folklore because it is both old-looking and continually managed. It is not simply a relic preserved unchanged from “pagan times”. Recent academic work on the Stonehaven Fireballs traces the ceremony through British Library newspaper archives from 1848 onwards, describing its movement from a local, sometimes unruly custom of the late nineteenth century into a major tourist attraction, shaped by decline, revival, safety concerns, media attention and community resilience.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pure.hud.ac.uk/en/publications/no-pyro-no-party-the-evolution-and-history-of-the-stonehaven-hogm/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pure.hud.ac.uk">[Huddersfield Research Portal]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pure.hud.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">No Pyro, No Party: The Evolution and History of the Stonehaven Hogmanay Fireballs - University of Huddersfield Research Portal...</span></span></span>

That does not make it less traditional. It makes it more interesting. Living customs survive by adapting: they acquire stewards, safety rules, publicity, donation systems and visitor expectations. Stonehaven’s Fireballs still answer an old seasonal need — marking the turn of the year with danger, light, noise and collective presence — while also belonging to a modern town that has to host crowds safely.

For a folklore reader, the key distinction is between origin story and function. The origin may be debated or romantically explained, but the function is plain: it gives Stonehaven a shared ritual identity at the darkest point of the year.

## Fairies, brownies and what is missing from the record

Compared with parts of the Highlands, the Borders or Perthshire, Kincardineshire does not have a large public catalogue of famous named fairy hills or fairy roads. That does not mean fairy belief was absent. North-east Scottish folklore collections commonly include fairies, brownies, witches, selkies and seers, and modern storytelling collections for the broader Aberdeen and north-east region draw on that shared tradition.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://tracscotland.org/books/aberdeenshire-folk-tales/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: tracscotland.org">[TRACS]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">tracscotland.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">TRACSAberdeenshire Folk Tales</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">TRACSAberdeenshire Folk Tales</span></span></span>

The better conclusion is that Kincardineshire’s fairy material is less visible in easy-to-find public sources than its wells, saints, witch-pool tradition, Dunnottar history and Stonehaven custom. Some fairy and household-spirit lore may survive under estate, farm, glen or family names rather than under the county label. Brownie traditions, for example, are widespread in Scottish folklore as stories of helpful but touchy household or farm spirits, and they fit the agricultural world of the Mearns even when a specific Kincardineshire brownie tale is not strongly documented in mainstream sources.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://scotlands-stories.com/scottish-brownie/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: scotlands-stories.com">[Scotland&#x27;s Stories]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">scotlands-stories.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Scotland&#x27;s Stories The Scottish Brownie</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Scotland&#x27;s Stories The Scottish Brownie</span></span></span>

This thinness is useful to say plainly. Folklore pages can easily overinflate a county by borrowing famous motifs from elsewhere. For Kincardineshire, the stronger public evidence points to sacred wells, witchcraft ordeal memory, literary ballad tradition, Pictish-Christian stones, Dunnottar’s legendary history and Hogmanay fire. Fairy belief belongs in the wider north-east context, but it should not be made to carry the whole county story unless more local evidence is found.

## The collectors and preservers

Kincardineshire folklore reaches us through several kinds of source, each with its own strengths and limits. Antiquarian writers such as Mackinlay preserved notices of wells, saintly dedications and water beliefs, often by gathering older parish and local-history references into broad thematic studies. His value is breadth: he places Kincardineshire wells and the Dunnottar witch-pool tradition inside a wider Scottish pattern of water belief.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://electricscotland.com/history/water/chapter9.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: electricscotland.com">[electricscotland.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">electricscotland.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Folklore of Scottish Lochs and Springs</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Folklore of Scottish Lochs and Springs</span></span></span>

Joseph Grant represents a different kind of preservation. Born in Kincardineshire and writing in the early nineteenth century, he turned local traditions into verse. His *Kincardineshire Traditions* is not a field notebook, but it matters because it shows local legend becoming literature for a county audience. The fact that it appeared in 1830, in verse, and was later remembered as rooted in local stories of love, war and witchcraft makes it a key Kincardineshire folklore artefact.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/details/kincardineshiret00gran" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on archive.org.</span></span></span>

Modern institutions preserve another layer. Historic Environment Scotland and Trove document monuments such as Dunnottar and the Fordoun Stone, while Aberdeenshire Council’s archaeology resources explain the wider Pictish context for carved stones. These sources do not usually tell tales in a fireside style, but they keep the physical anchors of folklore visible and verifiable.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://portal.historicenvironment.scot/apex/f?p=1505%3A300%3A%3A%3A%3A%3AVIEWTYPE%2CVIEWREF%3Adesignation%2CSM986" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: portal.historicenvironment.scot">[historicenvironment.scot]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">portal.historicenvironment.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Historic Environment Scotland Dunnottar Castle (SM986</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Historic Environment Scotland Dunnottar Castle (SM986</span></span></span>

Finally, community organisations preserve living practice. Stonehaven Fireballs Association does not simply describe an old custom; it helps make the custom happen. In that sense, it is not just a source about folklore but an active carrier of it.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://stonehavenfireballs.com/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: stonehavenfireballs.com">[stonehavenfireballs.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">stonehavenfireballs.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Stonehaven Fireballs</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Stonehaven Fireballs</span></span></span>

## Why Kincardineshire folklore matters

Kincardineshire’s folklore matters because it shows how a small historic county can hold several versions of Scottish tradition at once. It has Pictish stones without turning into a generic Pictland page; saintly wells without becoming only church history; witchcraft memory without needing sensational witch legends; a nationally resonant castle without losing its local setting; and a living fire custom that is both traditional and modern.

The county’s strongest stories are tied to real places:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **Dunnottar** gives the county its cliff-edge theatre: hidden regalia, imprisonment, sacred ground, wells and witch-pool memory.
* **Fordoun** gathers Palladius tradition, a holy well, a fair, a chapel and the Fordoun Pictish cross-slab.
* **Fetteresso** shows medieval pilgrimage and miracle belief in the landscape just west of Stonehaven.
* **Stonehaven** keeps seasonal folklore visible through the Fireballs Ceremony.
* **The Mearns countryside** supplies the agricultural and parish world in which ballads, brownies, charms and witchcraft fears could circulate.

</div>

The most honest reading is not that Kincardineshire is packed with famous monsters. It is a county where folklore clings to edges: the edge of the sea, the edge of the year, the edge between pagan-looking stones and Christian explanation, the edge between healing water and judicial ordeal, and the edge between oral tradition and printed local poetry.
