## Introduction

Black Annis is Leicester’s great bogeywoman: a blue-faced, claw-handed hag said to haunt the Dane Hills on the west side of the city, where she lived in a cave called Black Annis’ Bower and frightened children away from dangerous ground. She is not simply a generic witch dropped into [Leicestershire]({{ 'where-leicestershire-turns-history-into/' | relative_url }}). The tradition is unusually place-bound, tied to a named cave, an oak tree, an old Easter gathering ground, Leicester Castle stories, and printed accounts that can be traced from eighteenth-century land records through nineteenth-century folklore collection. The strongest evidence does not prove an ancient goddess or a real child-eating monster. It shows something more locally revealing: how a rough city-edge landscape became a theatre of warning, fear, seasonal custom and later literary reinvention.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.storyofleicester.info/faith-belief/black-annis-and-dane-hills/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: storyofleicester.info">[storyofleicester.info]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">storyofleicester.info</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Black Annis</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Story of Leicester...</span></span></span>

For Leicestershire folklore, Black Annis matters because she is both vivid and testable. The cave has disappeared into modern Leicester, but the old sources preserve enough detail to show how the story worked: parents used her name to frighten children, poets made her more monstrous, antiquarians tried to explain her origins, and modern Leicester still recognises her as one of the county’s signature supernatural figures.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.storyofleicester.info/faith-belief/black-annis-and-dane-hills/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: storyofleicester.info">[storyofleicester.info]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">storyofleicester.info</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Black Annis</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Story of Leicester...</span></span></span>

## The Dane Hills cave and local landscape

The heart of the Black Annis tradition is the Dane Hills, historically on the western side of Leicester, between the city and the direction of Glenfield. Story of Leicester, the city’s heritage resource, places the figure in a cave in the Dane Hills and notes that the area’s earliest written mention is from 1689. It also records that the Dane Hills were associated with Easter Monday fairs and gatherings from the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries into the early twentieth century.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.storyofleicester.info/faith-belief/black-annis-and-dane-hills/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: storyofleicester.info">[storyofleicester.info]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">storyofleicester.info</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Black Annis</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Story of Leicester...</span></span></span>

That landscape is crucial. Black Annis belongs to a threshold zone: not deep countryside, not the civic centre, but the rough edge where town, fields, tracks and old earthworks met. A 1907 description cited by Story of Leicester gives the Dane Hills a striking physical character, with entrenchments, sunken roads and ridges. This is exactly the sort of terrain where folklore often gathers: uneven ground that looks older than ordinary fields, close enough for children to reach, yet wild enough to be treated as unsafe.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.storyofleicester.info/faith-belief/black-annis-and-dane-hills/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: storyofleicester.info">[storyofleicester.info]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">storyofleicester.info</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Black Annis</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Story of Leicester...</span></span></span>

The nineteenth-century folklore collector Charles James Billson preserved a much fuller account in *County Folk-Lore: Leicestershire and Rutland*, published by the Folklore Society in 1895. In that collection, the cave is described as a round hollow excavated from sandstone, roughly ten or twelve feet across and about five feet high, on the side of one of the Dane Hills knolls. The same passage says the cave was known as Black Annis’s Bower and that local talk made her a savage woman with great teeth and long nails who devoured human victims.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/stream/countyfolklore00folkuoft/countyfolklore00folkuoft_djvu.txt" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Internet Archive Full text of &quot;County folklore</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Internet Archive Full text of &quot;County folklore</span></span></span>

This physicality makes the legend memorable. The bower was not just “somewhere in Leicestershire”; it was a named place people could point towards. Its cave, tree, rocks and approach from Leicester formed a local map of danger. In John Heyrick’s late eighteenth-century poem, the path runs from Glenfield towards Leicester’s old walls, and the bower sits in a glen under an oak, screened by thorny bushes. Even allowing for poetic exaggeration, the setting shows how the legend drew power from a recognisable route and a particular piece of ground.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/stream/countyfolklore00folkuoft/countyfolklore00folkuoft_djvu.txt" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Internet Archive Full text of &quot;County folklore</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Internet Archive Full text of &quot;County folklore</span></span></span>

The cave’s loss has also become part of the tradition. Modern accounts generally treat Black Annis’s Bower as built over or no longer accessible, while Story of Leicester presents the place through historical description, map imagery and an 1870 view from the Dane Hills rather than a surviving site visit. The result is a folklore landmark that now exists more strongly in memory, archives and retellings than in the visible landscape.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.storyofleicester.info/faith-belief/black-annis-and-dane-hills/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: storyofleicester.info">[storyofleicester.info]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">storyofleicester.info</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Black Annis</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Story of Leicester...</span></span></span>

## What the story says Black Annis did

The familiar Black Annis is a night-roaming hag with blue or dark features, iron-like claws, a taste for children and lambs, and a lair beneath an oak. Her terror lies in the way the story moves between the open hillside and the home. She waits outdoors, but she can also reach into cottages; she belongs to the wild edge, but the fear she creates is domestic and parental.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_Annis" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Black Annis</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Black Annis</span></span></span>

Billson’s collected material preserves the child-warning form very clearly. A Leicester Chronicle account from 1874, reprinted in the 1895 collection, says that children who went to run on the Dane Hills were told Black Anna lay in wait to snatch them to her bower, where she would claw them, suck their blood and hang their skins to dry. This is brutal imagery, but its function is familiar in bogey traditions: the monster gives a frightening face to adult warnings about wandering, trespass, quarries, caves, rough ground and nightfall.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/stream/countyfolklore00folkuoft/countyfolklore00folkuoft_djvu.txt" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Internet Archive Full text of &quot;County folklore</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Internet Archive Full text of &quot;County folklore</span></span></span>

Later summaries add details that make her especially suited to oral warning. She was said to grind her teeth so loudly that people could hear her coming; cottages were supposedly built with small windows so she could only get one arm inside; and householders fastened openings or used protective herbs when she howled. These details should not be read as architectural history without proof. They are better understood as explanatory folklore: a story that turns ordinary features of older cottages, night noises and household precautions into evidence of a feared local being.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_Annis" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Black Annis</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Black Annis</span></span></span>

Black Annis also appears under related names, including Black Anna, Black Agnes and Cat Anna. The Cat Anna form links her to Leicester Castle folklore. Billson recorded a report from Miss Henrietta Ellis that warehouse girls in Leicester described Cat Anna as a witch living in the cellars under the castle, with an underground passage running to the Dane Hills. Story of Leicester also notes a version in which Black Annis’s cave was connected to Leicester Castle by a tunnel and she waited near Rupert’s Gateway.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/stream/countyfolklore00folkuoft/countyfolklore00folkuoft_djvu.txt" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Internet Archive Full text of &quot;County folklore</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Internet Archive Full text of &quot;County folklore</span></span></span>

Those castle-tunnel stories matter because they pull Black Annis into the city. She is not confined to a rural hill outside Leicester; she can move below the streets, under elite buildings and through imagined hidden passages. That makes her a Leicester bogeywoman rather than merely a countryside witch. She joins a wider British pattern of underground-passage legends, but with a very local route: Dane Hills to Leicester Castle.

## From warning tale to printed monster

The oldest firm evidence is not a full narrative but a place-name. Story of Leicester notes a 1764 legal reference to land known as “Black Anny’s Bower Close”, and Billson’s 1895 collection likewise refers to eighteenth-century title deeds using that name. This shows that “Black Anny’s Bower” was established as a local place-name by the mid-eighteenth century, even if the story behind it may be older.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.storyofleicester.info/faith-belief/black-annis-and-dane-hills/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: storyofleicester.info">[storyofleicester.info]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">storyofleicester.info</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Black Annis</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Story of Leicester...</span></span></span>

The most influential literary fixing of the legend came in 1797 with John Heyrick’s poem, usually titled “On a Cave Called Black Annis’s Bower”. The poem does not merely record a monster; it heightens her into Gothic spectacle. It gives her livid blue features, talons instead of hands, a waist wrapped in human skins, and a cave with rooms scooped out beneath the rock. Story of Leicester singles out the line about “vast talons” as central to her frightening image, while Billson prints the poem as a key early account.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.storyofleicester.info/faith-belief/black-annis-and-dane-hills/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: storyofleicester.info">[storyofleicester.info]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">storyofleicester.info</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Black Annis</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Story of Leicester...</span></span></span>

That printed version shaped later Black Annis. It took what may have been a local warning tale and gave it a memorable literary body: blue face, claws, gore, cave, oak, shepherds and missing children. This does not make Heyrick the inventor of the whole tradition. The 1764 place-name suggests the bower already mattered, and the 1874 Leicester Chronicle account points to continuing child-warning use. But Heyrick’s poem helped decide what Black Annis looked like when the tradition moved from local talk into books.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://brombonesbooks.com/2023/04/19/shape-shifting-into-a-witch-the-transformation-of-black-annis/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: brombonesbooks.com">[BROM BONES BOOKS]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">brombonesbooks.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">BROM BONES BOOKSTurned into a Witch: The Transformation of Black Annis</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">BROM BONES BOOKSTurned into a Witch: The Transformation of Black Annis</span></span></span>

The legend also entered the theatre. Billson notes that in December 1837 a play called *Black Anna’s Bower, or the Maniac of the Dane Hills* was performed at Leicester Theatre. Its plot involved the famous Blue Boar Inn murder story, with Black Anna playing a role compared with the witches in *Macbeth*. That stage version shows how quickly a place-bound bogey could be folded into melodrama, crime memory and civic entertainment.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/stream/countyfolklore00folkuoft/countyfolklore00folkuoft_djvu.txt" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Internet Archive Full text of &quot;County folklore</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Internet Archive Full text of &quot;County folklore</span></span></span>

By the late nineteenth century, Black Annis had become a layered figure: part oral warning, part poetic monster, part antiquarian puzzle, part Leicester stage property. That layering is why modern readers often meet contradictory versions. Some retellings emphasise the cave and children; others the castle tunnel; others the cat form; others an ancient goddess. The contradictions are not just mistakes. They are evidence of a tradition repeatedly adapted to new audiences.

## The Easter custom and “Black Monday”

One of the strangest pieces of the Black Annis cluster is not the child-eating hag but the Easter Monday custom associated with Black-Annis’-Bower Close. Billson records that Leicester’s mayor and civic officers once went in formal dress to a close called Black-Annis’-Bower Close, bordering or forming part of Leicester Forest, to watch a hunting diversion in which a cat was trailed before hounds.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/stream/countyfolklore00folkuoft/countyfolklore00folkuoft_djvu.txt" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Internet Archive Full text of &quot;County folklore</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Internet Archive Full text of &quot;County folklore</span></span></span>

This custom is often treated cautiously because it is difficult to interpret. It may preserve an old seasonal sport, a rough civic outing, a parody hunt, or an Easter Monday gathering later drawn into the Black Annis legend. Billson’s collection also notes that the Dane-hill fair was still crowded with young working-class visitors in 1842 and that the day was called Black Monday.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/stream/countyfolklore00folkuoft/countyfolklore00folkuoft_djvu.txt" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Internet Archive Full text of &quot;County folklore</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Internet Archive Full text of &quot;County folklore</span></span></span>

The cat detail has encouraged modern writers to connect Black Annis with Cat Anna. That connection is tempting, but it should not be overstated. What can be said safely is that the same local complex includes the bower place-name, the Dane Hills Easter gatherings, the cat-trailing custom, and the later Cat Anna castle-cellar story. Together, they show how Black Annis sat at the junction of seasonal custom, animal symbolism, civic ritual and child-frightening tale.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/stream/countyfolklore00folkuoft/countyfolklore00folkuoft_djvu.txt" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Internet Archive Full text of &quot;County folklore</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Internet Archive Full text of &quot;County folklore</span></span></span>

For a Leicestershire reader, this is one of the most valuable parts of the evidence. Black Annis was not only a bedtime threat. Her name was attached to land, a public holiday route, a fair-going landscape and an old civic performance. That gives her more depth than many printed bogeys whose only evidence is a single literary mention.

## Agnes Scott, goddess theories and cautious readings

The hardest question is where Black Annis came from. The honest answer is that no single origin theory is proven. The evidence supports a local tradition by the eighteenth century, a powerful printed version in 1797, and several nineteenth-century attempts to explain her. It does not securely carry the figure back to prehistoric religion.

One older explanation links Black Annis with Agnes Scott, a medieval religious woman associated with Swithland. Billson’s collection reprints material connecting an Agnes Scott memorial with a cave near Leicester called Black Agnes’s Bower, and later discussion has treated Agnes as an anchoress or religious woman whose memory may have been altered by time, Reformation hostility or local storytelling.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/stream/countyfolklore00folkuoft/countyfolklore00folkuoft_djvu.txt" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Internet Archive Full text of &quot;County folklore</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Internet Archive Full text of &quot;County folklore</span></span></span>

Ronald Hutton’s discussion in *The Triumph of the Moon* is important because it pushes against grand pagan-origin claims while still allowing that a real local religious memory may have contributed to the legend. Modern summaries of Hutton’s view describe Black Annis as possibly developing from Agnes Scott, later distorted into an ogress or bogeywoman, while Victorian and twentieth-century writers confused or expanded the figure through goddess comparisons.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://crowspirit.org.uk/who-is-black-annis/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: crowspirit.org.uk">[crowspirit.org.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">crowspirit.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Who is Black Annis</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Who is Black Annis</span></span></span>

The goddess theories are more speculative. Some writers have linked Black Annis with Anu, Danu, Kali, the Cailleach, Demeter or broad “dark mother” figures. Billson’s own source material includes nineteenth-century antiquarian speculation comparing Black Annis with distant divine figures and even imagining ancient cannibal rites near the cavern. Those claims tell us a great deal about Victorian and early modern habits of explanation, but they are weak evidence for the actual origin of the Leicester story.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/stream/countyfolklore00folkuoft/countyfolklore00folkuoft_djvu.txt" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Internet Archive Full text of &quot;County folklore</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Internet Archive Full text of &quot;County folklore</span></span></span>

A cautious reading is more useful. Black Annis may have grown from several overlapping elements: a real cave, a frightening local place-name, warnings to children, rough seasonal customs, memories or misunderstandings of a religious woman, and literary Gothic colouring. That mixed origin is less dramatic than “ancient goddess survives in Leicester”, but it fits the evidence better. It also makes the tradition more interesting, because it shows folklore forming through accumulation rather than arriving fully made from a single source.

## Why Leicester kept telling the story

Black Annis survived because she did useful cultural work. She made danger memorable. A cave in soft rock, rough hills, dusk, stray animals, working tracks, fairs and the edge of town all became easier to talk about through one terrifying figure. The story gave children a reason not to wander and adults a local shorthand for fear.

She also gave Leicester a monster with an address. Many British bogey figures are vague: they lurk in ponds, lanes, woods or chimneys. Black Annis has the Dane Hills, the bower, the oak, the road from Glenfield, the castle tunnel and Rupert’s Gateway. That density of place helps explain why she remains Leicestershire’s best-known supernatural figure.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.storyofleicester.info/faith-belief/black-annis-and-dane-hills/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: storyofleicester.info">[storyofleicester.info]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">storyofleicester.info</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Black Annis</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Story of Leicester...</span></span></span>

The tradition also speaks to Leicester’s changing edge. The Dane Hills moved from rough open ground and fair-going landscape into mapped, developed urban space. As the physical bower disappeared, the story became more archival and imaginative. People no longer needed to fear the cave as a real place on a child’s walk, but the lost cave became part of the appeal: a vanished landmark under modern streets and houses.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.storyofleicester.info/faith-belief/black-annis-and-dane-hills/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: storyofleicester.info">[storyofleicester.info]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">storyofleicester.info</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Black Annis</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Story of Leicester...</span></span></span>

Modern retellings keep adapting her. Local heritage pages present her as part of Leicester’s historical imagination; folklore blogs and books retell her as a witch, hag or ogress; and community performance groups have borrowed her name and imagery. Black Annis Morris, for example, uses the legend in a local performance identity, describing Black Annis as a Dane Hills witch said to lure and devour children.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.storyofleicester.info/faith-belief/black-annis-and-dane-hills/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: storyofleicester.info">[storyofleicester.info]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">storyofleicester.info</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Black Annis</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Story of Leicester...</span></span></span>

That modern afterlife should be read as folklore too, not as contamination of a pure original. Black Annis has always changed with the medium: oral warning, legal place-name, poem, theatre, antiquarian collection, heritage website, seasonal performance, horror retelling. What remains stable is her role as Leicester’s city-edge hag.

## What is well-attested, and what should be treated carefully

The Black Annis tradition is strong by folklore standards, but not every claim about her is equally secure. The best-attested points are the local place-name, the Dane Hills setting, the association with a cave or bower, the child-warning function, the 1797 Heyrick poem, and the nineteenth-century collection of related variants. These are supported by local heritage material and Billson’s Folklore Society compilation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.storyofleicester.info/faith-belief/black-annis-and-dane-hills/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: storyofleicester.info">[storyofleicester.info]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">storyofleicester.info</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Black Annis</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Story of Leicester...</span></span></span>

More cautiously handled points include the exact origin of her name, the historical reality of Agnes Scott’s connection to the bower, and the meaning of the Easter Monday cat-trailing custom. These may be part of the tradition’s development, but they do not provide a simple origin story.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/stream/countyfolklore00folkuoft/countyfolklore00folkuoft_djvu.txt" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Internet Archive Full text of &quot;County folklore</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Internet Archive Full text of &quot;County folklore</span></span></span>

The weakest claims are the sweeping prehistoric-goddess readings. They are part of Black Annis’s modern interpretive history and should not be erased, because many readers will encounter them. But they are best presented as later speculation rather than established Leicestershire tradition. Hutton’s sceptical handling of such mythic genealogies is a useful guardrail: the local evidence is strong enough without needing to turn Black Annis into a surviving pan-European goddess.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://crowspirit.org.uk/who-is-black-annis/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: crowspirit.org.uk">[crowspirit.org.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">crowspirit.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Who is Black Annis</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Who is Black Annis</span></span></span>

Black Annis endures because she is specific. She is not just “a witch from Leicester”, but a figure made from the Dane Hills, a cave, an oak, frightened children, Easter gatherings, castle rumours, printed verse and local argument. That combination makes her one of the clearest examples of how Leicestershire folklore binds supernatural fear to real ground.

    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-selected-market-flag], [data-ebay-trigger-market-flag]')).forEach(function (flag) {
      flag.className = flagClass(marketId);
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-trigger-market-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = selectedText;
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-domain-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = domainLabel(marketId);
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-option]')).forEach(function (button) {
      button.setAttribute('aria-selected', button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-option') === marketId ? 'true' : 'false');
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      var active = button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button') === marketId;
      button.setAttribute('aria-pressed', active ? 'true' : 'false');
    });
    section.setAttribute('data-ebay-selected-market', marketId);
    if (persist) {
      try { if (window.localStorage) localStorage.setItem('phoenix-ebay-market', marketId); } catch (err) {}
    }
    closeMenu(section);
  }

    if (section.getAttribute('data-ebay-localized-init') === '1') return;
    section.setAttribute('data-ebay-localized-init', '1');

    var marketId = defaultMarket;
    if (window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation && window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation.inferMarket) {
      marketId = window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation.inferMarket(available, {
        storageKey: 'phoenix-ebay-market',

      });

      marketId = available[0] || defaultMarket;
    }
    var select = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-select]');
    if (select) {
      select.addEventListener('change', function () {
        applyMarket(section, select.value, true);
      });

    var trigger = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-trigger]');
    var menu = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-menu]');
    if (trigger && menu) {
      trigger.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
        event.stopPropagation();
        var expanded = trigger.getAttribute('aria-expanded') === 'true';
        menu.hidden = expanded;
        trigger.setAttribute('aria-expanded', expanded ? 'false' : 'true');
        var picker = trigger.closest('.fr-ebay-market-picker');
        if (picker) picker.classList.toggle('fr-ebay-market-picker--open', !expanded);
      });
      Array.prototype.slice.call(menu.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-option]')).forEach(function (button) {
        button.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
          event.stopPropagation();
          applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-option'), true);
        });
      });
      document.addEventListener('click', function () { closeMenu(section); });
    }
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      button.addEventListener('click', function () {
        applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button'), true);
      });
    });
    applyMarket(section, marketId, false);
  });
