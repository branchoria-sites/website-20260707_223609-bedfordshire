## Introduction

Bodfari’s red-clad elf dancers are one of the most striking pieces of fairy evidence attached to historic [Flintshire]({{ 'where-flintshires-strange-stories-still/' | relative_url }}): a named field, named witnesses, a dated childhood encounter, and later collectors arguing over what the witnesses had actually seen. The core story says that, around midsummer in 1757, young Edward Williams and three girls saw a small company of red-dressed beings dancing in Cae Caled near Bodfari. One broke away and chased the children to a stile, but did not cross it. Williams, who became a respected Independent minister and tutor, later treated the incident not as a joke but as one of his “unknowables”.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/files/20096/20096.txt" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Project Gutenbergwww.gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Project Gutenbergwww.gutenberg.org</span></span></span>



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/Flintshire_823310_bodfari_elf_dancers_229586-overview.webp" | relative_url }}" alt="Overview image for Elf Dancers" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

The tale matters because it is not just another vague “fairy hill” memory. It shows how Flintshire fairy tradition was preserved, reclassified, doubted, misquoted and partly forgotten. Bodfari itself sits awkwardly across old county lines: the ancient parish included Bodfari in historic Flintshire and Aberwheeler in historic Denbighshire, while today the area is in Denbighshire. That boundary confusion is part of the story’s afterlife, but within a historic-county reading it belongs naturally in the Flintshire folklore map.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.clwydfhs.org.uk/en/miscellanea/ancient-flintshire-parishes" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: clwydfhs.org.uk">[Clwyd Family History]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">clwydfhs.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Clwyd Family History Ancient Flintshire Parishes &#124; Clwyd Family History</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Clwyd Family History Ancient Flintshire Parishes &#124; Clwyd Family History</span></span></span>

## The Cae Caled encounter

The fullest printed version was published by Elias Owen in *Welsh Folk-Lore* in the 1890s, using an account attributed to Dr Edward Williams. Owen introduces Williams as a man born at Glan Clwyd, Bodfari, in 1750, later connected with Oswestry and Rotherham, and says the passage came from Williams’s own handwriting.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/files/20096/20096.txt" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Project Gutenbergwww.gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Project Gutenbergwww.gutenberg.org</span></span></span>

Williams placed the incident in 1757. He described himself, his eldest sister, and two neighbouring girls, Barbara and Ann Evans, playing in a field called Cae Caled near their neighbours’ house. The time is unusually precise for a fairy story: a fine summer day, about midsummer, between noon and one o’clock. The children were by a hedge under a tree, close to a stile, when one of them noticed figures dancing in the middle of the field. Williams did not simply call them fairies. His hesitation is part of the power of the account: he called them “beings” and said they seemed neither men, women nor children.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/files/20096/20096.txt" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Project Gutenbergwww.gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Project Gutenbergwww.gutenberg.org</span></span></span>

The visual details are what make the Bodfari story memorable. Williams said the dancers were less than a hundred yards away, perhaps seven or eight couples, moving so quickly that the children could not count them properly. They were dressed uniformly in red, with red headcloths spotted or sprigged with yellow, and white handkerchiefs held loose in their hands. Their size was odd: smaller than the children’s own, but more like dwarfs than children.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/files/20096/20096.txt" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Project Gutenbergwww.gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Project Gutenbergwww.gutenberg.org</span></span></span>

The encounter then turns from spectacle to pursuit. The children wondered whether the figures might be soldiers or May dancers, but rejected both ideas. There were no soldiers in the district, Williams said, and it was not the season for such dancers. As the children made for the stile, one figure left the dance and ran towards them. Williams, the youngest, was last over. He remembered the pursuing figure vividly: a “grim elf” with a dark, aged, frightening face. The being reached the stile and leaned across it, but did not come over. The children ran to the house, raised the alarm, and adults searched the neighbourhood without finding any trace of an explanation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/files/20096/20096.txt" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Project Gutenbergwww.gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Project Gutenbergwww.gutenberg.org</span></span></span>

For folklore readers, the stile is one of the most important details. It makes the story feel like a boundary tale as much as a sighting tale. The dancers occupy the field; the children cross a human threshold; the pursuer stops at the edge. Whether read as remembered terror, fairy narrative pattern, local rumour, or misinterpreted human activity, the drama depends on that small piece of rural infrastructure.

## Fairies, knockers and collector caution

The Bodfari dancers became evidence not because everyone agreed about them, but because collectors disagreed. Wirt Sikes, the American-born writer and United States consul for Wales, included the Cae Caled figures in *British Goblins* in 1880 under his discussion of Welsh fairy types. He treated them as “coblynau”, or mine fairies, and called the witness “Egbert Williams”, a mistake later writers have noticed. In Sikes’s retelling, the dancers are “fifteen or sixteen coblynau”, dancing like Morris dancers and dressed in red like British soldiers.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/files/34704/34704-h/34704-h.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Project Gutenberg eBook of British Goblins: Welsh Folk-lore, Fairy Mythology, Legends and Traditions, by Wirt Sikes...</span></span></span>

Elias Owen pushed back. He explicitly said Sikes was not justified in classifying the Cae Caled beings as knockers. That matters because “knockers” belong mainly to mining folklore: underground spirits associated with mines, tapping noises and hidden ore. The Bodfari figures, by contrast, appear in an open field at noon, dancing in coordinated dress, with no mine setting in the witness account. Owen’s correction is a useful warning against treating every small Welsh supernatural being as the same kind of creature.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/files/20096/20096.txt" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Project Gutenbergwww.gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Project Gutenbergwww.gutenberg.org</span></span></span>

The case also shows how a folklore item can shift as it passes from witness to collector to popular retelling. In Owen’s version, Williams names four child witnesses and stresses repeated later questioning. He says all four had “uniformly and often” attested the fact, under separate examination. That is not proof of fairies, but it is evidence of a durable family or neighbourhood memory: the event was apparently not a single throwaway anecdote told once by a frightened child.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/files/20096/20096.txt" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Project Gutenbergwww.gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Project Gutenbergwww.gutenberg.org</span></span></span>

A modern source file prepared by Simon Young notes that there were two versions of the encounter: an earlier version associated with Edmund Jones’s apparition-collecting tradition, and the later autobiographical version printed by Owen. Young also flags a problem with Owen’s claim that Williams’s account had appeared in a published autobiography: he found no trace of such a work, though the story was previewed in *Bye-Gones* in 1891 before Owen’s fuller publication.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.academia.edu/53238330/Source_File_The_Elf_Dancers_of_Cae_Caled" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: academia.edu">[Academia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">academia.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">PDF) Source File: The Elf Dancers of Cae Caled</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">PDF) Source File: The Elf Dancers of Cae Caled</span></span></span>

This is exactly the kind of caution a good county folklore page needs. The Cae Caled story is unusually concrete, but not simple. It is strong as a recorded tradition because it has a named witness, a dated setting, a named field and collector commentary. It is weaker if treated as literal evidence for a supernatural species, because every printed form has passed through memory, editing, classification and antiquarian interpretation.

## Why the story nearly vanished

One of the strangest parts of the Bodfari case is not the dancers but the silence around them. After printing Williams’s account, Owen says that R. Prys Jones, a schoolmaster at Denbigh, read the story to intelligent boys from Bodfari and none of them had heard it. Owen found this surprising: the story had “died so soon” in the neighbourhood where it began.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/files/20096/20096.txt" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Project Gutenbergwww.gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Project Gutenbergwww.gutenberg.org</span></span></span>

That small comment is important. Many local legends survive because they attach themselves to a visible public landmark: a well, a stone, a castle, a haunted road, a churchyard. Cae Caled was different. It was a field-name and a household memory, not a civic shrine. If the family moved, the field-name changed, or the printed book failed to circulate locally, the story could drop out of ordinary village storytelling even while surviving in folklore collections.

Place has not vanished entirely, however. Modern local retellings connect Cae Caled with the Glan Clwyd Isa area near Bodfari. The present Glan Clwyd Isa site describes itself as rural accommodation in the Vale of Clwyd and includes a property called Cae Caled, while local folklore writing places the remembered field in the wider Glan Clwyd landscape rather than in a generic “Bodfari” setting.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://glanclwydisa.co.uk/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: glanclwydisa.co.uk">[Glan clwyd isa]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">glanclwydisa.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on glanclwydisa.co.uk.</span></span></span>

This creates a revealing contrast. The story nearly disappeared from oral local memory in the nineteenth century, yet it now circulates online because antiquarian print and modern folklore blogs have made it searchable again. That is not the same as an unbroken living tradition. It is a revived, source-led tradition: a local legend that returned through books, databases, podcasts and heritage curiosity.

## What the Bodfari evidence can and cannot prove

The Cae Caled case is valuable because it gives readers a rare look at how fairy evidence works in practice. It is not laboratory evidence, and it should not be presented as proof that red-clad elves once danced near Bodfari. It is evidence of a remembered encounter, recorded by a named man who became a serious religious and educational figure, then preserved and debated by folklore collectors.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/files/20096/20096.txt" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Project Gutenbergwww.gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Project Gutenbergwww.gutenberg.org</span></span></span>

Several features make the account unusually strong as folklore evidence:

* **Named witness:** Edward Williams is identifiable beyond the tale. The Dictionary of Welsh Biography records him as born at Glanclwyd between Denbigh and Bodfari in 1750, later becoming an Independent divine and tutor.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://biography.wales/article/s-WILL-EDW-1750" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: biography.wales">[Dictionary of Welsh Biography]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">biography.wales</span><span class="citation-popover-title">s WILL EDW 1750</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">s WILL EDW 1750</span></span></span>

<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **Named place:** Cae Caled is not a vague fairy realm but a field associated with Bodfari and Glan Clwyd.
* **Multiple child witnesses:** Williams’s account says four children saw the figures, and that the event was repeatedly attested by the parties afterwards.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/files/20096/20096.txt" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Project Gutenbergwww.gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Project Gutenbergwww.gutenberg.org</span></span></span>
* **Collector disagreement:** Sikes, Owen and later commentators did not simply copy one another; they argued over classification, wording and source reliability.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/files/34704/34704-h/34704-h.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Project Gutenberg eBook of British Goblins: Welsh Folk-lore, Fairy Mythology, Legends and Traditions, by Wirt Sikes...</span></span></span>

</div>

The weaknesses are just as important. The most famous version was printed long after the alleged 1757 event. Some details differ between retellings, including names and costume features. Sikes’s version reshapes the beings as coblynau and gives them a mining-fairy label that Owen rejects. Modern commentary has also questioned whether all of Owen’s source claims can be verified.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.strangehistory.net/2021/09/29/the-scariest-british-fairy-encounter-the-elf-dancers-of-cae-caled/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: strangehistory.net">[Beachcombing&#x27;s Bizarre History Blog]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">strangehistory.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on strangehistory.net.</span></span></span>

So the honest reading is not “this proves fairies”. It is: this is one of Flintshire’s clearest examples of a supernatural encounter becoming folklore evidence. It preserves how a witness framed the inexplicable, how collectors tried to classify it, and how local memory could fail even when print preserved the tale.

## Why Bodfari belongs in Flintshire fairy tradition

Bodfari can look confusing on a modern map because it is now in Denbighshire. For this project’s historic-county frame, the confusion is part of the evidence rather than a reason to exclude the tale. Clwyd Family History Society lists Bodfari among ancient Flintshire parishes and notes that the historic county bears little resemblance to the modern county formed in 1996. Its Bodfari church page is even clearer: St Stephen’s had Flintshire as its pre-1974 county, and the ancient parish comprised Bodfari in historic Flintshire and Aberwheeler in historic Denbighshire.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.clwydfhs.org.uk/en/miscellanea/ancient-flintshire-parishes" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: clwydfhs.org.uk">[Clwyd Family History]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">clwydfhs.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Clwyd Family History Ancient Flintshire Parishes &#124; Clwyd Family History</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Clwyd Family History Ancient Flintshire Parishes &#124; Clwyd Family History</span></span></span>

That mixed geography suits the story. The Cae Caled dancers belong to a borderland landscape of fields, lanes, stiles, parish edges and shifting county labels. They are not attached to Flint Castle, Mold, Holywell or another obvious county landmark. Instead, they show the quieter side of Flintshire folklore: domestic, rural, half-remembered and preserved because one witness later wrote down what frightened him as a child.

The story also helps balance Flintshire’s better-known folklore profile. St Winefride’s Well gives the county a major saintly and healing legend; witchcraft records show fear moving through households and courts; black dog and ghost traditions attach to roads and estates. Bodfari adds something different: a daylight fairy encounter with an unusually visual costume, a pursuit, and a later debate about what kind of beings the witnesses thought they had seen.

## How the elf dancers are retold today

Modern retellings often emphasise the story’s cinematic quality: the midday field, the red uniforms, the white handkerchiefs, the sudden chase and the figure stopped at the stile. Some online writers present it as one of the eeriest British fairy encounters; others focus on source criticism, noting that Sikes, Owen and Edmund Jones preserve related but not identical forms of the story.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.strangehistory.net/2021/09/29/the-scariest-british-fairy-encounter-the-elf-dancers-of-cae-caled/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: strangehistory.net">[Beachcombing&#x27;s Bizarre History Blog]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">strangehistory.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on strangehistory.net.</span></span></span>

The most responsible modern retelling keeps three things in view at once. First, the witness account is unusually detailed and deserves to be treated seriously as a piece of remembered belief culture. Second, the differences between versions matter: they show how folklore changes as it is copied, categorised and made more readable. Third, the story’s near-loss in local memory is not a failure; it is part of what makes the case so revealing.

Bodfari’s red-clad dancers therefore stand as more than a strange fairy anecdote. They are a compact lesson in Flintshire folklore evidence: a vivid tale rooted in a named landscape, preserved through print rather than continuous village memory, and still unsettled enough to make readers ask the right questions about witness, place, classification and survival.
