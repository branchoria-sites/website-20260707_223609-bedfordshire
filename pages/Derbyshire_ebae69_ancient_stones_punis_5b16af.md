## Introduction

[Derbyshire]({{ 'where-derbyshire-turns-landscape-into/' | relative_url }})’s ancient stones became stories because their original meanings were already mysterious by the time local people began naming, visiting and explaining them. On Stanton Moor, the Nine Ladies are said to be women turned to stone for dancing on a Sunday, with the nearby King Stone as their fiddler. At Arbor Low, the drama lies less in one neat legend than in the sight of a great henge and fallen limestone circle whose purpose is still not fully understood. At Hob Hurst’s House, a prehistoric barrow carries the name of a goblin, turning a burial mound into a place where archaeology and local supernatural naming meet. Together, these sites show a distinct Derbyshire pattern: prehistoric monuments survive as visible features in the Peak landscape, while later folklore gives them moral lessons, goblin owners, fairy-like associations and modern meanings. English Heritage identifies Nine Ladies as an early Bronze Age stone circle, Arbor Low as a major Neolithic henge, and Hob Hurst’s House as a prehistoric burial mound named after a local goblin.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.english-heritage.org.uk/visit/places/nine-ladies-stone-circle/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: english-heritage.org.uk">[English Heritage]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">english-heritage.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">English HeritageNine Ladies Stone CircleA small early Bronze Age stone circle traditionally believed to depict nine ladies turned to ston...</span></span></span>

## Why stones invite punishment stories

The basic problem for later communities was simple: the stones were plainly old, carefully placed and not obviously useful. Before modern archaeology, people did not have secure dates, excavation reports or heritage interpretation panels. They had striking shapes in the landscape and a need to explain them. Folklore supplied answers that were memorable, moral and local.

One of the commonest British explanations for stone circles is petrification: human beings break a religious or social rule, then become stones. The Derbyshire version at Nine Ladies belongs to this wider family of tales in which dancers, musicians or wedding parties are punished for dancing on the Sabbath. A scholarly article on the “Merry Maidens” motif describes the Sabbatarian belief as generally attached to stone circles, with maidens petrified for dancing on the Sabbath; Oxford Reference’s entry on Stanton Drew likewise notes similar legends in which dancers cannot stop and are turned to stone.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.jstor.org/stable/1260278" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: jstor.org">[JSTOR]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">jstor.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The &#x27;Merry Maidens&#x27; and the &#x27;Noce de Pierre</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The &#x27;Merry Maidens&#x27; and the &#x27;Noce de Pierre</span></span></span>

That does not mean Derbyshire borrowed a single fixed tale from elsewhere. It means local people used a familiar story-shape to make sense of a local monument. The stones were already arranged like figures in a ring; the outlying stone could become a fiddler; Sunday dancing supplied the offence; petrification supplied the punishment. The result is a story that feels rooted in Stanton Moor even while it belongs to a much wider English and British folklore pattern.

## Nine Ladies and the dancers turned to stone

The Nine Ladies stone circle stands on Stanton Moor, near Stanton in Peak and Youlgreave. English Heritage describes it as a small early Bronze Age stone circle, traditionally believed to show nine women turned to stone as punishment for dancing on a Sunday. The same official account explains that the King Stone, standing apart from the circle, is linked in the tradition with the fiddler who played for them.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.english-heritage.org.uk/visit/places/nine-ladies-stone-circle/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: english-heritage.org.uk">[English Heritage]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">english-heritage.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">English HeritageNine Ladies Stone CircleA small early Bronze Age stone circle traditionally believed to depict nine ladies turned to ston...</span></span></span>

The archaeology is more complicated than the name suggests. English Heritage’s history of the site notes that there are ten stones within the circle, because a fallen tenth stone was located in 1977. The stones are set on the inner edge of a slight bank and form a ring measuring about 11.5 by 10.5 metres. The monument was first recorded by Major Hayman Rooke in 1782, who thought there may have been stones in the centre, perhaps part of a small cairn or cist.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.english-heritage.org.uk/visit/places/nine-ladies-stone-circle/history/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: english-heritage.org.uk">[English Heritage]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">english-heritage.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on english-heritage.org.uk.</span></span></span>

That gap between name and archaeology is part of the folklore’s power. “Nine Ladies” is not a neutral survey label. It turns a monument into a cast of characters. Even the mismatch between nine named ladies and ten physical stones has become part of the site’s charm, reminding visitors that folklore often preserves a story-world rather than an exact measured plan.

Stanton Moor itself matters just as much as the circle. Historic England describes the moor as a discrete area of Peak District moorland rich in Bronze Age burial, ceremonial and settlement remains, including field systems, cairnfields, burial monuments, stone circles and later tracks, quarrying and woodland activity. The Nine Ladies are therefore not an isolated curiosity but part of a wider prehistoric landscape that has been reused, quarried, walked, interpreted and reinterpreted for centuries.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://historicengland.org.uk/listing/the-list/list-entry/1009300" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: historicengland.org.uk">[Historic England]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">historicengland.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on historicengland.org.uk.</span></span></span>

A 1998 English Heritage geophysical survey placed the Nine Ladies and King Stone within a “rich archaeological landscape” of Bronze Age burial mounds, ring cairns, field boundaries and clearance cairns. The same report also found that visitor activity had affected the survey results, a useful reminder that beloved folklore sites are not just inherited from the past; they are continually shaped by present-day feet, gatherings and attention.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://historicengland.org.uk/research/results/reports/4823/NINELADIESSTONECIRCLESTANTONMOORDERBYSHIREREPORTONGEOPHYSICALSURVEY1998" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: historicengland.org.uk">[Historic England]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">historicengland.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Historic England Ancient Monuments Laboratory</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Historic England Ancient Monuments Laboratory</span></span></span>

## Arbor Low and prehistoric mystery

Arbor Low, near Monyash and Bakewell, is the grander, stranger counterpoint to the compact Nine Ladies. English Heritage calls it the most important prehistoric site of the East Midlands: a Neolithic henge set amid high moorland, with an earthen bank and ditch enclosing a circle of about 50 white limestone slabs, all now fallen, around a central stone “cove”. Nearby stands Gib Hill, a large burial mound.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.english-heritage.org.uk/visit/places/arbor-low-stone-circle-and-gib-hill-barrow/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: english-heritage.org.uk">[English Heritage]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">english-heritage.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">English HeritageArbor Low Stone Circle and Gib Hill BarrowThe most important prehistoric site of the East Midlands, Arbor Low is a Neolit...</span></span></span>

Unlike Nine Ladies, Arbor Low does not need a tidy punishment legend to feel charged. Its folklore value comes from scale, uncertainty and atmosphere. English Heritage notes that it is sometimes called the “Stonehenge of the North”, partly because it combines henge bank and ditch, stone circle and cove, and bears a passing resemblance, though smaller, to Avebury in Wiltshire.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.english-heritage.org.uk/visit/places/arbor-low-stone-circle-and-gib-hill-barrow/history/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: english-heritage.org.uk">[English Heritage]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">english-heritage.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">English Heritage Arbor Low Stone Circle and Gib Hill Barrow</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">English Heritage Arbor Low Stone Circle and Gib Hill Barrow</span></span></span>

Historic England’s scheduled monument entry gives a more technical view. Arbor Low is a well-preserved Class II henge with a high diversity of features: burials, a linear earthwork, a stone setting, opposed entrances and a later bowl barrow. It also explains that bowl barrows are prehistoric funerary monuments dating from the Late Neolithic to the Late Bronze Age, roughly 2400–1500 BC, and can preserve evidence of burial practice and social organisation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://historicengland.org.uk/listing/the-list/list-entry/1011087" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: historicengland.org.uk">[Historic England]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">historicengland.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on historicengland.org.uk.</span></span></span>

Gib Hill deepens the sense that this was a ritual landscape rather than a single monument. Historic England describes it as a large bowl barrow superimposed on an earlier oval barrow, about 200 metres south-west of Arbor Low, and says it forms part of a rich prehistoric ritual landscape including Arbor Low and other barrows. Its long axis appears to be orientated on the henge.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://historicengland.org.uk/listing/the-list/list-entry/1011088" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: historicengland.org.uk">[Historic England]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">historicengland.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on historicengland.org.uk.</span></span></span>

For folklore, the important point is not that Arbor Low has one famous story equivalent to the Nine Ladies. It is that its archaeological uncertainty leaves room for repeated imaginative use. The fallen stones look deliberate and dramatic even when their original purpose is unknown. The central cove suggests ceremony without explaining it. The barrows nearby keep death in view. Visitors can therefore read the place as sacred, eerie, peaceful, ancient or communal without needing a single canonical legend.

That modern meaning is not just theoretical. In 2025, a Guardian report on an English Heritage and Photoworks exhibition described young artists using Derbyshire stone circles, including Arbor Low and Nine Ladies, to explore connection, belonging and peace. English Heritage prehistorian Jennifer Wexler was quoted as saying that stone circles link past and present, personal histories, community, memory and belonging.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.theguardian.com/culture/2025/feb/14/a-space-for-solace-stonehenge-show-explores-attraction-of-stone-circles" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theguardian.com">[The Guardian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theguardian.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on theguardian.com.</span></span></span>

## Hob Hurst’s House and goblin names

Hob Hurst’s House sits on Beeley Moor, near the Chatsworth side of the Derbyshire Peak. English Heritage describes it as a square prehistoric burial mound with an earthwork ditch and outer bank, named after a local goblin. Historic England’s official listing gives the fuller heritage description: “Hob Hurst’s House: a square, banked and ditched burial cairn with cist on Harland Edge”, scheduled as a nationally important monument.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.english-heritage.org.uk/visit/places/hob-hursts-house/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: english-heritage.org.uk">[English Heritage]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">english-heritage.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on english-heritage.org.uk.</span></span></span>

This is a different kind of folklore from the Nine Ladies. The legend is not a developed moral drama with dancers and a fiddler. Instead, the supernatural survives in the name. “Hob” is a familiar figure in northern and Midlands folklore: a small spirit, goblin or brownie-like being, sometimes helpful, sometimes troublesome. A place called Hob Hurst’s House suggests a mound understood, at least in later naming tradition, as the dwelling or property of such a being.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hob_%28folklore%29" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Hob (folklore</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Hob (folklore</span></span></span>

That naming is revealing. Barrows often invited otherworldly explanations because they looked like artificial hills but belonged to no living household. Buxton Museum’s discussion of Derbyshire ancient-site folklore notes that burial mounds were often imagined as entrances to the otherworld or fairy kingdom, while stone circles and standing stones could be associated with fairies or with people turned to stone.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://buxtonmuseumandartgallery.wordpress.com/2021/12/10/dancing-stones-and-peeing-giants-the-folklore-of-ancient-sites-in-derbyshire/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: buxtonmuseumandartgallery.wordpress.com">[Buxton Museum and Art Gallery]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">buxtonmuseumandartgallery.wordpress.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wordpress.com.</span></span></span>

Hob Hurst’s House therefore shows how a prehistoric burial place could be domesticated by folklore. Calling it a “house” makes the barrow legible: someone lives there, even if that someone is a goblin. The name also softens and sharpens the place at once. It is not merely a grave; it is a dwelling. It is not merely a ruin; it has an owner. That owner is not human.

## What archaeology changes, and what it does not

Modern archaeology does not prove the punishment legends, and it is not supposed to. Its job is to date, describe and interpret the monuments as material remains. Folklore answers a different question: how later people explained and emotionally handled ancient structures whose builders had left no readable story.

At Nine Ladies, archaeology shows a Bronze Age ceremonial and funerary landscape on Stanton Moor; folklore turns the circle into women punished for dancing. At Arbor Low, archaeology shows a Neolithic henge, stone settings, barrows and long-term ritual activity; modern imagination turns it into a “Stonehenge of the North” and a gathering place for reflection. At Hob Hurst’s House, archaeology shows a prehistoric cairn and cist; folklore preserves a goblin-name that makes the mound feel inhabited.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://historicengland.org.uk/listing/the-list/list-entry/1009300" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: historicengland.org.uk">[historicengland.org.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">historicengland.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on historicengland.org.uk.</span></span></span>

The two ways of knowing can clash when folklore is treated as literal prehistory. There is no good reason to think the Nine Ladies were really dancers, or that Hob Hurst was the original builder of the barrow. But folklore becomes more interesting, not less, when read as later cultural memory. It tells us what the monuments became in the minds of people who lived with them: warnings, haunts, homes, sacred places, curiosities and landmarks.

There is also a conservation lesson here. The more famous a legend becomes, the more visitors it attracts. The Nine Ladies geophysical survey noted the impact of intense visitor activity, while the Peak District National Park’s Stanton Moor Management Plan describes Stanton Moor as one of the “jewels” of the Peak District and important to many people for many reasons. Folklore helps protect places by making people care about them, but popularity can also wear them down.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://historicengland.org.uk/research/results/reports/4823/NINELADIESSTONECIRCLESTANTONMOORDERBYSHIREREPORTONGEOPHYSICALSURVEY1998" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: historicengland.org.uk">[Historic England]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">historicengland.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Historic England Ancient Monuments Laboratory</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Historic England Ancient Monuments Laboratory</span></span></span>

## Why these stories matter to Derbyshire

Derbyshire’s stone and barrow legends matter because they connect the county’s folklore to its most recognisable landscapes: gritstone moor, limestone plateau, upland tracks, burial mounds and open views. These are not stories that could be moved anywhere without loss. The Nine Ladies need Stanton Moor. Arbor Low needs the exposed White Peak plateau. Hob Hurst’s House needs the lonely feel of Beeley Moor and Harland Edge.

They also show how Derbyshire folklore often works by layering rather than replacing. A Bronze Age site becomes an antiquarian “Druidical” curiosity, then a protected monument, then a walking destination, then a solstice gathering place, then a subject for artists, photographers and local heritage writing. English Heritage notes that the Nine Ladies were first recorded by Hayman Rooke in 1782; Historic England’s Stanton Moor entry preserves references to antiquarian and archaeological work from the eighteenth century onwards; modern heritage and arts projects continue to reinterpret the stones for new audiences.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.english-heritage.org.uk/visit/places/nine-ladies-stone-circle/history/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: english-heritage.org.uk">[english-heritage.org.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">english-heritage.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on english-heritage.org.uk.</span></span></span>

The punishment legends in particular reveal a Christian moral overlay placed on much older monuments. Dancing, music, women, night-time, Sunday-breaking and the Devil are all familiar ingredients in post-medieval moral storytelling. Yet the tale is not only repressive. Today, many readers and visitors notice the opposite energy: the women are remembered because they danced. The stones become a symbol of forbidden movement, communal rhythm and the stubborn survival of older places beneath later rules.

That is why “Ancient Stones” is such a strong Derbyshire folklore theme. The county’s prehistoric monuments are not simply archaeological remains with stories attached. They are places where different eras meet: Bronze Age builders, eighteenth-century antiquaries, Victorian protection, modern walkers, pagans, artists, conservationists and curious families all bring new meanings to the same weathered stones.

## How the legends are retold today

Today, Derbyshire’s stone legends live in several overlapping forms. Heritage bodies present the sites as protected prehistoric monuments. Local tourism pages use the legends to help visitors remember them. Folklore writers retell the punished dancers and the goblin-name. Walkers and photographers share the atmosphere of the moors. Artists and modern spiritual visitors treat the circles as places of gathering, reflection and belonging.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://visitpeakdistrict.com/business-directory/nine-ladies-stone-circle" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: visitpeakdistrict.com">[visitpeakdistrict.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">visitpeakdistrict.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on visitpeakdistrict.com.</span></span></span>

The best retellings keep two truths in balance. First, these are ancient monuments whose original meanings are partly lost and should not be overwritten by confident fantasy. Second, the later stories are themselves part of Derbyshire’s cultural history. The Nine Ladies legend is not Bronze Age evidence, but it is real folklore. Hob Hurst may not be an archaeological explanation, but the goblin-name is part of the site’s identity. Arbor Low’s mystery should not be filled with invented certainty, yet its continuing emotional pull is part of why people still go there.

Read this way, Derbyshire’s stones are not silent. They speak in layers: earthwork, burial, antiquarian record, local warning, goblin name, walking route, conservation problem and modern place of meaning. Their folklore does not solve the mystery of why they were built. It shows how Derbyshire has kept asking the question.
