## Introduction

The Wookey Hole Witch is [Somerset]({{ 'where-somerset-turns-into-legend/' | relative_url }})’s most famous cave legend: a woman said to have lived in the caverns near Wells, cursed the neighbouring people, and been turned to stone by a monk from [Glastonbury]({{ 'glastonbury/' | relative_url }}). The “witch” visitors are shown today is a roughly human-shaped stalagmite in the first chamber of Wookey Hole Caves, but the power of the story comes from more than a suggestive rock. It gathers together a dangerous underground landscape, medieval Christian authority, early modern fear of harmful magic, antiquarian poetry, archaeology, tourism and real ritual protection marks scratched into the cave walls.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.wookey.co.uk/wookey-hole-witch" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wookey.co.uk">[wookey.co.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wookey.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wookey HoleUncover the Legend of the Famous Wookey Hole WitchDiscover the legend of the Wookey Hole Witch - Explore her cave, hear her ta...</span></span></span>

That is why Wookey Hole still matters in Somerset folklore. It is not simply a spooky tale attached to a tourist attraction. It is a case where a physical feature, a place of ancient occupation and a set of protective markings all show how people made sense of fear underground: by naming it, storying it, blessing it, marking it and eventually selling tickets to see it.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.wellsmuseum.org.uk/the-balch-room" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wellsmuseum.org.uk">[wellsmuseum.org.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wellsmuseum.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Wells Museum</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wells Museum</span></span></span>

## The witch, the monk and the stone figure

The familiar version of the legend is direct and memorable. A feared woman lives in Wookey Hole Caves. Villagers blame her for misfortune, curses and spoiled lives. In the tourist retelling, they appeal to Glastonbury Abbey, and a monk — often named Father Barnard in the modern Wookey Hole version — comes to confront her. He follows her into the cave, uses blessed water, and she is petrified into the stalagmite still pointed out as the Witch of Wookey Hole.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.wookey.co.uk/wookey-hole-witch" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wookey.co.uk">[Wookey Hole]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wookey.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wookey HoleUncover the Legend of the Famous Wookey Hole WitchDiscover the legend of the Wookey Hole Witch - Explore her cave, hear her ta...</span></span></span>

Older and fuller versions give the tale a more personal motive. One strand says a man from Glastonbury was engaged to a young woman from Wookey, but the witch cursed or obstructed the romance. The disappointed man became a monk and later took revenge by pursuing the witch into the cave, blessing the water and throwing it into the dark corner where she hid. That version makes the story less like a simple village rescue and more like a tale of failed courtship, resentment and religious power.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wookey_Hole_Caves" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Wookey Hole Caves</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wookey Hole Caves</span></span></span>

The stone figure matters because it gives the story a body. Many legends say “this happened here”, but Wookey Hole says “there she is”. A stalagmite is a natural cave formation built up by mineral-rich dripping water, yet in folklore it can become a punished person, a warning, a relic or a proof-like object. The Witch of Wookey Hole belongs to the same broad family of petrifaction tales as Somerset’s Stanton Drew stone circle, where stones are explained as people turned to rock after breaking a moral or religious rule. At Wookey, the offence is not dancing on a Sunday but being imagined as a dangerous woman whose power must be stopped.

The strongest evidence for the witch legend is therefore not evidence that such a witch existed. It is evidence that a particular formation in a particular cave has long carried a local story. Wookey Hole’s own presentation openly treats the Witch as an enduring legend, while the Somerset Historic Environment Record notes the cave’s archaeological importance and the apotropaic, or protective, marks recorded in the area known as the Witch’s Chimney.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.wookey.co.uk/wookey-hole-witch" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wookey.co.uk">[Wookey Hole]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wookey.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wookey HoleUncover the Legend of the Famous Wookey Hole WitchDiscover the legend of the Wookey Hole Witch - Explore her cave, hear her ta...</span></span></span>

## How old is the Wookey Hole Witch story?

The legend is often described in tourist language as very ancient or nearly a thousand years old, but the securely traceable literary record is later and more complicated. A key eighteenth-century witness is Henry Harington’s poem usually known as “The Witch of Wokey”. Project Gutenberg’s text of Percy’s *Reliques of Ancient English Poetry* says the poem was published in the 1756 collection *Euthemia*, written in 1748, and later associated with Dr Harington of Bath.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/files/45939/45939-h/45939-h.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">45939 h</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">45939 h</span></span></span>

A modern discussion of the poem notes that an anonymous version appeared in *The Gentleman’s Magazine* in 1755 and was reprinted in a pamphlet the following year as “Slander; or, the Witch of Wokey”. That matters because it pushes the written life of the legend back into the mid-eighteenth century, but it does not prove the whole story is medieval in the form now told to visitors. It suggests that a local tale or guide tradition was already available for literary treatment by that period.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://brombonesbooks.com/2023/04/23/the-witch-of-wookey-or-the-merciless-monk/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: brombonesbooks.com">[BROM BONES BOOKS]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">brombonesbooks.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">BROM BONES BOOKSThe Witch of Wookey; Or, The Merciless Monk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">BROM BONES BOOKSThe Witch of Wookey; Or, The Merciless Monk</span></span></span>

Harington’s version is not just a neutral folklore transcript. It is playful, satirical and literary. The poem uses the cave legend to explain why women in Wells supposedly lacked husbands, and modern readers should be careful not to treat every line as a verbatim survival of oral tradition. Even so, it is valuable because it shows that the witch-shaped stone at Wookey was already meaningful enough in the eighteenth century to be turned into verse and anthologised.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://brombonesbooks.com/2023/04/23/the-witch-of-wookey-or-the-merciless-monk/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: brombonesbooks.com">[BROM BONES BOOKS]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">brombonesbooks.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">BROM BONES BOOKSThe Witch of Wookey; Or, The Merciless Monk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">BROM BONES BOOKSThe Witch of Wookey; Or, The Merciless Monk</span></span></span>

The likely history, then, is layered:

* **Oral guide stories** seem to have circulated before or alongside eighteenth-century literary versions.
* **Harington’s poem** gave the tale a wider printed life.

That layered history is more interesting than a simple claim that the story is “true” or “ancient”. Wookey Hole is a good example of how folklore can be old in setting, later in surviving text, and still alive in modern performance.

## Caves, fear and Christian authority

Wookey Hole is not just any hiding place. It is a cave system in the Mendip Hills, near Wells, where the River Axe emerges from underground. The British Geological Survey describes Wookey Hole as the source of the River Axe and notes that the cave was formed by the underground river; unusually for the Mendips, it is formed in Triassic Dolomitic Conglomerate rather than the more familiar Carboniferous Limestone.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www2.bgs.ac.uk/mendips/localities/wookey.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: www2.bgs.ac.uk">[British Geological Survey]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">www2.bgs.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on bgs.ac.uk.</span></span></span>

That physical setting explains much of the legend’s force. Caves are natural places for stories about danger: they are dark, echoing, wet, difficult to read and linked with disappearance. A chamber with a human-like stone figure beside underground water is ready-made for a tale about a hidden woman, a pursuit, a blessing and transformation. The story turns a difficult landscape into a moral drama, with the cave as both lair and courtroom.

The monk’s role is equally important. In the common legend, ordinary villagers cannot solve the danger themselves. They need help from Glastonbury Abbey or a religious figure whose authority is stronger than the witch’s. The confrontation therefore stages a battle between two kinds of power: suspected harmful magic and Christian blessing. In modern terms, it is a story about social fear being managed through religious authority.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.wookey.co.uk/wookey-hole-witch" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wookey.co.uk">[Wookey Hole]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wookey.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wookey HoleUncover the Legend of the Famous Wookey Hole WitchDiscover the legend of the Wookey Hole Witch - Explore her cave, hear her ta...</span></span></span>

This does not mean that the tale should be read as a simple record of medieval events. It is better understood as a narrative pattern that made sense to people who believed dangerous forces could cling to places, people and thresholds. The monk blesses water because water in Christian practice can mark purification and protection; the witch becomes stone because the cave already contains a stone that looks as though it needs explaining.

The phrase “Cave of Fear” works because the site offers more than one kind of fear at once. There is the natural fear of underground space, the social fear of the outsider woman, the religious fear of evil, and the practical fear of misfortune in a rural community. Wookey Hole’s legend gathers those fears into one visible figure.

## Protection marks and belief underground

The most important evidence connected with Wookey Hole is not the stone witch but the protective marks found in the cave. The University of Bristol Spelæological Society’s publication summary for C. J. Binding and L. J. Wilson’s study says that the Witch’s Chimney in Wookey Hole contains the largest concentration of ritual protection marks so far discovered in any cave, and that the study also examines the growth of the Witch of Wookey Hole folk narrative.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ubss.org.uk/publications/proceedings/v25/s1/p47-73/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ubss.org.uk">[ubss.org.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ubss.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UBSS Proceedings 25(1), pp 47-73The Witch&#x27;s Chimney in Wookey Hole contains the largest concentration of ritual protection marks so far d...</span></span></span>

These marks are often called “witch marks”, though that popular term can mislead. They were not marks identifying witches. They were protective symbols, also known as apotropaic marks, made to turn away harm. Historic England explains that such marks are found in historic places including churches, houses, barns and caves, and were usually scratched near entry points such as doors, windows and fireplaces to protect people from witches and evil spirits.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://historicengland.org.uk/whats-new/features/discovering-witches-marks/what-are-witches-marks/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: historicengland.org.uk">[Historic England]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">historicengland.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on historicengland.org.uk.</span></span></span>

Wookey Hole’s marks are especially striking because caves are not ordinary domestic thresholds. A house doorway or chimney is an obvious place to worry about harmful entry. A cave opening, crevice or dark passage works on a larger and stranger scale: it can feel like a threshold between the human world and something beneath it. The presence of protective marks in the Witch’s Chimney suggests that some people did not merely enjoy the cave as scenery. They treated parts of it as spiritually risky space.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ubss.org.uk/publications/proceedings/v25/s1/p47-73/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ubss.org.uk">[ubss.org.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ubss.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UBSS Proceedings 25(1), pp 47-73The Witch&#x27;s Chimney in Wookey Hole contains the largest concentration of ritual protection marks so far d...</span></span></span>

The Somerset Historic Environment Record confirms that a large number of apotropaic marks were recorded in the Witch’s Chimney and compares them with marks known from houses dated to the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries. Museum Crush likewise reported that symbols and letters scratched into Wookey Hole’s walls, around 400 years old, were being displayed to the public for the first time in 2017.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.somersetheritage.org.uk/record/24355" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: somersetheritage.org.uk">[somersetheritage.org.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">somersetheritage.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">24355: Wookey Hole cave, Wookey HoleWookey Hole cave was excavated in 1911 and later by Balch, revealing an average of 4-7ft of stratific...</span></span></span>

This changes how the Witch legend should be read. The marks do not prove the monk-and-witch story happened. But they do prove that Wookey Hole was part of a real protective-belief landscape. People made physical signs in the cave to guard against feared forces. The legend gives that fear a named character; the marks show that protective action was not just fictional.

## The skeleton that became part of the legend

Wookey Hole also has an archaeological afterlife that has fed the witch story. Wells & Mendip Museum’s Balch Room displays finds from Herbert Balch’s excavations at Wookey Hole, including bones associated with the “Wookey Hole Witch”, a child burial, and Stone Age and Iron Age artefacts.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.wellsmuseum.org.uk/the-balch-room" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wellsmuseum.org.uk">[Wells Museum - Main]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wellsmuseum.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Wells Museum</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wells Museum</span></span></span>

The cave’s archaeology is much broader than the legend. The Somerset Historic Environment Record describes excavations by Balch at Wookey Hole, with 4–7 feet of stratification, mostly Iron Age material sealed by Romano-British deposits. That means the cave was a place of long human use and deposition before it became a modern show-cave attraction.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.somersetheritage.org.uk/record/24355" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: somersetheritage.org.uk">[somersetheritage.org.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">somersetheritage.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">24355: Wookey Hole cave, Wookey HoleWookey Hole cave was excavated in 1911 and later by Balch, revealing an average of 4-7ft of stratific...</span></span></span>

The “witch” skeleton, however, is a cautionary example of how folklore can attach itself to remains. A paper in the *Proceedings of the Somerset Archaeological and Natural History Society* states that the skeleton excavated by Balch in 1908–12 was originally described in 1914 as that of a “Goatherd”, with associated items called the “Goatherd’s Relics”; according to that account, the skeleton only became the “witch” when Wookey Hole opened as a show cave in 1926.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://sanhs.org/wp-content/uploads/ShorterPapers-2.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sanhs.org">[sanhs.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sanhs.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Shorter papers</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Shorter papers</span></span></span>

That distinction matters. The bones may be genuinely old and locally important, but their identification as the Witch of Wookey Hole is part of interpretation and display history rather than a secure archaeological fact. It is a good example of how museums, guidebooks and attractions can fuse archaeology and legend in ways that are memorable but not always evidentially tidy.

For readers, the useful takeaway is simple: Wookey Hole has real archaeology, real human remains, real protective marks and a real long-running legend. Those things overlap, but they are not the same kind of evidence. The skeleton belongs to archaeology and museum history; the stalagmite belongs to geology and legend; the marks belong to the history of protective belief.

## Why the legend fits Somerset so well

The Witch of Wookey Hole feels especially Somerset because it joins three county themes: caves, religious landscapes and stories attached to stone. The Mendip Hills supply the underground setting. Nearby Wells and Glastonbury supply the Christian and legendary frame. The stalagmite supplies the visible “proof” that makes the tale easy to remember and retell.

Somerset folklore often works like this. It does not float free as abstract myth. It clings to recognisable places: Glastonbury Tor, Stanton Drew, the Quantocks, Exmoor, orchard villages and Mendip caves. Wookey Hole is one of the clearest examples because the visitor can stand in the place where the story is said to have happened and see the feature that carries the name.

The legend also has a local moral tension. On the surface, it is a victory over a dangerous witch. Look closer, and it is also a story about an isolated woman blamed for communal misfortune, pursued by a male religious authority and fixed forever as a monstrous figure. Eighteenth-century literary versions could even make the monk look harsh or absurd rather than wholly heroic.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://brombonesbooks.com/2023/04/23/the-witch-of-wookey-or-the-merciless-monk/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: brombonesbooks.com">[BROM BONES BOOKS]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">brombonesbooks.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">BROM BONES BOOKSThe Witch of Wookey; Or, The Merciless Monk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">BROM BONES BOOKSThe Witch of Wookey; Or, The Merciless Monk</span></span></span>

That ambiguity is part of the tale’s survival. Modern visitors can enjoy the witch as a spooky cave character, but the story also opens questions about how communities have imagined dangerous women, outsiders, curses, failed relationships and the need for protection. It is a legend with a showman’s simplicity and a deeper social unease beneath it.

## How Wookey Hole retells the witch today

Today, Wookey Hole presents the Witch as one of the central attractions of the cave experience. The official site describes Wookey Hole as a Somerset destination of caves, legends and adventure, and its dedicated Witch page frames the story as a battle between magic and faith involving villagers, Glastonbury Abbey and Father Barnard.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.wookey.co.uk/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wookey.co.uk">[Wookey Hole]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wookey.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wookey.co.uk.</span></span></span>

Tourism has not killed the legend; it has standardised it. The visitor version tends to be clearer, more dramatic and easier to follow than the older textual trail. It gives names, motives and a neat confrontation. It also turns the Witch into a marketable figure: part folklore character, part cave mascot, part seasonal Halloween presence.

That modern retelling should not be dismissed as fake folklore. Folklore often survives through performance, display and repetition. A show cave guide, a school trip, a leaflet, a museum label and a family day out can all transmit a legend, even if they do so differently from an eighteenth-century poem or an older oral tale.

The risk is that the tourist version can flatten the evidence. It may imply a continuous medieval story where the secure sources show a more layered history: eighteenth-century literary treatment, nineteenth- and twentieth-century collecting and display, Balch’s archaeological work, the 1926 show-cave context, later media attention, and recent scholarly interest in protective marks.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/files/45939/45939-h/45939-h.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[gutenberg.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">45939 h</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">45939 h</span></span></span>

The best way to understand Wookey Hole is therefore not to choose between “real history” and “made-up legend”. The place contains both. Its geology made the figure. Its archaeology gave depth. Its protective marks show lived belief. Its printed poems and tourist scripts carried the tale outward. Together they made the Witch of Wookey Hole one of Somerset’s most durable supernatural landmarks.

## What the Cave of Fear really preserves

Wookey Hole preserves an unusually complete folklore chain. There is a named supernatural figure, a visible stone form, a dangerous landscape, a religious defeat narrative, early printed literary evidence, archaeological remains that were later folded into the story, and physical marks made for protection. Few county legends offer so many different kinds of material in one place.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ubss.org.uk/publications/proceedings/v25/s1/p47-73/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ubss.org.uk">[ubss.org.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ubss.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UBSS Proceedings 25(1), pp 47-73The Witch&#x27;s Chimney in Wookey Hole contains the largest concentration of ritual protection marks so far d...</span></span></span>

The “fear” in the Cave of Fear is not just modern horror branding. It reflects older ways of handling uncertainty. A cave mouth could be a boundary. An underground river could feel uncanny. A strange stone could demand explanation. Misfortune could be blamed on a witch. A mark scratched into rock could make people feel guarded. A monk in a story could impose order on a place that felt beyond ordinary control.

That is the real value of the Wookey Hole Witch within Somerset folklore. She is not important because she can be proved as a historical witch. She is important because the cave shows how legend, landscape and protective belief can reinforce one another until a natural formation becomes a county icon. The stone witch stands at the meeting point of geology and storytelling; the marks around the cave remind us that for some earlier visitors, the fear was serious enough to carve into the dark.
