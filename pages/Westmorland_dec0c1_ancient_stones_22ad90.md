## Introduction

The ancient stones of Shap are among [Westmorland]({{ 'westmorland/' | relative_url }})’s most striking examples of how prehistoric places became part of a living landscape of stories. The Shap Stone Avenue, stone circles and nearby monuments were created thousands of years ago, probably during the Late Neolithic and Early Bronze Age, but later generations gave them new meanings through memory, curiosity and legend.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://historicengland.org.uk/listing/the-list/list-entry/1011637" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: historicengland.org.uk">[Historic England]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">historicengland.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Historic England Shap Stone Alignment, Shap</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Historic EnglandShap Stone Alignment, Shap - 1011637 &#124; Historic EnglandSeptember 29, 1949...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: September 29, 1949</span></span></span>



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/Westmorland_dec0c1_ancient_stones_22ad90-overview.webp" | relative_url }}" alt="Overview image for Ancient Stones" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

For local tradition, these stones were never simply abandoned relics. Their unusual shapes, great age and mysterious purpose encouraged explanations involving forgotten peoples, sacred places and supernatural events. Unlike the archaeological evidence, which can show when monuments were built and how they survive, folklore reveals how communities imagined their relationship with the landscape. Shap’s stones therefore stand at the meeting point between prehistoric archaeology and Westmorland’s tradition of legendary places.

## Shap’s prehistoric monuments and stories

The Shap area contains one of the most remarkable concentrations of prehistoric monuments in historic Westmorland. The landscape around the village includes stone alignments, circles, cairns and other ancient features, forming part of a wider prehistoric ceremonial landscape in the Eden Valley. The surviving Shap Stone Alignment is recognised as a scheduled monument by Historic England and consists of a line of surviving granite stones stretching for more than two kilometres, with nearby monuments including the Shap stone circle and burial features.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://historicengland.org.uk/listing/the-list/list-entry/1011637" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: historicengland.org.uk">[Historic England]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">historicengland.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Historic England Shap Stone Alignment, Shap</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Historic EnglandShap Stone Alignment, Shap - 1011637 &#124; Historic EnglandSeptember 29, 1949...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: September 29, 1949</span></span></span>

The original monument was much larger than what visitors see today. Early descriptions recorded a substantial avenue of stones, with some accounts suggesting a complex comparable in scale to other famous prehistoric landscapes. Much of the avenue was later lost through agricultural activity, building and changes in the landscape, but surviving stones still preserve the sense of a deliberately arranged route through the countryside.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://wikishire.co.uk/wiki/Shap_Stone_Avenue" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wikishire.co.uk">[Wikishire]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wikishire.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Wikishire Shap Stone Avenue</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Shap Stone Avenue - WikishireMay 31, 2017...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 31, 2017</span></span></span>

The purpose of the Shap monuments remains uncertain. Archaeologists generally interpret stone alignments and circles as places connected with communal activity, ceremony or ritual practices rather than ordinary settlement. Historic England notes that stone alignments are often associated with burial monuments and other ceremonial sites, and the Shap example is significant because of its length and its relationship with neighbouring prehistoric remains.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://historicengland.org.uk/listing/the-list/list-entry/1011637" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: historicengland.org.uk">[Historic England]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">historicengland.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Historic England Shap Stone Alignment, Shap</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Historic EnglandShap Stone Alignment, Shap - 1011637 &#124; Historic EnglandSeptember 29, 1949...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: September 29, 1949</span></span></span>

This uncertainty helped create the conditions for legend. When later communities encountered enormous stones whose builders were unknown, stories often supplied answers. Across Britain, ancient monuments became linked with giants, witches, magical transformations and lost ages. Shap belongs to this wider tradition, where the mystery of the stones became part of the identity of the place.

## Long Meg and local tradition

Although Long Meg and Her Daughters lies just outside the Shap stone complex, it belongs to the same prehistoric landscape of eastern Cumbria and illustrates the way Westmorland and neighbouring Cumbrian communities attached stories to ancient stone circles. The monument is one of the best-known prehistoric circles in the region, consisting of a large ring of stones with a separate standing stone known as Long Meg.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Long_Meg_and_Her_Daughters" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Long Meg and Her Daughters</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Long Meg and Her Daughters</span></span></span>

The best-known legend says that Long Meg was a witch and the surrounding stones were her companions or daughters, transformed into stone as punishment. Another recurring tradition claims that the stones cannot be counted correctly twice; attempts to prove the true number are said to bring bad luck or reveal the enchantment surrounding the site. These stories are examples of a common folklore pattern in which prehistoric monuments are explained as people or groups frozen in time.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Long_Meg_and_Her_Daughters" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Long Meg and Her Daughters</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Long Meg and Her Daughters</span></span></span>

Such tales should not be read as evidence that the stones were originally understood in this way. The archaeological monument predates the written folklore by thousands of years. Instead, the legends show how later communities interpreted an ancient place whose original meaning had become lost. A mysterious circle of standing stones naturally invited stories about magic, punishment and transformation because those themes gave human meaning to an otherwise unexplained landscape.

The survival of Long Meg’s story also shows the importance of named landscapes in northern folklore. A named stone feels more like a character than an object. Once a monument has a personality, such as “Long Meg”, it becomes easier for oral traditions to preserve and retell stories around it.

## Archaeology meeting folklore

The relationship between archaeology and folklore at Shap is not a conflict between fact and fiction. The two traditions answer different questions. Archaeology investigates the physical evidence: the stones themselves, their arrangement, their materials and their likely dates. Folklore explores how later people remembered, explained and valued those same places.

The Shap stones demonstrate this process clearly. The archaeological evidence points towards prehistoric communities creating a carefully arranged ceremonial landscape using local granite and other natural features. Historic accounts from antiquarians, including writers who recorded Britain’s ancient monuments, helped preserve awareness of the site after parts of it had disappeared.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://historicengland.org.uk/listing/the-list/list-entry/1011637" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: historicengland.org.uk">[Historic England]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">historicengland.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Historic England Shap Stone Alignment, Shap</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Historic EnglandShap Stone Alignment, Shap - 1011637 &#124; Historic EnglandSeptember 29, 1949...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: September 29, 1949</span></span></span>

Later storytelling added another layer. Ancient stones could become landmarks of mystery, places where the boundary between ordinary countryside and legendary landscape seemed thinner. This did not require people to know the original purpose of the monuments; the very absence of an explanation became part of their attraction.

For modern visitors, Shap’s importance lies in this combination of evidence and imagination. The stones are valuable because they are prehistoric monuments, but they are also valuable because generations of people have looked at them, wondered about them and woven them into the character of Westmorland. They preserve not only the work of ancient communities but also the long history of human curiosity about the landscapes around them.
