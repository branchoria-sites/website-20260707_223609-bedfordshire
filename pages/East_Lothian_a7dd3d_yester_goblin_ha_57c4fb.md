## Introduction

Yester Castle’s Goblin Ha’ is the [East Lothian]({{ 'where-east-lothians-legends-still-have/' | relative_url }}) legend where a real medieval ruin becomes a story about wizardry, hobgoblins and noble power. The site lies near Gifford in historic East Lothian, also known as Haddingtonshire, and its most famous survival is not a high tower but a dark vaulted underground chamber: the Goblin Ha’, or Hobgoblin Ha’. Historic environment records place Yester Castle and the Goblin Ha’ at National Grid Reference NT 5563 6668, in the parish and former county of East Lothian, and identify the site as medieval.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.trove.scot/place/56062" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: trove.scot">[Trove Scot]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">trove.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Scot Yester Castle And Goblin Ha&#x27; &#124; Place &#124; trove.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Yester Castle And Goblin Ha&#x27; &#124; Place &#124; trove.scot...</span></span></span>



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/East_Lothian_a7dd3d_yester_goblin_ha_57c4fb-overview.webp" | relative_url }}" alt="Overview image for Goblin Ha" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

The tradition centres on Sir Hugo de Giffard, a 13th-century lord remembered in sober history as a powerful landholder and in legend as the “Wizard of Yester”. The most important point is that this is not just a modern ghost-story pasted onto a ruin. The core claim that Hugo’s cavern or dungeon was said to have been made by demonic art appears in the medieval *Scotichronicon*, in the continuation associated with Walter Bower, an East Lothian-born abbot writing in the 15th century.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://libraryblogs.is.ed.ac.uk/edinburghuniversityarchives/2020/10/30/the-scotichronicon-and-the-goblin-ha-witchcraft-and-hobgoblins-in-our-manuscripts/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: libraryblogs.is.ed.ac.uk">[Library &amp; University Collections]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">libraryblogs.is.ed.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Library &amp; University CollectionsThe Scotichronicon and the Goblin Ha’ – witchcraft and hobgoblins in our manuscripts! &#124; Archives @ Univer...</span></span></span>

## Hugo de Giffard and the medieval source trail

The wizard legend is unusually strong because it has three layers that can still be separated: the historical Hugo, the medieval written notice, and the later romantic retelling. That makes Yester more interesting than a simple “haunted castle” tale. It shows how an elite medieval building, a striking underground space, and a local reputation for uncanny knowledge could fuse into a durable piece of East Lothian folklore.

Hugo de Giffard belonged to the powerful family associated with Yester. Historic Environment Scotland’s garden and designed landscape record says the manor of Yester was granted in the 13th century to Hugh Gifford, and that late-13th-century Yester Castle was built by Hugo de Gifford about a mile south-east of what later became Yester House. The same official account preserves the traditional claim that he constructed the subterranean Goblin Ha’ “by magic”.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://portal.historicenvironment.scot/apex/f?p=1505%3A300%3A%3A%3A%3A%3AVIEWTYPE%2CVIEWREF%3Adesignation%2CGDL00388" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: portal.historicenvironment.scot">[Historic Environment Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">portal.historicenvironment.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Historic Environment ScotlandYESTER HOUSE (GDL00388)...</span></span></span>

The University of Edinburgh Archives account is especially useful because it follows the legend back through the problem of citation. Many online versions used to attribute the key quotation to John of Fordun, but Fordun’s own chronicle ends too early to include Hugo’s mid-13th-century castle. The Edinburgh archivist’s check led instead to Walter Bower’s continuation of Fordun, known as the *Scotichronicon*, and to a manuscript copy in Edinburgh University Library.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://libraryblogs.is.ed.ac.uk/edinburghuniversityarchives/2020/10/30/the-scotichronicon-and-the-goblin-ha-witchcraft-and-hobgoblins-in-our-manuscripts/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: libraryblogs.is.ed.ac.uk">[Library &amp; University Collections]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">libraryblogs.is.ed.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Library &amp; University CollectionsThe Scotichronicon and the Goblin Ha’ – witchcraft and hobgoblins in our manuscripts! &#124; Archives @ Univer...</span></span></span>

That matters because it stops the story being dismissed too quickly as a Sir Walter Scott invention. The medieval source does not prove that goblins built anything. It proves something more historically valuable: by the 15th century, or at least in the tradition Bower recorded, Hugo’s underground work at Yester was already associated with demonic art, old tales and a marvellous subterranean space.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://libraryblogs.is.ed.ac.uk/edinburghuniversityarchives/2020/10/30/the-scotichronicon-and-the-goblin-ha-witchcraft-and-hobgoblins-in-our-manuscripts/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: libraryblogs.is.ed.ac.uk">[Library &amp; University Collections]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">libraryblogs.is.ed.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Library &amp; University CollectionsThe Scotichronicon and the Goblin Ha’ – witchcraft and hobgoblins in our manuscripts! &#124; Archives @ Univer...</span></span></span>

The wording is important. The legend is not simply “a castle was spooky”. It is attached to a particular feature: a cave, cellar, dungeon or underground chamber. The medieval notice is therefore place-based in the strongest sense. It ties supernatural reputation to a piece of architecture that still anchors the story on the ground.

## What the Goblin Ha’ looks like on the ground

The Goblin Ha’ survives because it is not just a tale but a built space. Trove, the Historic Environment Scotland collections portal, records Yester Castle and Goblin Ha’ under the alternative names “Hobgoblin Ha’” and “Goblin Hall”, with medieval classifications including motte and bailey and tower house. The site is described as lying on a slightly crescent-shaped peninsula, defended naturally by steep sides towards Hopes Water and another stream, with large ditches cutting off the ends of the castle platform.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.trove.scot/place/56062" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: trove.scot">[Trove Scot]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">trove.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Scot Yester Castle And Goblin Ha&#x27; &#124; Place &#124; trove.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Yester Castle And Goblin Ha&#x27; &#124; Place &#124; trove.scot...</span></span></span>

The old survey description gives the chamber its most concrete form. From the western range of buildings, a stair leads down to the Goblin Ha’, described as an oblong ashlar-built chamber measuring 37 feet by 13 feet 2 inches. The same record notes that the Goblin Ha’ may date from the 13th century, while later masonry around it belongs to later medieval phases.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.trove.scot/place/56062" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: trove.scot">[Trove Scot]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">trove.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Scot Yester Castle And Goblin Ha&#x27; &#124; Place &#124; trove.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Yester Castle And Goblin Ha&#x27; &#124; Place &#124; trove.scot...</span></span></span>

That physical detail changes how the legend should be read. The chamber is not a vague “secret tunnel” invented by storytellers. It is a substantial undercroft: a lower vaulted room, partly embedded in the earth, associated with an earlier tower and later covered over when the upper works were altered. Historic records describe the Goblin Ha’ as the undercroft of a 13th-century tower, originally partly dug into the mound of a motte.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.trove.scot/place/56062" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: trove.scot">[Trove Scot]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">trove.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Scot Yester Castle And Goblin Ha&#x27; &#124; Place &#124; trove.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Yester Castle And Goblin Ha&#x27; &#124; Place &#124; trove.scot...</span></span></span>

The atmosphere comes from that combination of engineering and concealment. A descending stair, a large stone chamber, darkness, damp, woodland and ruined walls are exactly the conditions in which folklore gathers. Visitors and writers repeatedly respond to the Ha’ as something felt as much as seen: an underground space where the castle seems to have retreated beneath the surface rather than disappeared.

The name also matters. “Ha’” is simply a Scots form of “hall”, but “Goblin Ha’” turns a practical architectural space into a legendary one. “Hobgoblin Ha’” and “Goblin Hall” are not separate traditions so much as variant names for the same charged place. Trove lists those alternatives together, which reflects how the site moves between heritage inventory, local speech and romantic retelling.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.trove.scot/place/56062" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: trove.scot">[Trove Scot]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">trove.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Scot Yester Castle And Goblin Ha&#x27; &#124; Place &#124; trove.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Yester Castle And Goblin Ha&#x27; &#124; Place &#124; trove.scot...</span></span></span>

## Did goblins build it, or did the building create the goblins?

The safest answer is that skilled medieval masons built the Goblin Ha’, but the unusual chamber helped generate a story in which ordinary human labour felt insufficient. This is a common folklore mechanism: when a structure looks too old, too hidden, too technically impressive, or too uncanny for everyday explanation, tradition gives it supernatural builders.

At Yester, the “goblins built it” claim works because the architecture already behaves like a legend. The chamber is underground, vaulted and attached to a lord remembered as powerful and ambiguous. The *Scotichronicon* does not present a detailed construction myth with named goblins and a neat plot. It records an older claim that Hugo’s castle, or at least its cave and dungeon, was said to have been made by demonic art.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://libraryblogs.is.ed.ac.uk/edinburghuniversityarchives/2020/10/30/the-scotichronicon-and-the-goblin-ha-witchcraft-and-hobgoblins-in-our-manuscripts/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: libraryblogs.is.ed.ac.uk">[Library &amp; University Collections]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">libraryblogs.is.ed.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Library &amp; University CollectionsThe Scotichronicon and the Goblin Ha’ – witchcraft and hobgoblins in our manuscripts! &#124; Archives @ Univer...</span></span></span>

That distinction keeps the story honest. The medieval evidence supports an old supernatural reputation. It does not support a literal building history. The undercroft’s likely 13th-century date, ashlar construction and relationship to the castle’s structural phases place it firmly within medieval aristocratic building culture.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.trove.scot/place/56062" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: trove.scot">[Trove Scot]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">trove.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Scot Yester Castle And Goblin Ha&#x27; &#124; Place &#124; trove.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Yester Castle And Goblin Ha&#x27; &#124; Place &#124; trove.scot...</span></span></span>

The legend’s force lies in how it translates power into magic. Hugo was not remembered as a poor cunning-man on the edge of society. He was remembered as a lord whose status, knowledge, building works and political role made him exceptional. The University of Edinburgh Archives account notes that he is known historically as one of the Guardians of the young Alexander III and as one of the Regents appointed by the Treaty of Roxburgh in 1255.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://libraryblogs.is.ed.ac.uk/edinburghuniversityarchives/2020/10/30/the-scotichronicon-and-the-goblin-ha-witchcraft-and-hobgoblins-in-our-manuscripts/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: libraryblogs.is.ed.ac.uk">[Library &amp; University Collections]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">libraryblogs.is.ed.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Library &amp; University CollectionsThe Scotichronicon and the Goblin Ha’ – witchcraft and hobgoblins in our manuscripts! &#124; Archives @ Univer...</span></span></span>

In that setting, “wizard” is not only a spooky label. It is a folk explanation for intimidating lordship. The man who could command land, masons, retainers and political influence becomes, in story, the man who could command spirits.

## How Scott kept the Wizard of Yester alive

Sir Walter Scott did not invent the Yester legend, but he helped give it a long afterlife. In *Marmion*, published in 1808, Scott places Hugo and the Goblin Hall inside a romantic medieval scene. The host’s tale identifies Sir Hugo as the founder of the Goblin Hall and describes the cavern beneath the castle as a place not made by mortal labour but by “word and charm”.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://mypoeticside.com/show-classic-poem-25626" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: mypoeticside.com">[mypoeticside.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">mypoeticside.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Marmion Canto III: The Inn</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Sir Walter Scott - My poetic side...</span></span></span>

Scott’s version does what literary retellings often do to folklore: it sharpens atmosphere, dramatises character and gives the tale memorable imagery. Hugo emerges from underground in strange wizardly clothing, marked by magical signs and linked to demons, prophecy and King Alexander III.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://mypoeticside.com/show-classic-poem-25626" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: mypoeticside.com">[mypoeticside.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">mypoeticside.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Marmion Canto III: The Inn</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Sir Walter Scott - My poetic side...</span></span></span>

This is where many modern readers meet the Wizard of Yester: not as a footnote in a medieval chronicle, but as a gothic figure in the age of romantic literature. Scott connects East Lothian’s local legend to a broader Scottish historical imagination, where ruined castles, old wars, aristocratic memory and supernatural lore all feed one another.

The important caution is that Scott’s poetry is not the same kind of evidence as Bower’s chronicle or the archaeological inventory. Scott preserves and amplifies. He is part of the legend’s transmission, not proof of its medieval details. The strongest reading is layered: Bower gives evidence for an old tradition; the surviving Ha’ gives it a physical anchor; Scott gives it a popular literary form.

## Why this legend belongs so strongly to East Lothian

Yester’s Goblin Ha’ fits East Lothian because the county’s folklore often clings to specific, documentable places rather than floating in a general mist of “old Scotland”. The tale is not set in an unnamed otherworld. It is tied to Gifford, Hopes Water, the Yester estate, the old county of Haddingtonshire and a real medieval structure recorded by national heritage bodies.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.trove.scot/place/56062" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: trove.scot">[Trove Scot]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">trove.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Scot Yester Castle And Goblin Ha&#x27; &#124; Place &#124; trove.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Yester Castle And Goblin Ha&#x27; &#124; Place &#124; trove.scot...</span></span></span>

It also belongs to the county’s wider pattern of powerful local memory. East Lothian’s best-known supernatural traditions include witchcraft at North Berwick, saintly landscapes around the coast, and uncanny ruins such as Yester. The Goblin Ha’ sits at the medieval aristocratic end of that spectrum: not a witch-trial courtroom or a holy well, but a noble stronghold where local tradition imagined hidden knowledge beneath visible power.

The landscape reinforces the story. Yester Castle’s position on a defended peninsula, cut off by ditches and bounded by steep drops, gives it the feeling of a place apart. Historic records describe precipitous sides, Hopes Water, a tributary stream and major earthworks, so the site’s separateness is not just mood; it is built into the terrain.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.trove.scot/place/56062" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: trove.scot">[Trove Scot]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">trove.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Scot Yester Castle And Goblin Ha&#x27; &#124; Place &#124; trove.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Yester Castle And Goblin Ha&#x27; &#124; Place &#124; trove.scot...</span></span></span>

That is why the legend has survived in public imagination. A castle tower can fall, but a chamber beneath the ground remains suggestive. The Goblin Ha’ lets East Lothian offer one of the most memorable kinds of folklore site: a place where the reader can separate evidence from enchantment without losing the pleasure of either.

## Modern retelling and the problem of access

Today, Yester is retold through heritage records, walking blogs, local-history pages, tourism writing and social media. That modern afterlife keeps the Wizard of Yester visible, but it also tends to simplify the story into a darker, neater formula: evil wizard, pact with the Devil, goblin workers, haunted underground hall. Those elements are recognisable in later retellings, but the evidence is more textured than the headline version.

There is also a practical modern issue: the ruin is fragile. In October 2021, STV reported that Yester Castle had been closed to the public after a substantial theft of stone from the subterranean room. Police Scotland said the damage affected a wall supporting the vault of the underground chamber, raising safety concerns. The report also noted that the ruin is legally protected as a nationally important monument under the Ancient Monuments and Archaeological Areas Act 1979.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://news.stv.tv/east-central/medieval-castle-closed-to-public-after-substantial-theft-of-stone" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: news.stv.tv">[STV News]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">news.stv.tv</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on stv.tv.</span></span></span>

That incident is part of the folklore story in a less romantic way. The Goblin Ha’ survives because stone, law, land management and public behaviour all matter. A legend can make a ruin famous, but fame can also bring pressure. The chamber’s magic, in the public sense, depends on not treating it as a disposable backdrop for adventure.

For readers encountering the story now, the best approach is to hold three truths together. First, Yester’s underground hall is a real medieval structure with a strong heritage record. Secondly, the wizard tradition has unusually old documentary support, though not as proof of literal magic. Thirdly, the modern site is vulnerable, and any retelling that values the legend should also value the survival of the stone that carries it.

## What the Goblin Ha’ tells us

Yester Castle’s Goblin Ha’ matters because it shows folklore forming around the meeting point of architecture, status and memory. A 13th-century undercroft became more than a room because people saw in it something excessive: too deep, too strange, too impressive, too well suited to a man remembered as powerful beyond the ordinary.

The medieval source trail gives the tradition weight, the surviving chamber gives it presence, and Scott’s *Marmion* gives it literary reach. None of these layers needs to cancel the others. The Goblin Ha’ is not less interesting because goblins did not literally build it. It is more interesting because East Lothian has preserved a place where medieval engineering, local rumour, chronicle writing and romantic poetry still meet underground.
