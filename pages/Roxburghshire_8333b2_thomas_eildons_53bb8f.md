## Introduction

Yes: in the Border tradition, Thomas the Rhymer enters Elfland through the Eildon landscape near Melrose. The core tale is not just “a fairy story about Thomas”; it is a place-bound legend in which Huntlie Bank, the Eildon Tree, the Rhymer’s Stone and the three-peaked Eildon Hills turn part of [Roxburghshire]({{ 'where-roxburghshires-legends-meet-the/' | relative_url }}) into a threshold between ordinary country and the fairy otherworld. The famous ballad opens with Thomas lying on Huntlie Bank and seeing a bright lady ride down by the Eildon Tree; she proves to be the Queen of Elfland, takes him away, and returns him with prophecy and truth-speaking.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.scottishpoetrylibrary.org.uk/poem/thomas-the-rhymer/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: scottishpoetrylibrary.org.uk">[Scottish Poetry Library]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">scottishpoetrylibrary.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on scottishpoetrylibrary.org.uk.</span></span></span>

That is why this legend matters so strongly in Roxburghshire. Thomas himself belongs to Ercildoune, now Earlston in neighbouring Berwickshire, but the fairy encounter is imagined and visited around Melrose and the Eildons, in historic Roxburghshire. Modern walking routes, tourist interpretation and the Rhymer’s Stone keep the story attached to a real hillside rather than leaving it as a floating medieval romance.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://scotlandstartshere.com/point-of-interest/rhymers-stone/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: scotlandstartshere.com">[Scotland Starts Here]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">scotlandstartshere.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on scotlandstartshere.com.</span></span></span>

## The ballad encounter on Huntlie Bank

The most memorable version begins with stillness: Thomas is lying outdoors, and the uncanny arrives not in a castle or churchyard but on a bank below the Eildons. In the Scottish Poetry Library text, “True Thomas” sees a richly dressed lady riding by the Eildon Tree, her horse hung with silver bells. He first greets her as the Queen of Heaven, but she corrects him: she is the Queen of Elfland.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.scottishpoetrylibrary.org.uk/poem/thomas-the-rhymer/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: scottishpoetrylibrary.org.uk">[Scottish Poetry Library]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">scottishpoetrylibrary.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on scottishpoetrylibrary.org.uk.</span></span></span>

The setting is doing more than providing scenery. Huntlie Bank and the Eildon Tree make the encounter local, visible and repeatable in the imagination. The reader or walker does not need to know the whole medieval romance to grasp the force of it: a man lies in a recognisable Border landscape, misreads a supernatural lady through Christian expectation, and is pulled instead into a fairy geography beside the hills.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.tam-lin.org/library/thomas_text.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: tam-lin.org">[tam-lin.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">tam-lin.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on tam-lin.org.</span></span></span>

The ballad is catalogued as Child Ballad 37 and Roud 219, which matters because it shows that the song is part of the wider recorded ballad tradition, not merely a modern tourist anecdote. The Vaughan Williams Memorial Library index records “Thomas the Rhymer” under Roud 219 with Child 37 as another identifying number, while Francis James Child’s collected texts preserve variant openings and details of the fairy encounter.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archives.vwml.org/songs/45c821f7-f0ee-48ab-bdff-898377ce3aa7" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.vwml.org">[archives.vwml.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.vwml.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">THOMA S THE RHYMER</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">THOMA S THE RHYMER</span></span></span>

The tale’s fairy road is also striking. In common versions, the Queen shows Thomas three roads: the narrow road of righteousness, the broad road of wickedness, and the bonny road to Elfland. The supernatural geography is moral as well as magical, but it is not a simple sermon. Thomas does not merely choose between heaven and hell; he follows the Queen into a third realm, a fairy country with its own rules, time and danger.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.tam-lin.org/library/thomas_text.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: tam-lin.org">[tam-lin.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">tam-lin.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on tam-lin.org.</span></span></span>

## Eildon Tree, Melrose and disputed places

The most visitable focus today is the Rhymer’s Stone, near Melrose, which marks the reputed site of the Eildon Tree. Scotland Starts Here, the regional tourism site, describes it carefully as the “reputed (fictional) site” where Thomas met the Queen of the Fairies, while Scottish Borders Council’s walking guide similarly calls it the reputed site of the Eildon Tree and notes that a display board relates the story.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://scotlandstartshere.com/point-of-interest/rhymers-stone/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: scotlandstartshere.com">[Scotland Starts Here]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">scotlandstartshere.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on scotlandstartshere.com.</span></span></span>

That wording is useful because it avoids a common trap. The Rhymer’s Stone does not prove that a historical Thomas literally met a fairy queen at that exact point. It shows that local memory, literary tradition and later commemoration have fixed the legend to a particular walkable place. Undiscovered Scotland records that the stone was erected in 1929 by the Melrose Literary Society to mark where the fabled Eildon Tree was believed to have stood.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.undiscoveredscotland.co.uk/melrose/rhymersstone/index.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: undiscoveredscotland.co.uk">[Undiscovered Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">undiscoveredscotland.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Undiscovered Scotland Rhymer&#x27;s Stone</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Undiscovered Scotland Rhymer&#x27;s Stone</span></span></span>

The place-name evidence in the ballad is not perfectly tidy. Some ballad versions mention Huntly or Huntlie Bank and the Eildon Tree; other material uses different local phrasing such as “Farnalie”. Child’s notes and later summaries point back to the Eildon Hills and the Earlston-Melrose area, but they do not give modern readers a single unarguable grid reference for the medieval or oral scene.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.tam-lin.org/library/thomas_text.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: tam-lin.org">[tam-lin.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">tam-lin.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on tam-lin.org.</span></span></span>

That uncertainty is part of the Eildon fairy landscape’s character. It is not a heritage site like a dated battle memorial, where the aim is to pin one event to one spot. It is a legendary zone: a tree, a bank, a hill-road, a stone, a view, and a remembered route between Melrose, Earlston and the Eildon slopes. Modern “Rhymer’s Route” walking material keeps that zone alive by linking the Rhymer’s Stone with paths into the Eildon Hills rather than treating the stone as the whole story.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://walkmelrose.org.uk/route-9-rhymers-route/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: walkmelrose.org.uk">[Walk Melrose]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">walkmelrose.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on walkmelrose.org.uk.</span></span></span>

## Why the Eildons make such a convincing fairy threshold

The Eildon Hills are among the most distinctive landmarks in the Borders, rising just south of Melrose. Their three summits gave the nearby Roman site of Trimontium its name, and the hills dominate views from Melrose and the Tweed valley.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.trimontium.co.uk/archive-item/the-eildon-hills/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: trimontium.co.uk">[trimontium.co.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">trimontium.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on trimontium.co.uk.</span></span></span>

Long before Thomas’s legend was written down, Eildon Hill North was already a remarkable human landscape. Historic Environment Scotland’s Trove record identifies the site as a prehistoric fort in the parish of Melrose and former county of Roxburghshire; its archaeology notes describe Eildon Hill North as the largest hill-fort in Scotland, extending over 39 acres on the summit.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.trove.scot/place/55668" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: trove.scot">[Trove Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">trove.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Scotland Eildon Hill North &#124; Place &#124; trove.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Scotland Eildon Hill North &#124; Place &#124; trove.scot</span></span></span>

That does not mean the Thomas story is a coded memory of the hillfort. It means the legend attached itself to a place that was already visually and historically charged. A hill with ancient ramparts, Roman associations, broad views and a dramatic threefold shape is exactly the kind of landscape where later story can plausibly gather. The fairy world feels close because the hills already seem to hold more than ordinary scenery.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.trimontium.co.uk/archive-item/the-eildon-hills/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: trimontium.co.uk">[trimontium.co.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">trimontium.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on trimontium.co.uk.</span></span></span>

The “hollow hill” idea deepens this effect. Modern retellings often say Thomas was led into or beneath the Eildons, while the ballad’s fairy road carries him away into Elfland. The detail varies, but the imaginative movement is consistent: the hillside is not just a backdrop but a doorway.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://highlandtrails.com/legend-thomas-rhymer/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: highlandtrails.com">[highlandtrails.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">highlandtrails.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Legend of Thomas the Rhymer</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Legend of Thomas the Rhymer</span></span></span>

## Prophecy, truth-speaking and fairy time

Thomas’s return from Elfland explains two parts of his identity: prophecy and truth-speaking. He becomes “True Thomas”, a figure who can foretell events and cannot lie. In the ballad tradition, this gift comes after his time with the Queen of Elfland; in later popular lore, it helps explain why prophecies were attached to his name for centuries.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://mainlynorfolk.info/steeleye.span/songs/thomastherhymer.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: mainlynorfolk.info">[Mainly Norfolk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">mainlynorfolk.info</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on mainlynorfolk.info.</span></span></span>

The fairy time element is one of the tale’s most durable features. Thomas is commonly said to spend seven years in Elfland, though modern retellings sometimes play with the contrast between time as experienced in fairyland and time passed in the human world. That “lost time” motif is familiar in fairy tradition, but here it is anchored to a named Border landscape rather than an anonymous fairy mound.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.undiscoveredscotland.co.uk/melrose/rhymersstone/index.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: undiscoveredscotland.co.uk">[Undiscovered Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">undiscoveredscotland.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Undiscovered Scotland Rhymer&#x27;s Stone</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Undiscovered Scotland Rhymer&#x27;s Stone</span></span></span>

His prophetic reputation also outgrew the fairy encounter. The Internet Archive record for James A. H. Murray’s 1875 edition, *The Romance and Prophecies of Thomas of Erceldoune*, shows how the Thomas material was treated as both romance and prophetic literature, printed from five manuscripts with discussion of later prophetic traditions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/details/romanceprophecie00thomuoft" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on archive.org.</span></span></span>

Walter Scott was crucial in shaping the modern form of Thomas’s Border identity. Scott included “Thomas the Rhymer” in *Minstrelsy of the Scottish Border*, and the Walter Scott educational site notes its appearance there; National Library of Scotland material also places the *Minstrelsy* in Scott’s early Border-rooted collecting and publishing career.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://walterscott.eu/education/ballads/supernatural-ballads/thomas-the-rhymer/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: walterscott.eu">[Walter Scott]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">walterscott.eu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on walterscott.eu.</span></span></span>

## What is old tradition, and what is later retelling?

The Thomas tradition has several layers, and mixing them up makes the story seem simpler than it is. At the oldest literary level there is the medieval romance and prophetic material associated with Thomas of Erceldoune. Then there is the ballad tradition, catalogued by Child and Roud. Then there is Scott’s influential literary handling. Finally, there are modern heritage boards, walking routes, podcasts, songs and tourist retellings.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/details/romanceprophecie00thomuoft" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[archive.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on archive.org.</span></span></span>

The fairy landscape of the Eildons sits across those layers. Huntlie Bank and the Eildon Tree belong to the ballad’s setting; the Rhymer’s Stone belongs to modern commemoration; the Eildon Hills themselves belong to the long physical landscape of Melrose and Roxburghshire. A reader should not treat all three as the same kind of evidence. The ballad is evidence for tradition, the stone is evidence for local memorialisation, and the hills are the real terrain on which both have settled.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.scottishpoetrylibrary.org.uk/poem/thomas-the-rhymer/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: scottishpoetrylibrary.org.uk">[scottishpoetrylibrary.org.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">scottishpoetrylibrary.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on scottishpoetrylibrary.org.uk.</span></span></span>

This distinction does not weaken the legend. It makes it more interesting. The story has survived because each age found a usable Thomas: medieval seer, ballad hero, Scott-era Border prophet, and modern figure of fairy tourism. The Eildon setting gives those versions a shared centre, even when the details of the encounter, the length of the absence or the exact site of the tree shift between sources.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_the_Rhymer" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Thomas the Rhymer</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Thomas the Rhymer</span></span></span>

## How the legend is encountered today

For most modern visitors, the Eildon fairy landscape is encountered on foot. Scottish Borders Council’s *Paths Around Melrose* includes a Rhymer’s Route starting and finishing at the Abbey car park in Melrose, giving a 14.5 km route that visits the Rhymer’s Stone and continues towards the Eildon Hills. The guide explicitly links the stone with Thomas, the Queen of the Fairies and the reputed site of the Eildon Tree.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.scotborders.gov.uk/downloads/file/680/melrose.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: scotborders.gov.uk">[Scottish Borders Council]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">scotborders.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Scottish Borders Council</span></span></span>

That walking context matters. The legend is not just consumed as a paragraph in a book; it is built into a route through fields, old roads, hill paths and viewpoints. The same guide notes the old Bogleburn Road between Melrose and Newtown St Boswells near the Rhymer’s Stone, a name that gives the route an extra local uncanny flavour even before the walker reaches the hills.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.scotborders.gov.uk/downloads/file/680/melrose.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: scotborders.gov.uk">[Scottish Borders Council]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">scotborders.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Scottish Borders Council</span></span></span>

Modern official and semi-official heritage language is also notably cautious. Scotland Starts Here calls the Rhymer’s Stone a point of interest and says it marks the reputed site where Thomas met the Queen of the Fairies, but it labels the site fictional; the council guide uses “reputed” rather than presenting the episode as literal history.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://scotlandstartshere.com/point-of-interest/rhymers-stone/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: scotlandstartshere.com">[Scotland Starts Here]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">scotlandstartshere.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on scotlandstartshere.com.</span></span></span>

That caution is exactly right for a public folklore page. The value of the Eildon tradition is not that it can be proven as an event. Its value is that a famous Border prophecy legend became inseparable from a specific Roxburghshire landscape, leaving a trail through ballad, manuscript, antiquarian editing, local memorial and modern walking culture.

## Why this belongs to Roxburghshire folklore

Thomas the Rhymer is a Border figure, not a figure who fits neatly inside one modern council boundary or one historic county label. Earlston, the old Ercildoune associated with Thomas, lies in neighbouring Berwickshire, while Melrose and the Eildon Hills are central to Roxburghshire’s historic landscape. The legend therefore crosses county edges in exactly the way Border folklore often does: people, songs and roads move, but the story remains anchored in recognisable places.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_the_Rhymer" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Thomas the Rhymer</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Thomas the Rhymer</span></span></span>

For Roxburghshire, the Eildon element is the key. The county’s contribution is not simply “Thomas existed” or “Thomas prophesied”; it is the transformation of the Melrose hillside into Elfland’s threshold. The Eildon Tree, Huntlie Bank and the Rhymer’s Stone make the story local, while the hills themselves give it the scale and strangeness that a fairy-entrance legend needs.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.scottishpoetrylibrary.org.uk/poem/thomas-the-rhymer/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: scottishpoetrylibrary.org.uk">[Scottish Poetry Library]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">scottishpoetrylibrary.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on scottishpoetrylibrary.org.uk.</span></span></span>

The tale also links naturally with other Roxburghshire and Border traditions: hollow hills, wizard stories around the Eildons, Melrose Abbey’s historic imagination, old roads, and the wider ballad culture collected and reshaped by Walter Scott. But Thomas’s Eildon fairy landscape should remain its own subject, because it answers a very specific reader question: not simply who Thomas was, but where the Border imagination placed the door to Elfland.
