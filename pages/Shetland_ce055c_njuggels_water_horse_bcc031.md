## Introduction

The njuggel is [Shetland]({{ 'shetland/' | relative_url }})’s own water-horse warning: a beautiful, deceptive horse or pony that appears near freshwater, tempts a traveller to mount, then bolts towards a loch, burn or mill-water. It belongs to the same broad family as the Scottish kelpie, but Shetland tradition gives it a local shape, setting and behaviour. Rather than a generic monster, the njuggel makes sense in an island landscape where old moorland journeys, peat work, sheep, mills, darkness and sudden water danger were part of ordinary life. Shetland Museum and Archives describes the creature as a freshwater horse that does not quite match the shapeshifting kelpie: its trick is simpler and more practical, because it looks rideable until it is too late.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.shetlandmuseumandarchives.org.uk/blog/the-most-deceptive-of-creatures-the-njuggel" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: shetlandmuseumandarchives.org.uk">[Shetland Museum &amp; Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">shetlandmuseumandarchives.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Shetland Museum &amp; Archives Who&#x27;s afraid of the njuggel?</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">It was an approachable animal and when it came trotting up to you, your inclination was to hop onto its back. That is why the story still matters. The njuggel turns a realistic hazard — deep water beside a lonely track — into a memorable image: never trust the waiting horse by the loch. It is folklore as landscape-sense, fear, entertainment and child-safety lesson in one.

## What makes a njuggel different from a kelpie?

The njuggel is often introduced as “Shetland’s kelpie”, but that shorthand can flatten what is distinctive about the tradition. In common with kelpies and other water-horses, it is connected with water and danger; it uses the attractive form of a horse to lure people close. Yet Shetland Museum and Archives stresses an important difference: in its account, the njuggel is not a full shapeshifter in the way a kelpie may be. It does not need to become a handsome stranger or a changing beast. It works because, in the right place and light, it seems like a useful or harmless animal.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.shetlandmuseumandarchives.org.uk/blog/the-most-deceptive-of-creatures-the-njuggel" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: shetlandmuseumandarchives.org.uk">[Shetland Museum &amp; Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">shetlandmuseumandarchives.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Shetland Museum &amp; Archives Who&#x27;s afraid of the njuggel?</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">It was an approachable animal and when it came trotting up to you, your inclination was to hop onto its back. As...Read more...</span></span></span>

Older and dictionary evidence also points to a local creature with its own vocabulary. The Dictionaries of the Scots Language record “neugle” as an animal with the appearance of a dark bluish-grey horse, amphibious, never seen far from water, and especially associated with the underhouse of a watermill. The related “shoupiltin” entry describes the Shetland “Shoopiltee” as a beautiful small horse that invites a rider to mount, then runs into the sea and drowns them.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://dsl.ac.uk/entry/snd/neugle" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: dsl.ac.uk">[dsl.ac.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">dsl.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on dsl.ac.uk.</span></span></span>

That vocabulary matters because Shetland folklore is strongly place-bound. “Njuggel”, “njuggle”, “nuggle”, “neugle” and “shoepultie” are not just alternative spellings for a pan-Scottish monster. They show a tradition passing through dialect, local telling and antiquarian collection. Shetland.org’s modern folklore guide still treats njuggles as a distinct Shetland feature, associated with burns, lochs, marshes and damp ground rather than with one famous named loch or a single tourist legend.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.shetland.org/blog/shetland-folklore" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: shetland.org">[Shetland.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">shetland.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on shetland.org.</span></span></span>

The detail that most sharply marks the creature is its tail. Shetland Museum and Archives says the njuggel’s unusual form could be recognised when a person came close, especially its flowing, circular tail. Later summaries of the tradition likewise describe a wheel-like tail as the sign that the tempting horse is not a normal horse at all.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.shetlandmuseumandarchives.org.uk/blog/the-most-deceptive-of-creatures-the-njuggel" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: shetlandmuseumandarchives.org.uk">[Shetland Museum &amp; Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">shetlandmuseumandarchives.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Shetland Museum &amp; Archives Who&#x27;s afraid of the njuggel?</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">It was an approachable animal and when it came trotting up to you, your inclination was to hop onto its back. ## Lochs, mills and old moorland journeys

The njuggel’s power depends on Shetland geography. A horse beside water is not frightening in itself. A horse beside a lonely loch at dusk, on a moorland route, in a landscape without modern roads, is another matter. Shetland Museum and Archives makes this point directly: in earlier centuries people moved across moors throughout the year for tasks such as cutting rushes, transporting peat and driving sheep. In that world, an unexpected horse was not automatically impossible or absurd; it could appear to belong to the working landscape until its wrongness became clear.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.shetlandmuseumandarchives.org.uk/blog/the-most-deceptive-of-creatures-the-njuggel" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: shetlandmuseumandarchives.org.uk">[Shetland Museum &amp; Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">shetlandmuseumandarchives.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Shetland Museum &amp; Archives Who&#x27;s afraid of the njuggel?</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">It was an approachable animal and when it came trotting up to you, your inclination was to hop onto its back. As...Read more...</span></span></span>

This is also why the njuggel is not only a loch creature. It belongs to a wider freshwater network: burns, streams, marshy places and mill-water. Shetland.org says njuggles are almost always linked with water, including burns and lochs as well as damp meadows and marshes. The Dictionaries of the Scots Language give the watermill as one of the creature’s favoured haunts, specifically the underhouse — the lower part of the mill where the water-wheel or mechanism worked.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.shetland.org/blog/shetland-folklore" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: shetland.org">[Shetland.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">shetland.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on shetland.org.</span></span></span>

The mill association is especially revealing. Shetland’s small horizontal watermills were not grand romantic ruins; they were part of subsistence life. Shetland.org explains that the common Shetland horizontal mill used water diverted from a stream to turn paddles below the mill building. Historic Environment Scotland’s listing for the Huxter Water Mills describes these buildings as a simple traditional type, made largely from stone, driftwood and local materials, and calls Huxter among the best surviving Mainland Shetland examples.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.shetland.org/blog/shetlands-ancient-mills" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: shetland.org">[Shetland.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">shetland.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Self sufficiency in action: Shetland&#x27;s ancient mills</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Self sufficiency in action: Shetland&#x27;s ancient mills</span></span></span>

A njuggel lurking in a mill’s underhouse therefore connects supernatural fear with real machinery, rushing water and awkward night-time work. Shetland.org preserves the belief that njuggles could get into the mill’s underhouse and stop the “tirl”, the mechanism driving the mill; the remedy was to throw down a burning peat, after which the creature vanished with thunder, flash and blue flame.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.shetland.org/blog/shetland-folklore" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: shetland.org">[Shetland.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">shetland.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on shetland.org.</span></span></span>

That image is more than decorative. It makes the hidden danger of the mill visible. Beneath the floor was moving water and machinery; outside were burns, darkness and rough ground. A story about a water-horse in the underhouse taught caution around places where a child, tired worker or distracted traveller could be hurt.

## How the warning works

The njuggel story is built around a simple trap. The creature does not usually begin by attacking. It approaches or waits as an inviting animal. A tired walker, curious child or unwary traveller climbs on. Then the horse runs for deep water. Shetland Museum and Archives gives the core sequence plainly: the njuggel entices people; once a person mounts, it gallops to the nearby loch and throws them into the deepest part.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.shetlandmuseumandarchives.org.uk/blog/the-most-deceptive-of-creatures-the-njuggel" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: shetlandmuseumandarchives.org.uk">[Shetland Museum &amp; Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">shetlandmuseumandarchives.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Shetland Museum &amp; Archives Who&#x27;s afraid of the njuggel?</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">It was an approachable animal and when it came trotting up to you, your inclination was to hop onto its back. As...Read more...</span></span></span>

The warning works because every part of the story maps onto a real risk:

**Do not trust appearances near water.** The horse looks approachable, but the danger is in accepting the invitation. This makes the njuggel a story about judgement, not only about monsters.

**Take special care at dusk.** Shetland Museum and Archives notes that the njuggel appears at dusk, already close to water. Twilight is when ordinary details blur: the tail, the edge of the loch, the depth of the pool and the safety of the path.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.shetlandmuseumandarchives.org.uk/blog/the-most-deceptive-of-creatures-the-njuggel" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: shetlandmuseumandarchives.org.uk">[Shetland Museum &amp; Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">shetlandmuseumandarchives.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Shetland Museum &amp; Archives Who&#x27;s afraid of the njuggel?</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">It was an approachable animal and when it came trotting up to you, your inclination was to hop onto its back. As...Read more...</span></span></span>

**Respect mills and burns.** Dictionary evidence and modern Shetland interpretation both tie the creature to watermills and running water. The folklore gives a personality to places where hidden currents, wet stones and working machinery made danger easy to underestimate.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://dsl.ac.uk/entry/snd/neugle" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: dsl.ac.uk">[dsl.ac.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">dsl.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on dsl.ac.uk.</span></span></span>

**Teach children through fear they remember.** The idea that such stories worked as safety warnings is not only a modern reading. John Spence’s nineteenth-century Shetland folklore collection is repeatedly cited as treating many spirit stories as precautions for keeping children out of danger, while Jessie Saxby’s later Shetland Traditional Lore is associated with the view that fear of the nuggle helped keep children away from deep water and watermills.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/details/shetlandfolklor00spengoog" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[archive.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on archive.org.</span></span></span>

The tale even includes a possible escape, which makes it feel like practical knowledge rather than pure doom. According to Shetland Museum and Archives, if a rider had the wit to cry out the creature’s name, the njuggel lost stamina, slowed to a trot and gave the rider a chance to jump off.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.shetlandmuseumandarchives.org.uk/blog/the-most-deceptive-of-creatures-the-njuggel" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: shetlandmuseumandarchives.org.uk">[Shetland Museum &amp; Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">shetlandmuseumandarchives.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Shetland Museum &amp; Archives Who&#x27;s afraid of the njuggel?</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">It was an approachable animal and when it came trotting up to you, your inclination was to hop onto its back. As...Read more...</span></span></span>

That detail is important. Many folk warnings are not simply “be afraid”; they also teach alertness. Notice the tail. Know the name. Keep your head. Get off before the water.

## The blacksmith’s daughter and the useful cruelty of iron

One of the best-known modern retellings is “The Blacksmith and the Njuggle”, published by NorthLink Ferries as a Shetland folk tale. It begins with the standard definition: a njuggle is the Shetland name for a water horse, living in freshwater lochs and haunting nearby roads to lure people onto its back. In this version, the creature is not merely mischievous; it may carry victims away or kill them.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.northlinkferries.co.uk/shetland-blog/a-shetland-folk-tale-the-blacksmith-and-the-njuggle/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: northlinkferries.co.uk">[NorthLink Ferries]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">northlinkferries.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on northlinkferries.co.uk.</span></span></span>

The story’s human centre is the blacksmith’s daughter. In many versions of water-horse lore, the victim is a child or traveller who fails to recognise danger. Here, the daughter is harder to catch, and the blacksmith’s craft becomes the answer. Retellings emphasise iron — horseshoes, nails, forge-work — as the material that can master or neutralise the supernatural horse.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.northlinkferries.co.uk/shetland-blog/a-shetland-folk-tale-the-blacksmith-and-the-njuggle/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: northlinkferries.co.uk">[NorthLink Ferries]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">northlinkferries.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on northlinkferries.co.uk.</span></span></span>

This tale belongs on a njuggel page because it shows the tradition doing something more interesting than repeating “water is dangerous”. It sets up a contest between local skill and local danger. The njuggel is strongest around lochs, roads and water; the blacksmith is strongest at the hearth, anvil and forge. The creature’s horse-shape makes it vulnerable to horse-handling knowledge. The very disguise it uses to deceive people becomes the form through which people defeat it.

It also explains why some retellings are harsher than others. Shetland Museum and Archives presents the njuggel as deceptive and deadly if the rider fails to escape, but also specific in its behaviour. Other popular retellings call it one of Shetland’s most dangerous creatures and sharpen the threat for storytelling effect. That difference does not make the modern versions worthless; it shows how a warning tale can become more dramatic when retold for visitors, ferry readers, podcasts or folklore blogs.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.shetlandmuseumandarchives.org.uk/blog/the-most-deceptive-of-creatures-the-njuggel" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: shetlandmuseumandarchives.org.uk">[shetlandmuseumandarchives.org.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">shetlandmuseumandarchives.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Shetland Museum &amp; Archives Who&#x27;s afraid of the njuggel?</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">It was an approachable animal and when it came trotting up to you, your inclination was to hop onto its back. ## How old and well-attested is the tradition?

The njuggel is not a newly invented tourist mascot. It is rooted in older Shetland folklore collecting, dialect evidence and nineteenth- to early twentieth-century antiquarian writing, though the surviving record is uneven in the way oral tradition often is.

John Spence’s *Shetland Folk-lore* was published in 1899 and is available through the Internet Archive; Spence says in his preface that he had spent nearly forty years gathering sayings and superstitions from older people. That makes his work important, though not perfect: it is a late Victorian collection shaped by its author’s interests, language and assumptions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/details/shetlandfolklor00spengoog" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on archive.org.</span></span></span>

Jessie M. E. Saxby’s *Shetland Traditional Lore*, published in 1932, is another key source in the later written tradition. Saxby was from Unst and collected customs and lore at a time when she believed many older practices and beliefs were disappearing. Booksellers’ and bibliographic records describe the book as a collection of Shetland traditions and customs, with Saxby explicitly concerned to preserve vanishing material.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://books.google.co.uk/books/about/Shetland_Traditional_Lore.html?id=-YUmAAAAMAAJ&amp;redir_esc=y" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: books.google.co.uk">[Google Books]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">books.google.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on google.co.uk.</span></span></span>

There is also a striking diasporic source. Shetland Museum and Archives notes that James A. Teit, a Shetland-born anthropologist who lived in British Columbia, published “Water-beings in Shetland folk-lore, as remembered by Shetlanders in British Columbia” in the *Journal of American Folklore* in 1918. The article is valuable because it shows Shetland water-being stories remembered by emigrants, not only preserved inside the islands themselves.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.shetlandmuseumandarchives.org.uk/blog/james-a-teit-tackling-racism-in-the-late-1800s" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: shetlandmuseumandarchives.org.uk">[Shetland Museum &amp; Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">shetlandmuseumandarchives.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">james a teit tackling racism in the late 1800s</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">james a teit tackling racism in the late 1800s</span></span></span>

The Dictionaries of the Scots Language add a different kind of evidence: not a single tale, but recorded vocabulary. Their entries for “neugle” and “shoupiltin” preserve definitions and quotations tying the creature to horse-form, water, mills and drowning. That lexical trail supports the idea that the njuggel was part of a wider spoken and written Shetland vocabulary of water-beings, not merely a one-off story.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://dsl.ac.uk/entry/snd/neugle" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: dsl.ac.uk">[dsl.ac.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">dsl.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on dsl.ac.uk.</span></span></span>

## Why the njuggel belongs to Shetland’s identity

Shetland has many supernatural beings, but the njuggel occupies a particular corner of the islands’ imagination. [Trows]({{ 'trows-5401a8/' | relative_url }}) belong strongly to mounds, hills, music and the hidden folk. [Selkies]({{ 'selkies/' | relative_url }}) and mermaids draw attention seaward. The njuggel pulls the eye inland, to freshwater margins: lochs, burns, mills, marshes and moor paths. Shetland.org’s folklore guide places it among the islands’ recognisable figures but distinguishes it from trows by making it more feared and more directly water-bound.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.shetland.org/blog/shetland-folklore" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: shetland.org">[Shetland.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">shetland.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on shetland.org.</span></span></span>

It also reflects Shetland’s mixed northern inheritance without needing a tidy origin story. The wider family resemblance to northern European water-horses is real: scholars and collectors have long compared Shetland water-horse traditions with Scandinavian and Germanic water spirits. Karl Blind’s nineteenth-century discussion, reprinted in historical form, explicitly links the Shetland water-stallion with the Scandinavian Nyk or Nökken.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://randomscottishhistory.com/2021/01/31/kelpies-water-horse-water-bulls-c/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: randomscottishhistory.com">[Scottish History Collection]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">randomscottishhistory.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Scottish History Collection Kelpies, Water-horses, Water-bulls, &amp;c</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Scottish History Collection Kelpies, Water-horses, Water-bulls, &amp;c</span></span></span>

But the most convincing reason to treat the njuggel as Shetlandic is not etymology alone. It is the fit between creature and place. The njuggel’s world is not an abstract mythic Scotland. It is an island landscape of small horses, croft work, burns, peat roads, horizontal mills, winter darkness and freshwater danger. The story is frightening because it uses ordinary Shetland materials.

This is also why the njuggel remains useful in modern retellings. Shetland Museum and Archives can use it to explain how folklore made sense of older travel and risk. Shetland.org can place it in a public-facing guide to local supernatural beings. Ferry and tourism pages can retell the blacksmith story for visitors. Each version shifts tone, but the core warning stays recognisable: water is never just scenery.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.shetlandmuseumandarchives.org.uk/blog/the-most-deceptive-of-creatures-the-njuggel" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: shetlandmuseumandarchives.org.uk">[shetlandmuseumandarchives.org.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">shetlandmuseumandarchives.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Shetland Museum &amp; Archives Who&#x27;s afraid of the njuggel?</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">It was an approachable animal and when it came trotting up to you, your inclination was to hop onto its back. ## Old belief, modern retelling and what not to overclaim

A careful reading should avoid two mistakes. The first is to treat every njuggel detail as if it comes from one ancient, fixed belief system. The evidence is a patchwork: oral tales, dialect entries, antiquarian collections, twentieth-century scholarly references and modern heritage retellings. Some versions stress the creature’s murderous danger; others make it more mischievous, frightening or “feeble” compared with darker water-horses.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuggle" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wikipedia.org.</span></span></span>

The second mistake is to reduce the story to a dry safety poster. The practical warning is central, but folklore works because it gives danger a memorable shape. A sign saying “deep water” may be ignored. A beautiful horse with a wheel-like tail waiting by the loch is harder to forget.

The njuggel therefore sits between belief, teaching and entertainment. It warned children away from mills and deep water. It gave adults a vivid tale for winter nights. It marked certain places — lochsides, burns, marshy tracks, mill underhouses — as places requiring respect. And it gave Shetland a water-horse that is related to the kelpie but not swallowed by it: local in name, local in landscape, and local in the risks it dramatises.
