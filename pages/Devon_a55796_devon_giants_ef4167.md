## Introduction

The giants carved into Plymouth Hoe were part of [Devon]({{ 'what-makes-devon-folklore-so-place-bound/' | relative_url }})’s most spectacular origin legend: the story that the Trojan warrior Corineus fought the giant Goemagot, or Gogmagog, on the Hoe and hurled him from the cliff into the sea. The tale belongs to the medieval legendary history of Britain, especially Geoffrey of Monmouth’s twelfth-century account of Brutus of Troy, but Plymouth made it local by marking the supposed combat in the ground itself. The evidence is unusually interesting for folklore because this was not only a story in books. Late medieval and early modern records point to a real turf-cut giant figure, or figures, maintained on the Hoe from at least the late fifteenth century until the landscape was altered by fortifications.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://devonassoc.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2019/01/Trojans-at-Totnes-Clark-TDA-2016.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: devonassoc.org.uk">[devonassoc.org.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">devonassoc.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on devonassoc.org.uk.</span></span></span>



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/Devon_a55796_devon_giants_ef4167-overview.webp" | relative_url }}" alt="Overview image for Giants" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

This makes Plymouth Hoe one of Devon’s strongest examples of legend becoming landscape. It ties Plymouth to Totnes, Dartmoor and the Tamar border, and turns a harbour headland into a mythic threshold: the place where the old giant race of Albion was defeated and Britain’s legendary human history began.

## Goemagot, Corineus and legendary Britain

The oldest famous written framework for this story is Geoffrey of Monmouth’s *Historia Regum Britanniae*, usually dated to 1136. Cambridge University Library describes the work as a hugely popular pseudo-historical account of Britain’s kings, beginning with the Trojan Brutus’s foundation of the kingdom.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cudl.lib.cam.ac.uk/view/MS-FF-00001-00025-00005/5" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cudl.lib.cam.ac.uk">[Cambridge Digital Library]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cudl.lib.cam.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cam.ac.uk.</span></span></span> In Geoffrey’s version, Brutus and his followers arrive in Albion, encounter giants, and hold a feast at or near the place where they have landed. A band of giants attacks them, led by Goemagot, also later known as Gogmagog. The Trojans kill the other giants but keep Goemagot alive for a wrestling match with Corineus, Brutus’s companion and the legendary namesake of Cornwall.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://devonassoc.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2019/01/Trojans-at-Totnes-Clark-TDA-2016.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: devonassoc.org.uk">[devonassoc.org.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">devonassoc.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on devonassoc.org.uk.</span></span></span>

The fight is brutal and memorable. Goemagot breaks Corineus’s ribs, but Corineus rallies, lifts the giant, carries him to the cliff and throws him into the sea. Geoffrey says the place was called “Goemagot’s Leap”, in Latin *Saltus Goemagot*, “to this very day”.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://devonassoc.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2019/01/Trojans-at-Totnes-Clark-TDA-2016.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: devonassoc.org.uk">[devonassoc.org.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">devonassoc.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on devonassoc.org.uk.</span></span></span> The story therefore does two things at once. It gives Britain a heroic foundation myth, and it gives the south-west a special place within that myth: the giant is not defeated in an abstract wonderland, but at a named western shore.

For Devon readers, the geography is the puzzle. Geoffrey’s text is usually read as placing Brutus’s landing at Totnes, and Totnes later developed its own Brutus tradition, including the Brutus Stone in Fore Street and the naming of Brutus Bridge in 1982. Yet Totnes lies well inland on the River Dart, which makes it an awkward landing place for Geoffrey’s imagined fleet. M. A. John Clark’s Devon Association study argues that Geoffrey’s “Totnesian” coast may reflect a more complicated south Devon geography, possibly tied to Norman lordship, river access and remembered coastal places rather than a simple modern map reading.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://devonassoc.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2019/01/Trojans-at-Totnes-Clark-TDA-2016.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: devonassoc.org.uk">[devonassoc.org.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">devonassoc.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on devonassoc.org.uk.</span></span></span>

Plymouth’s claim is different. It does not rest mainly on the landing, but on the fight. By the early modern period, Plymouth people and writers were identifying the Hoe as the place where Corineus threw Goemagot down. Richard Carew, writing in 1602, argued for Plymouth rather than rival locations such as Dover, and Thomas Westcote later described the Hoe as a cliff well suited to such a fall.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://devonassoc.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2019/01/Trojans-at-Totnes-Clark-TDA-2016.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: devonassoc.org.uk">[devonassoc.org.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">devonassoc.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on devonassoc.org.uk.</span></span></span> That localising instinct is central to the folklore: the tale mattered because the Hoe looked like the right kind of place for it.

## Plymouth Hoe’s turf figures

The most distinctive evidence for Plymouth’s giant tradition is the lost turf figure, or figures, cut into the Hoe. Clark’s study notes that the Plymouth “Gogmagog” was a turf-cut image, comparable in broad type to chalk or turf hill figures elsewhere in England, although Plymouth’s original is now lost. The first recorded local expenditure appears in 1494–5 for “the renewyng of the pycture of Gogmagog”, and similar payments for cleaning, cutting or scouring continued at intervals into the sixteenth century, with the last occasion noted by John Wasson in 1574–5.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://devonassoc.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2019/01/Trojans-at-Totnes-Clark-TDA-2016.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: devonassoc.org.uk">[devonassoc.org.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">devonassoc.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on devonassoc.org.uk.</span></span></span>

That matters because the Plymouth figure is not just a romantic Victorian invention. Its exact age is unknown, but it must predate the late fifteenth-century reference to its renewal. Clark points out that even a fifteenth-century date is significant, since it comes earlier than the first documentary evidence for several other famous English giant figures, including the Cerne Abbas Giant in Dorset and the Long Man of Wilmington in Sussex.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://devonassoc.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2019/01/Trojans-at-Totnes-Clark-TDA-2016.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: devonassoc.org.uk">[devonassoc.org.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">devonassoc.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on devonassoc.org.uk.</span></span></span>

There is, however, a useful caution. The earliest Plymouth town records seem to refer to “Gogmagog” in the singular: “Gogmagog upon the Hoe”, “the picture of Gogmagog”, or “Gogmagog the picture of the Giant”. Later writers describe two figures. Carew says that on the Hoe there were two men cut into the ground, one larger and one smaller, with clubs in their hands, called “Gog-Magog”. Westcote, writing in the 1630s, identifies the two as Corineus and Gogmagog and says they represented the wrestling match.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://devonassoc.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2019/01/Trojans-at-Totnes-Clark-TDA-2016.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: devonassoc.org.uk">[devonassoc.org.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">devonassoc.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on devonassoc.org.uk.</span></span></span>

This is where the evidence becomes folklore rather than simple documentation. It is possible that there were always two figures and that the town accounts used “Gogmagog” as a shorthand name for the whole image. It is also possible that a single giant figure was later interpreted through the better-known story of Corineus and Goemagot, turning one giant into a pair of combatants. Clark treats Westcote carefully, noting that his account may depend partly on Carew and that his local knowledge cannot simply be assumed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://devonassoc.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2019/01/Trojans-at-Totnes-Clark-TDA-2016.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: devonassoc.org.uk">[devonassoc.org.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">devonassoc.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on devonassoc.org.uk.</span></span></span>

The end of the figures is also partly uncertain. There are no known maintenance records after 1574–5, and Carew in 1602 is the last certain witness used by Clark for their existence. Later tradition often says they were lost when the Royal Citadel changed the Hoe’s landscape in the seventeenth century. Historic England dates the Royal Citadel’s main construction to 1665–1675, and Visit Plymouth’s modern account likewise states that the original giant figures were lost when the Citadel was built or when the Hoe was reshaped by it.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://historicengland.org.uk/listing/the-list/list-entry/1012943" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: historicengland.org.uk">[historicengland.org.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">historicengland.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">list entry</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">list entry</span></span></span>

## Why the Hoe was the right stage for a giant fight

Plymouth Hoe works so well in the story because it is both a natural stage and a civic viewpoint. It stands above Plymouth Sound, between town and sea, with the Tamar close by and Cornwall visible across the water. Wikishire describes Plymouth as a city on Devon’s south coast, between the mouths of the Plym and Tamar where they join Plymouth Sound, and notes that the Hoe is a large flat-topped hill dominated by the Citadel.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://wikishire.co.uk/wiki/Plymouth" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wikishire.co.uk">[Wikishire]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wikishire.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wikishire.co.uk.</span></span></span>

That geography helps explain why the legend stayed powerful. A giant thrown from the Hoe is not merely killed; he is cast out of the land and into the sea at a boundary point. In the medieval legendary scheme, Corineus then receives Cornwall, while Brutus takes the wider island that becomes Britain. The story therefore turns a cliff-edge wrestling match into a mythic explanation of western identity: Devon’s harbour, Cornwall’s name, and Britain’s beginning are all pulled into one scene.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://devonassoc.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2019/01/Trojans-at-Totnes-Clark-TDA-2016.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: devonassoc.org.uk">[devonassoc.org.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">devonassoc.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on devonassoc.org.uk.</span></span></span>

Dartmoor National Park’s retelling shows how the Plymouth tale connects inland Devon to the wider giant landscape of the West Country. Its education page says many West Country place-names and features are linked to giants, then retells Brutus’s arrival, the giants’ defeat, the wrestling match on Plymouth Hoe, and the flight of remaining giants into Cornwall and Dartmoor. It ends by linking Goemagot to other Dartmoor giant stories, including Blunderbus.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.dartmoor.gov.uk/learning/dartmoor-legends/the-legend-of-goemagot" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: dartmoor.gov.uk">[Dartmoor National Park]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">dartmoor.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Dartmoor National Park The Legend of Goemagot &#124; Dartmoor</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Dartmoor National Park The Legend of Goemagot &#124; Dartmoor</span></span></span>

That is a good example of how Devon giant folklore works as a cluster rather than a single isolated tale. Plymouth Hoe supplies the great foundation scene. Totnes supplies the Brutus landing tradition. Dartmoor supplies a landscape of giant-haunted tors, rocks, basins and caves. The Tamar supplies the sense of a border crossed by defeated or retreating giants. The result is a mythic map of south-west Devon, not just a monster story.

## Old tradition, literary invention and civic revival

The Plymouth giants sit in the interesting middle ground between medieval literary invention and local tradition. Geoffrey of Monmouth was not writing modern history. His work is a medieval legendary history, and many of its place-name explanations and origin stories are now read as imaginative, political and literary rather than factual. Clark notes that Geoffrey’s story of Brutus was not wholly new, since a ninth-century Welsh historian had already connected Britain’s origin with a descendant of Aeneas called Brutus or Britto, but Geoffrey added vivid circumstantial detail, including the south Devon landfall and Goemagot’s Leap.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://devonassoc.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2019/01/Trojans-at-Totnes-Clark-TDA-2016.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: devonassoc.org.uk">[devonassoc.org.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">devonassoc.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on devonassoc.org.uk.</span></span></span>

What makes Plymouth special is that the legend gained material form. The Hoe figure gave people something to maintain, point to and argue from. In the seventeenth century, even “giant bones” reportedly found during Citadel works were drawn into the story. William Scawen wrote sceptically about claims that great jaws and teeth found during digging on the Hoe were those of Gogmagog; later Plymouth historian Richard Worth interpreted such finds as the remains of extinct animals from local cave deposits. Clark notes that large fossil bones were often interpreted as giant remains in earlier periods, and that the Plymouth finds probably reinforced local belief in Gogmagog’s historical reality.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://devonassoc.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2019/01/Trojans-at-Totnes-Clark-TDA-2016.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: devonassoc.org.uk">[devonassoc.org.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">devonassoc.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on devonassoc.org.uk.</span></span></span>

The modern revival continues the same pattern of remaking. In 2021, two giant figures were recreated on Plymouth Hoe as a temporary art installation by artist Charles Newington, supported by local organisations including Plymouth Waterfront Partnership. Visit Plymouth described the project as a reimagining of figures thought to have been carved into the Hoe in the 1400s, painted onto the grass below the Royal Citadel and maintained through the summer.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.visitplymouth.co.uk/visitor-information/news/2021/6/10/the-giants-are-returning-to-plymouth-hoe-a3804" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: visitplymouth.co.uk">[Visit Plymouth]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">visitplymouth.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Visit Plymouth The giants are returning to Plymouth Hoe</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Visit Plymouth The giants are returning to Plymouth Hoe</span></span></span>

That recreation should not be mistaken for the original medieval or early modern turf-cut work. It is a modern public artwork and heritage gesture. But it is still folklore in action: a city choosing to make an old story visible again in the landscape where earlier generations had placed it.

## What the giant carvings tell us about Devon

The Plymouth Hoe giants matter because they show Devon folklore doing several jobs at once. They give a dramatic origin story to a harbour city, connect Devon to the medieval “Matter of Britain”, and place the county’s south-western edge inside a grand narrative of giants, Trojans, cliffs and borders. They also show how a legend can move between forms: manuscript history, local antiquarian argument, civic records, landscape marking, reported discoveries, tourism and modern public art.

The strongest evidence is not that a giant fight happened, but that people in Plymouth treated the story as locally meaningful for centuries. The town paid to renew or scour a giant image on the Hoe. Antiquarian writers connected the figure with Corineus and Gogmagog. Later people explained the vanished figures through the building of the Citadel. Modern Plymouth has revived them as a symbol of deep local time and maritime identity.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://devonassoc.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2019/01/Trojans-at-Totnes-Clark-TDA-2016.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: devonassoc.org.uk">[devonassoc.org.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">devonassoc.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on devonassoc.org.uk.</span></span></span>

For a Devon folklore map, this makes the Plymouth giants a key bridge between heroic legend and place-based tradition. Dartmoor may be the county’s great engine of [pixies]({{ 'pixies/' | relative_url }}), witches, ghosts and moorland giant tales, but Plymouth Hoe offers something different: a civic myth of beginnings, carved into public ground and remembered at the edge of the sea.
