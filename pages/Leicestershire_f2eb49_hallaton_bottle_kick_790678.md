## Introduction

Hallaton Bottle Kicking is [Leicestershire]({{ 'where-leicestershire-turns-history-into/' | relative_url }})’s great living Easter custom: a public, noisy, physical rite held at Hallaton on Easter Monday, combining a hare pie, penny loaves, a church blessing, a village procession and a rough contest between Hallaton and neighbouring Medbourne. The “bottles” are not glass bottles but small beer-filled barrels, fought over across fields, hedges and streams. What makes the custom so striking is not just its age, but its survival as a performed community event rather than a museum-piece tradition. It belongs to a very specific Leicestershire landscape: St Michael’s Church, the Butter Cross, Hare Pie Bank and the fields between Hallaton and Medbourne. Modern accounts still describe crowds, parades, a church service and the best-of-three struggle for the barrels, while local museum and archaeology projects have helped frame the custom as part of a much longer ritual landscape.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.efdss.org/learning/resources/beginners-guides/48-british-folk-customs-from-plough-monday-to-hocktide/3388-hare-pie" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: efdss.org">[efdss.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">efdss.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">English Folk Dance and Song SocietyEnglish Folk Dance and Song Society - Hare Pie Scramble and Bottle Kicking...</span></span></span>

## Hare pie, penny loaves and the procession

The day is usually explained as two joined customs: the Hare Pie Scramble and the Bottle Kicking. The first part looks like an old charitable dole, in which food and drink are ritually provided and distributed; the second becomes a mass village contest. The English Folk Dance and Song Society describes the event as beginning with a large hare pie, made to a secret recipe, blessed at the parish church and cut up by the local vicar before portions are thrown to the crowd. The other half of the pie is carried onward, accompanied by the decorated beer barrels that will later become the focus of the game.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.efdss.org/learning/resources/beginners-guides/48-british-folk-customs-from-plough-monday-to-hocktide/3388-hare-pie" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: efdss.org">[English Folk Dance and Song Society]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">efdss.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">English Folk Dance and Song SocietyEnglish Folk Dance and Song Society - Hare Pie Scramble and Bottle Kicking...</span></span></span>

That mixture of church, food and disorder is central to the custom’s character. The procession is not simply a pre-match parade. It ties together the official and the unruly: the church blessing, the bread, the pie, the beer, the brass band, the crowd and the walk towards Hare Pie Bank. In modern local descriptions, the event still includes traditional parades and a church service before the contest begins, showing that Hallaton has not separated the “sport” from the ceremonial frame that gives it meaning.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://harboroughfm.co.uk/all-set-for-hallaton-bottle-kicking-spectacular/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: harboroughfm.co.uk">[HFM]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">harboroughfm.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">HFMAll Set For Hallaton Bottle Kicking Spectacular – HFM</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">HFMAll Set For Hallaton Bottle Kicking Spectacular – HFM</span></span></span>

The penny loaves matter because they link the custom to older patterns of Easter charity. The familiar origin story says that a gift of land or money was made on the condition that the rector should provide hare pie, loaves and ale for the poor of Hallaton every Easter Monday. Whether the story preserves a precise historical event is uncertain, but the structure is plausible as folklore: a remembered obligation, a parish setting, food for the poor and a custom that becomes too important for villagers to allow it to lapse.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.medbourne.org.uk/HTML/bottle-kicking---history-or-legend.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: medbourne.org.uk">[Medbourne]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">medbourne.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Medbourne Bottle Kicking</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Medbourne Bottle Kicking</span></span></span>

Hallaton Museum’s account preserves one of the custom’s best-known resistance stories. A parson who disapproved of the annual disorder is said to have refused to provide the hare pie, only for villagers to demand the custom’s return with the cry “No pie – no parson”. The story is partly comic, partly constitutional: the village asserts that the ritual is not an optional entertainment but a customary right. In folklore terms, that is one reason the tale matters. It dramatises the community defending its Easter custom against respectable authority.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://hallaton-museum.co.uk/a-tale-of-rituals-relics-and-restoration/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: hallaton-museum.co.uk">[Hallaton Museum]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">hallaton-museum.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on hallaton-museum.co.uk.</span></span></span>

The hare itself gives the day its most memorable animal image. In local legend, two women were saved from a charging bull when a hare startled or distracted it. In gratitude, the women or their family endowed the Easter dole of hare pie, loaves and ale. This is best treated as an origin legend rather than a proven foundation story. It explains why a hare pie should appear in a Christian Easter procession, why food is distributed, and why the custom feels older than ordinary village sport.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.medbourne.org.uk/HTML/bottle-kicking---history-or-legend.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: medbourne.org.uk">[Medbourne]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">medbourne.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Medbourne Bottle Kicking</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Medbourne Bottle Kicking</span></span></span>

## The rough contest between Hallaton and Medbourne

Bottle Kicking is often compared with rugby or old mass football, but it has its own local logic. The “bottles” are small wooden barrels, traditionally filled with beer and decorated before the contest. EFDSS describes the first barrel being thrown up three times, with the struggle beginning on the third throw; the object is to carry the bottle to the proper scoring place, with Hallaton’s and Medbourne’s goals set in the landscape rather than marked out on a tidy pitch.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.efdss.org/learning/resources/beginners-guides/48-british-folk-customs-from-plough-monday-to-hocktide/3388-hare-pie" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: efdss.org">[English Folk Dance and Song Society]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">efdss.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">English Folk Dance and Song SocietyEnglish Folk Dance and Song Society - Hare Pie Scramble and Bottle Kicking...</span></span></span>

The contest is rough because the field of play is not a field in the neat sporting sense. Modern accounts describe villagers battling across open ground and over fields, and specialist observers note that hedges, ditches, streams and the lie of the land are part of the challenge. That is why the custom feels so different from a revived heritage performance. It still depends on bodies, weather, mud, local knowledge and the hard geography between two villages.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://harboroughfm.co.uk/all-set-for-hallaton-bottle-kicking-spectacular/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: harboroughfm.co.uk">[HFM]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">harboroughfm.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">HFMAll Set For Hallaton Bottle Kicking Spectacular – HFM</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">HFMAll Set For Hallaton Bottle Kicking Spectacular – HFM</span></span></span>

The rivalry with Medbourne gives the day its human plot. Hallaton is the host village and the custom is strongly identified with Hallaton, but Medbourne supplies the opposition and helps turn the rite from a food scramble into a contest between communities. Local tradition says Medbourne became involved after villagers from there stole or seized the ale, forcing Hallaton people to cooperate in getting it back. As with the bull-and-hare story, the point is not that every detail can be proved, but that the legend neatly explains why two neighbouring villages still meet in ritualised conflict.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.martinurch.com/blog/hallaton-hare" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: martinurch.com">[Martin Urch]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">martinurch.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Martin Urch Hallaton Hare</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Martin Urch Hallaton Hare</span></span></span>

There are only a few rules in popular descriptions of the game, which is part of its fame. The spectacle is usually described as a best-of-three contest, and local reporting in recent years has continued to present it as Hallaton and Medbourne wrestling bottles over fields to claim victory. A 2023 Harborough FM report called it one of the country’s oldest continued customs and expected crowds of thousands, which shows how far the event now reaches beyond the two villages while still depending on their rivalry.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://harboroughfm.co.uk/all-set-for-hallaton-bottle-kicking-spectacular/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: harboroughfm.co.uk">[HFM]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">harboroughfm.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">HFMAll Set For Hallaton Bottle Kicking Spectacular – HFM</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">HFMAll Set For Hallaton Bottle Kicking Spectacular – HFM</span></span></span>

The aftermath is as important as the winning. EFDSS notes that the scorer is expected to climb the old Butter Cross and drink from the shaken beer in the winning bottle before it is shared around. Hallaton Museum also describes winners scaling the Butter Cross to sample the beer. That closing action returns the contest to the village centre and turns the fought-over object back into shared drink.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.efdss.org/learning/resources/beginners-guides/48-british-folk-customs-from-plough-monday-to-hocktide/3388-hare-pie" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: efdss.org">[English Folk Dance and Song Society]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">efdss.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">English Folk Dance and Song SocietyEnglish Folk Dance and Song Society - Hare Pie Scramble and Bottle Kicking...</span></span></span>

## Why Hare Pie Bank matters

Hallaton Bottle Kicking is not just “an odd custom in Leicestershire”. It is tied to a remarkable local landscape. Hare Pie Bank, where the Easter event gathers momentum, is also the site of the lost medieval chapel of [St Morrell]({{ 'st-morrell/' | relative_url }}). Community excavations by the Hallaton Field Work Group, supported by University of Leicester Archaeological Services, uncovered the chapel and a medieval graveyard at the spot associated with the Easter Bottle Kicking custom. The Council for British Archaeology summarises the importance of the site as a place where medieval pilgrimage, nearby Iron Age ritual activity and the modern Easter custom meet in the same landscape.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archaeologyuk.org/resource/buildings--burials-and-bones--the-story-of-hallaton-s-lost-pilgrims-chapel.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archaeologyuk.org">[Archaeology UK]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archaeologyuk.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">buildings burials and bones the story of hallaton s lost pilgrims chapel</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">buildings burials and bones the story of hallaton s lost pilgrims chapel</span></span></span>

This does not prove that Bottle Kicking is prehistoric. Folklore pages often overstate continuity when a custom takes place on an old or sacred site. The stronger, more careful point is that Hallaton’s custom has attached itself to a hill already rich in religious and communal memory. University of Leicester Archaeological Services reported that geophysical survey and excavation revealed the chapel walls, tiled floor, masonry, plaster, window lead and coins dating from the 12th to the 16th centuries. That gives Hare Pie Bank a documented medieval depth quite apart from the later Easter sport.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ulasnews.com/2014/09/16/hallatons-lost-chapel/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ulasnews.com">[ULAS News]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ulasnews.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ULAS News Hallaton&#x27;s Lost Chapel</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ULAS News Hallaton&#x27;s Lost Chapel</span></span></span>

The Diocese of Leicester’s account of St Morrell’s Round adds another layer: the chapel beneath the place where the Easter Bottle Kicking starts was built around 1250 and became a pilgrimage destination for several centuries before pilgrimage was suppressed during the Reformation. This matters because it helps explain why Hallaton’s folklore feels so place-bound. The custom is not floating free; it is performed on ground already associated with gathering, devotion, movement and memory.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.leicester.anglican.org/news/launch-of-st-morrells-round-by-the-bishop-of-leicester.php" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: leicester.anglican.org">[Diocese of Leicester]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">leicester.anglican.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">launch of st morrells round by the bishop of leicester</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">launch of st morrells round by the bishop of leicester</span></span></span>

Local planning and heritage material has gone further, describing the lost chapel as overlooking Hallaton and noting archaeological statements that Roman evidence beneath the medieval chapel suggests the hilltop has been a “special place” for more than 2,000 years. That is useful context, but it should be handled cautiously. It supports the idea of a long ritual landscape, not a simple unbroken line from Iron Age shrine to modern Bottle Kicking.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.harborough.gov.uk/download/downloads/id/6762/hallaton_appendix_10_rev2_22-2-21.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: harborough.gov.uk">[Harborough District Council]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">harborough.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Harborough District Council Buildings and structures of local significance</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Harborough District Council Buildings and structures of local significance</span></span></span>

## Origin stories, records and modern survival

The safest historical claim is that Hallaton’s Bottle Kicking is well attested as a long-standing Easter Monday custom, with records generally said to reach at least the late 18th century. Modern local reporting describes it as dating back to the late 18th century, while BBC Archive material refers to an annual Easter Monday battle since 1770. These claims place the custom firmly among England’s older surviving seasonal village contests, even if its deeper origin remains debated.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://harboroughfm.co.uk/all-set-for-hallaton-bottle-kicking-spectacular/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: harboroughfm.co.uk">[HFM]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">harboroughfm.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">HFMAll Set For Hallaton Bottle Kicking Spectacular – HFM</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">HFMAll Set For Hallaton Bottle Kicking Spectacular – HFM</span></span></span>

The origin stories are more varied than the public retellings sometimes suggest. The bull-and-hare tale explains the hare pie and the charitable dole. The Medbourne theft tale explains the rivalry. The “No pie – no parson” story explains the custom’s survival against clerical disapproval. Some modern accounts also connect the hare with older spring or Easter symbolism, but such pagan readings are much harder to prove than the documented parish, procession and village-contest elements.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.medbourne.org.uk/HTML/bottle-kicking---history-or-legend.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: medbourne.org.uk">[medbourne.org.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">medbourne.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Medbourne Bottle Kicking</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Medbourne Bottle Kicking</span></span></span>

That uncertainty is part of the custom’s folklore value. A living tradition does not survive only because an archive can prove every stage of its origin. It survives because people keep finding reasons to perform it: as local rivalry, as Easter gathering, as spectacle, as inherited obligation, as village identity and as Leicestershire distinctiveness. Hallaton Museum’s description is especially revealing here: it acknowledges that the origins are “shrouded” and contested, but stresses that present-day villagers, visitors and contestants still enjoy the annual day.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://hallaton-museum.co.uk/a-tale-of-rituals-relics-and-restoration/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: hallaton-museum.co.uk">[Hallaton Museum]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">hallaton-museum.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on hallaton-museum.co.uk.</span></span></span>

The custom has also adapted. The hare pie is commonly reported today as being made from beef rather than hare, and the scramble itself is less central for some spectators than the bottle contest that follows. Yet the names and gestures remain: hare pie, penny loaves, blessing, barrels, Hare Pie Bank, Butter Cross. In folklore terms, this is continuity through performance rather than fossilised accuracy. The custom changes enough to keep going, but not so much that it loses its recognisable shape.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.countryfile.com/go-outdoors/walks/bottle-kicking-in-hallaton-leicestershire" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: countryfile.com">[Countryfile]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">countryfile.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Bottle Kicking in Hallaton, Leicestershire</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Bottle Kicking in Hallaton, Leicestershire</span></span></span>

Its modern survival is also practical, organised and public-facing. Recent local coverage gives timetabled elements such as the Medbourne parade, the church service, the Hare Pie parade from the Fox Inn and the assembly near the bottle-kicking field. ## Why Hallaton Bottle Kicking matters to Leicestershire folklore

Hallaton Bottle Kicking matters because it shows Leicestershire folklore in action rather than only in story. Many county legends survive as tales attached to a place: [Black Annis]({{ 'black-annis/' | relative_url }}) at the Dane Hills, ghost stories in old buildings, saintly memory at holy sites. Hallaton is different because the tradition still happens in public, on a fixed seasonal day, with named roles, objects, routes and rival villages. It is not only remembered; it is enacted.

The custom also demonstrates how Christian practice, local legend and rough festivity can coexist. A parish church blessing sits beside a scramble for pie. A charitable dole becomes a competitive drama. Beer barrels become ritual objects. A village rivalry becomes a repeated Easter performance. That combination is why the event belongs naturally within a county folklore map rather than only a sports-history one.

It also gives Leicestershire a distinctive answer to better-known mass games elsewhere in Britain, such as Shrovetide football traditions or the Haxey Hood. Hallaton’s version is smaller in geography but unusually rich in symbolic furniture: the hare, the pie, the loaves, the bottles, the Butter Cross, Hare Pie Bank and the rivalry with Medbourne. The event’s strength lies in that density. It is not merely a fight over a barrel; it is a whole Easter grammar of food, procession, place and belonging.

Above all, Hallaton Bottle Kicking is a reminder that folklore is not always delicate, ancient-looking or safely picturesque. Sometimes it is noisy, muddy, bruising and disputed. Its history cannot be reduced to a single origin story, and its oldness should not be exaggerated beyond the evidence. But as a living Easter custom, rooted in Hallaton’s landscape and kept alive by repeated local performance, it remains one of Leicestershire’s most vivid traditional survivals.
