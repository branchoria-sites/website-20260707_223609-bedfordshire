## Introduction

Trows are [Orkney]({{ 'where-orkneys-stones-meet-the-sea/' | relative_url }})’s underground folk: mischievous, dangerous and strangely musical beings said to live in mounds, ruined places and old earthworks. They are not simply “fairies” in a soft modern sense. In Orcadian tradition they belong to a harder Northern Isles world of night travel, hidden halls, stolen children, uncanny fiddlers and ancient grassy knowes that people once avoided after dark. The Dictionaries of the Scots Language define a trow as a mischievous sprite or fairy, linked with Scandinavian mythology and especially with Shetland and Orkney folk belief; the same entry notes hill, land and sea trows according to their supposed haunts.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.dsl.ac.uk/entry/snd/trow_n1" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: dsl.ac.uk">[dsl.ac.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">dsl.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Dictionaries of the Scots Language:: SND:: trow n1</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Dictionaries of the Scots Language:: SND:: trow n1</span></span></span>



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/Orkney_9ae683_trows_haunted_mounds_f19664-overview.webp" | relative_url }}" alt="Overview image for Trows" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

The reason trow stories matter in Orkney is that they turn the islands’ old mounds into inhabited places. A grassy rise might be a chambered tomb, a broch mound, a natural hillock or a place only vaguely understood by later generations, but folklore could make it active: a door into another society under the ground. That does not mean trow lore preserves direct Neolithic belief. It means later Orcadians interpreted a landscape crowded with ancient remains through the supernatural language available to them: Norse-influenced mound-dwellers, household danger, music, taboo and the sense that some places should not be disturbed.

## What trows are in Orkney tradition

In Orkney and Shetland, trows sit somewhere between trolls, fairies, goblins and the dead-under-the-hill, but none of those English labels quite fits. The word itself belongs to the Northern Isles dialect world. The Scots dictionary gives forms such as trow, trowe and trouw, and explains the term as a Scots form connected with “troll”; it also records older descriptions of sea-trows, land-trows and other local varieties.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.dsl.ac.uk/entry/snd/trow_n1" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: dsl.ac.uk">[dsl.ac.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">dsl.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Dictionaries of the Scots Language:: SND:: trow n1</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Dictionaries of the Scots Language:: SND:: trow n1</span></span></span>

Orcadian trows are usually best understood by their behaviour rather than by a fixed appearance. They come out at night. They enter houses. They resent carelessness around hearth, water, babies and livestock. They love music and dancing. They may invite, trick or carry off humans, especially fiddlers or the unwary. Their world is close enough to the human world to overlap with it, but it is governed by different rules.

The older material does not present them as charming little sprites. In nineteenth- and early twentieth-century collections, trow tales often involve risk: a child nearly stolen, a cow “changed”, a person paralysed after calling too often to the hidden folk, or a household punished because ordinary protective customs were neglected. One collected account says trows expected a clean hearth and clean water on Saturday night; when these were lacking, trow-wives entered the house, washed a strange child in household liquid, spoiled the food supply and fled only when a watching boy was forced to blink.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/stream/countyfolklore03folkuoft/countyfolklore03folkuoft_djvu.txt" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Internet Archive Full text of &quot;County folklore</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Internet Archive Full text of &quot;County folklore</span></span></span>

That mixture of domestic detail and supernatural threat is important. Trow stories did not only explain strange mounds out in the fields. They also gave shape to ordinary anxieties: whether a baby had been properly protected, whether the byre had been blessed, whether the house was clean, whether anyone had mocked powers that should be treated carefully.

## Why mounds and ruins became trowie places

Orkney is unusually rich in visible prehistoric remains, and that matters for its folklore. Historic Environment Scotland describes Maeshowe as a monumental chambered tomb built around 5,000 years ago and set within one of Europe’s richest Neolithic landscapes, surrounded by stone circles, villages and burial monuments.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.historicenvironment.scot/visit/all/maeshowe-chambered-cairn/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: historicenvironment.scot">[Historic Environment Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">historicenvironment.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Historic Environment Scotland Maeshowe Chambered Cairn &#124; Historic Scotland</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Historic Environment Scotland Maeshowe Chambered Cairn &#124; Historic Scotland</span></span></span> From outside, Maeshowe looks like a large grassy mound, and Historic Environment Scotland notes that “howe” comes from the Old Norse word for hill.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.historicenvironment.scot/visit/all/maeshowe-chambered-cairn/history-and-stories/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: historicenvironment.scot">[Historic Environment Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">historicenvironment.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Historic Environment Scotland History and stories &#124; Maeshowe &#124; Historic Scotland</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Historic Environment Scotland History and stories &#124; Maeshowe &#124; Historic Scotland</span></span></span>

That visual fact is central to trow lore. A mound is not just a lump of earth when a community knows, suspects or imagines that something lies inside it. It may have a passage, a chamber, bones, treasure, old stonework or simply a reputation. Folklore makes that uncertainty socially meaningful. It says: do not dig without care; do not mock; do not pass alone after dark; do not assume the old place is empty.

The Orkney trow tradition therefore works as a kind of haunted-landscape mechanism. It does not need every trowie mound to be an actual tomb. Some places associated with trows were archaeological mounds, while others were natural or geological formations that looked like they might contain a hidden world. What mattered was the mound’s presence in the lived landscape: raised, grassy, old-looking, prominent and slightly apart from ordinary ground.

Maeshowe also shows how different periods of Orkney memory can sit inside one mound. The tomb belongs to Neolithic Orkney, but Historic Environment Scotland records that Norsemen entered it about 3,000 years after it had been closed, leaving the largest collection of runic inscriptions known outside Scandinavia and reminding visitors that Orkney remained under Norwegian rule until 1469.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.historicenvironment.scot/visit/all/maeshowe-chambered-cairn/history-and-stories/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: historicenvironment.scot">[Historic Environment Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">historicenvironment.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Historic Environment Scotland History and stories &#124; Maeshowe &#124; Historic Scotland</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Historic Environment Scotland History and stories &#124; Maeshowe &#124; Historic Scotland</span></span></span> Trow stories are not evidence for what Maeshowe’s builders believed, but they belong to the same long habit of later people re-entering, re-reading and re-imagining ancient places.

## The mound as doorway, warning and memory

The most useful way to read Orkney’s trowie mounds is not as a map of literal “haunted sites”, but as a set of rules for living beside older places. The mound becomes three things at once.

First, it is a doorway. Stories often imagine a hidden interior: a hall, a tunnel, a gathering, a dance or a feast. The outside may be only grass and stone, but the tale gives it depth.

Second, it is a warning. A person who enters the mound may not return in ordinary time. A passer-by who speaks lightly of the hidden folk may suffer for it. A farmer who disturbs old ground may invite misfortune. This warning function is common in mound lore because it makes old places socially protected even before modern heritage law exists.

Third, it is memory. Orkney’s mounds preserve visible traces of people who came before, but ordinary islanders did not always have archaeological explanations for them. Trow stories filled that interpretive space. They did not need to be historically “accurate” to be meaningful; they kept old places alive in conversation.

This is why modern Orkney interpretation often still links archaeology and folklore. Orkney Islands Council’s 2019 call for artists for a “landscape of tales” exhibition explicitly named mound-dwellers, trows, fairies and hogboons alongside walking standing stones, giants, selkies, mermaids and fin folk as part of the connection between Orkney’s landscape and its stories.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.orkney.gov.uk/latest-news/call-for-artists-to-contribute-to-landscape-of-tales-exhibition/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: orkney.gov.uk">[orkney.gov.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">orkney.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Call for artists to contribute to ‘landscape of tales’ exhibition</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Call for artists to contribute to ‘landscape of tales’ exhibition</span></span></span>

## Music, dancing and the danger of joining in

One of the most memorable features of trow lore is music. Trows are repeatedly described as lovers of dancing, fiddling and revelry. In collected Northern Isles tradition, an old man who saw trows crossing marshy ground called out that he would ride with them; they carried him away for a year, returned him to his roof, and the tune associated with the encounter was remembered afterwards as one of the local reels.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/stream/countyfolklore03folkuoft/countyfolklore03folkuoft_djvu.txt" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Internet Archive Full text of &quot;County folklore</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Internet Archive Full text of &quot;County folklore</span></span></span>

Music makes the trows attractive as well as frightening. A silent monster only threatens. A musical hidden people invite imitation. Their tunes can be learned, their dances watched, their gatherings overheard. This is why fiddlers are so vulnerable in trow stories: they possess the very skill that the hidden folk value.

The Orkney tale of Tam Bichan, associated with Dingieshowe, follows this pattern in modern retelling. Map of Stories records Tom Muir telling the story of the talented Orcadian fiddler who accepts an invitation to play for the trows; the same page places the tale at Dingieshowe and describes trows as music-loving creatures dwelling in Orkney’s ancient mounds.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://mapofstories.scot/the-story-of-tam-bichan/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: mapofstories.scot">[mapofstories.scot]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">mapofstories.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-title">the story of tam bichan</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">the story of tam bichan</span></span></span> Orkneyology’s account of Dingieshowe similarly presents the mound as a notorious trow site and says local folklore made it the place where Tam Bichan disappeared into the mound.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.orkneyology.com/dingieshowe.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: orkneyology.com">[Orkneyology.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">orkneyology.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Dingieshowe, Orkney</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Dingieshowe, Orkney</span></span></span>

The emotional force of these stories lies in the bargain. The hidden hall offers music, drink, admiration and wonder. But the human player risks losing ordinary time, ordinary home and ordinary life. The mound is not just a stage; it is a threshold.

## Household danger: babies, byres and careless thresholds

Trow stories are not confined to lonely mounds. They often move between the mound and the house, which makes them feel more intimate than many ghost legends. The trows may come indoors when the family sleeps. They may interfere with babies, food, cattle or stored drink. They may punish neglect of old customs.

In one collected tale, a woman called Marie Kirstan is taken to a trow’s wife and sees the trows preparing a false substitute for a cow. When she returns home, her own cow is dead because the byre had not been properly protected; the tale explains that when trows steal something, they leave a resemblance of it behind.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/stream/countyfolklore03folkuoft/countyfolklore03folkuoft_djvu.txt" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Internet Archive Full text of &quot;County folklore</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Internet Archive Full text of &quot;County folklore</span></span></span> This is classic changeling logic, but applied to livestock as well as children.

Yule was a particularly charged time. A collected account says trows were extremely fond of dancing and tried to join human revels in mortal disguise; anyone not properly protected gave them power.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/stream/countyfolklore03folkuoft/countyfolklore03folkuoft_djvu.txt" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Internet Archive Full text of &quot;County folklore</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Internet Archive Full text of &quot;County folklore</span></span></span> Later in the same seasonal material, iron is displayed because trows cannot bear the sight of it, people move in groups rather than alone, infants are guarded, and wise women protect them until daybreak marks the end of the dangerous Yule period.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/stream/countyfolklore03folkuoft/countyfolklore03folkuoft_djvu.txt" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Internet Archive Full text of &quot;County folklore</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Internet Archive Full text of &quot;County folklore</span></span></span>

These details show how trow lore worked as practical social storytelling. It reinforced care around babies, animals, food, fire, water, darkness and festival disorder. The trow was not only “out there” under the mound. It was also what might enter when the boundaries of the household were weak.

## Who preserved the trow stories?

Much of what can now be read as “Orkney folklore” survived because nineteenth-century collectors, local writers and later storytellers preserved material that had been carried orally. Walter Traill Dennison is central here. The Bottle Imp describes Dennison, born in Sanday in 1825, as a major preserver of Orkney stories who listened to tales remembered by older people at a time when such stories were fading under social and religious pressure.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.thebottleimp.org.uk/2022/08/orkney-folk-tales-and-literature/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: thebottleimp.org.uk">[The Bottle Imp]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">thebottleimp.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Bottle Imp Orkney Folk Tales and Literature</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Bottle Imp Orkney Folk Tales and Literature</span></span></span>

Modern Orkney storytelling has also played a major role. Orkneyology notes that 1994, the centenary of Dennison’s death, led to renewed work around his folklore, including an exhibition and the 1995 publication of *Orkney Folklore & Sea Legends*, which helped make some of the tales available again.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.orkneyology.com/orkney-storytelling.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: orkneyology.com">[Orkneyology.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">orkneyology.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Orkney Storytelling Lives Again</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Orkney Storytelling Lives Again</span></span></span> NorthLink’s interview with Orcadian storyteller Tom Muir explains how that Dennison centenary work fed into later books, talks, the Orkney Storytelling Festival and the Orkney Folklore Trail, which uses place-based stories to help visitors understand the landscape rather than seeing only scenery.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.northlinkferries.co.uk/orkney-blog/orkney-folklore-trail/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: northlinkferries.co.uk">[NorthLink Ferries]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">northlinkferries.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">North Link Ferries The Orkney Folklore Trail &#124; North Link Ferries</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">North Link Ferries The Orkney Folklore Trail &#124; North Link Ferries</span></span></span>

That chain of preservation matters because trow lore is not a single untouched “ancient myth”. It is oral tradition filtered through collectors, dialect writing, local memory, printed retellings, museum work, storytelling festivals, tourism and digital trails. A responsible reading holds those layers together: old belief, antiquarian record, literary shaping and modern performance.

## Why trows are so Orcadian

Trows are found across the Northern Isles, and Shetland has its own rich trow traditions, but they feel especially at home in Orkney because Orkney’s landscape is so visibly mound-haunted. The islands contain famous prehistoric monuments, smaller knowes, brochs, chambered cairns, natural hillocks and ruined places that invite stories about hidden interiors.

The wider historical setting also matters. The 1901 *County Folklore* volume for Orkney and Shetland emphasised the islands’ Scandinavian settlement, their late attachment to Scotland, and the persistence of Norn speech into the post-medieval period.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/stream/countyfolklore03folkuoft/countyfolklore03folkuoft_djvu.txt" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Internet Archive Full text of &quot;County folklore</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Internet Archive Full text of &quot;County folklore</span></span></span> Trow lore belongs to that Northern Isles inheritance, but it was reshaped in Orcadian speech and local storytelling rather than simply carried unchanged from Norway.

This is why the trow is one of the clearest signs of Orkney’s distinct folklore identity. Other Scottish counties have fairy hills, haunted ruins and stories of household spirits. Orkney has those motifs too, but the trow gathers them into a recognisably Orcadian pattern: old Norse-influenced vocabulary, grassy mounds, music from under the hill, danger at Yule, anxious care for the byre and cradle, and the feeling that ancient places are not silent.

## How the haunted mounds are retold today

Today, most readers meet trows through books, storytelling events, local heritage interpretation, websites, walking routes and tourism material rather than through inherited household belief. That changes the tradition, but it does not make it meaningless. In fact, trow stories are especially suited to modern Orkney because they help connect archaeology with imagination without claiming that one proves the other.

A visitor can stand before a mound such as Maeshowe and learn its archaeological significance: Neolithic engineering, winter-solstice alignment, Norse runes and World Heritage status.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.historicenvironment.scot/visit/all/maeshowe-chambered-cairn/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: historicenvironment.scot">[Historic Environment Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">historicenvironment.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Historic Environment Scotland Maeshowe Chambered Cairn &#124; Historic Scotland</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Historic Environment Scotland Maeshowe Chambered Cairn &#124; Historic Scotland</span></span></span> Nearby or elsewhere in the islands, the same visitor may hear of trows, hidden halls and fiddlers lost under the hill. The two kinds of knowledge should not be confused, but together they explain why Orkney’s old places feel so culturally charged.

The best modern retellings keep that balance. They do not ask the reader to believe that trows literally live under every mound. They show how Orcadians once made sense of ancient, uncanny and sometimes dangerous-looking places; how stories protected thresholds; how music and festivity could become risky; and how a landscape of archaeology also became a landscape of memory.

Trow lore endures because it gives Orkney’s mounds a second life. Archaeology tells us that people built, used, entered and reused these places across thousands of years. Folklore tells us that later islanders still felt them watching back.
