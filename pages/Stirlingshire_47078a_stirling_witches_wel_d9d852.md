## Introduction

Stirling’s witch-trial stories are not best understood as spooky tales about magical villains. They are fragments of persecution history, in which ordinary healing practices, neighbourly fear, religious suspicion and criminal law met in a dangerous way. The strongest [Stirlingshire]({{ 'where-stirlingshire-crosses-into-legend/' | relative_url }}) example is the 1659 case of Bessie Stevenson, linked to St Ninian’s Well at Wellgreen: a real spring, a civic water source, a remembered holy well, and the place where washing the clothes of the sick could be recast as witchcraft. The University of Edinburgh’s Survey of Scottish Witchcraft records 3,837 known accused people in Scotland between 1563 and 1736, while modern Stirling heritage accounts place Bessie’s case within the Tolbooth and Wellgreen landscape of the old burgh.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://witches.hca.ed.ac.uk/faq/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: witches.hca.ed.ac.uk">[witches.hca.ed.ac.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">witches.hca.ed.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Survey of Scottish Witchcraft</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Survey of Scottish Witchcraft</span></span></span>



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/Stirlingshire_47078a_stirling_witches_wel_d9d852-overview.webp" | relative_url }}" alt="Overview image for Witches" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

That is why this corner of Stirlingshire matters. It shows how folklore survives inside legal records: not as proof that witches existed, but as evidence that people believed in charms, healing water, transferred illness and dangerous words. St Ninian’s Well is still a named historic site at Wellgreen Place, recorded by Historic Environment Scotland’s Trove database as a holy well in the former county of Stirlingshire.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.trove.scot/place/46210" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: trove.scot">[Trove Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">trove.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on trove.scot.</span></span></span>

## Why Stirling’s witch stories gather around wells

Holy wells sit at an awkward meeting point between religion, medicine and folklore. In pre-modern Scotland, water could be practical, sacred and medicinal at the same time: a source for washing, drinking, blessing, cleansing and curing. Stirling City Heritage Trust describes wells as important focal points in Scotland and notes the wider association of water with life and healing; St Ninian’s Well is its clearest Stirling example, located in Wellgreen Place and protected by an unassuming stone structure.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.stirlingcityheritagetrust.org/blog/stirlings-ancient-wells" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: stirlingcityheritagetrust.org">[stirlingcityheritagetrust.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">stirlingcityheritagetrust.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">stirlings ancient wells</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">stirlings ancient wells</span></span></span>

The well’s later fabric is not a romantic ruin invented for tourists. Historic Environment Scotland’s place record gives the site as “Stirling, Wellgreen Place, St Ninian’s Well”, places it in the parish of Stirling and former county of Stirlingshire, and identifies its National Record of the Historic Environment ID as 46210. The same record notes the OS grid reference NS 79690 93012 and describes earlier accounts of a small building over the spring, with a lower vaulted chamber and a square recess where the spring rises.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.trove.scot/place/46210" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: trove.scot">[Trove Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">trove.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on trove.scot.</span></span></span>

This matters for folklore because the setting was not remote or theatrical. It was close to ordinary urban life. Stirling City Heritage Trust says the burgh decided after 1737 that a common washing house should be built at St Ninian’s Well for local inhabitants, and nineteenth-century descriptions still associated the structure with domestic washing.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.stirlingcityheritagetrust.org/blog/stirlings-ancient-wells" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: stirlingcityheritagetrust.org">[stirlingcityheritagetrust.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">stirlingcityheritagetrust.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">stirlings ancient wells</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">stirlings ancient wells</span></span></span>

That practical use helps explain the logic of the charm attributed to Bessie Stevenson. Washing the clothes of a sick person in running or sacred water is not hard to understand as folk medicine: the illness is imagined as something that can be loosened, removed or transferred. But in a witch-trial setting, the same action could be read as illicit ritual, especially if it involved words, repetition, saintly water, Catholic-looking practice, or a suspected power to pass harm from one person to another.

## Bessie Stevenson and the Stirling Tolbooth cases

The most important named figure in this Stirlingshire cluster is Bessie Stevenson, also spelled Stivenson or Stevinson in modern retellings. The University of Edinburgh Survey of Scottish Witchcraft records her trial and says she confessed to “healing with deeds” but denied witchcraft or “using of words”. The same record notes that witnesses were summoned in March 1659 and that she pleaded not guilty.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://witches.hca.ed.ac.uk/trial/T/LA/1130" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: witches.hca.ed.ac.uk">[witches.hca.ed.ac.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">witches.hca.ed.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on ed.ac.uk.</span></span></span>

Local heritage retellings connect Bessie with the Tolbooth in Stirling. The Women in Stirling Trail says Bessie Stevenson, Magdalena Blair and Isobel Bennet were tried on witchcraft-related charges at the Tolbooth in 1659, and describes the case as Stirling’s largest witch trial, involving 12 people. It also states that Bessie confessed to charms and folk cures at St Ninian’s Well, including washing the clothes of the sick and transferring disease to the clothes.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.yourstirling.com/women-in-stirling-trail/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: yourstirling.com">[Your Stirling]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">yourstirling.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Your Stirling Women in Stirling Trail</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Your Stirling Women in Stirling Trail</span></span></span>

The detail that makes the story memorable is the healing method itself. The Survey of Scottish Witchcraft’s case summary says the practice involved washing clothes in St Ninian’s Well for healing, with the outcome imagined in stark either/or terms: the rite would “end them or mend them”. It also records that Bessie was said to have learned the practice from Lady McFarlane’s daughter 24 years earlier.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://witches.hca.ed.ac.uk/case/C/EGD/317" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: witches.hca.ed.ac.uk">[witches.hca.ed.ac.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">witches.hca.ed.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on ed.ac.uk.</span></span></span>

Modern accounts differ slightly in how confidently they describe the outcome. Stirling City Heritage Trust says Bessie was found guilty but that her fate is not recorded in the material it summarises; a nursing-history project list based on the Scottish witchcraft data identifies Bessie Stevenson as a Stirling widow, about 49 years old, characterised as a folk healer using unorthodox religious practice and white magic, with the outcome “guilty” and “executed”.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.stirlingcityheritagetrust.org/blog/stirlings-witches" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: stirlingcityheritagetrust.org">[stirlingcityheritagetrust.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">stirlingcityheritagetrust.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Stirling’s Witches</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Stirling’s Witches</span></span></span>

That tension is worth keeping visible. For public folklore, the point is not to smooth every record into one dramatic certainty. The responsible version is this: Bessie was a real accused woman in the 1659 Stirling witchcraft proceedings; her case is strongly associated with St Ninian’s Well and folk healing; she was found guilty in some later source summaries; and at least one specialist-derived modern list records her as executed, while one local heritage account is more cautious about the surviving record of her fate.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://witches.hca.ed.ac.uk/trial/T/LA/1130" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: witches.hca.ed.ac.uk">[ed.ac.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">witches.hca.ed.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on ed.ac.uk.</span></span></span>

## St Ninian’s Well, charms and contested healing

St Ninian’s Well shows how a place can move between categories: saint’s site, civic water supply, wash house, healing spring, heritage monument and witch-trial landmark. The official Trove record classifies it as a holy well and notes that it is linked to the former county of Stirlingshire; it also preserves older antiquarian observations about the building over the spring and the possibility, not conclusively proven, that St Ninian’s Chapel and St Ninian’s Well may once have shared the same site.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.trove.scot/place/46210" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: trove.scot">[Trove Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">trove.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on trove.scot.</span></span></span>

The saintly name matters. “St Ninian” and the older form “St Ringan” occur widely in Scottish place-name and devotional traditions, and local holy wells often gathered stories of healing, blessing and cure. But by the seventeenth century, especially after the Scottish Reformation, practices that looked like unofficial saintly healing could be treated with suspicion. A 2024 open-access study of Scottish healers and midwives accused of witchcraft argues that, after the Protestant Reformation, many folk-healing practices were marginalised and treated as unorthodox or harmful, especially when associated with women or with practices that seemed to echo Catholic ritual.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0260691723003209" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on sciencedirect.com.</span></span></span>

Bessie’s alleged actions fit that wider pattern. The reported charm did not depend on a monster, fairy queen or midnight sabbath. It used water, clothing, bodily illness and the idea of transfer. In modern language, it looks like a cleansing or sympathetic cure: the garment stands in for the sick body, the water carries away disease, and the healer mediates the danger. In a witchcraft prosecution, however, the same ingredients could become evidence of hidden power.

That is the central ambiguity in Stirling’s witch-and-well tradition. The “folklore” is not a free-floating legend later attached to the site; it is partly preserved because the legal system treated folk healing as suspicious enough to record. The court’s fear gives us evidence of ordinary belief, but it also distorts that evidence. We hear the healing practice through the voices of interrogation, accusation and prosecution rather than through Bessie’s own safe, voluntary explanation.

## How to tell folklore from persecution history

A useful rule for Stirling’s witch material is to separate three things that are often blurred together in popular retellings.

**First, there is the landscape evidence.** St Ninian’s Well is a real place, officially recorded at Wellgreen Place, with a long civic and antiquarian history. Its identity as a holy well, wash-house site and former Stirlingshire landmark is not dependent on believing any supernatural claim.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.trove.scot/place/46210" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: trove.scot">[Trove Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">trove.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on trove.scot.</span></span></span>

**Second, there is the trial evidence.** The Survey of Scottish Witchcraft records Bessie Stevenson’s case, including the claim that she healed by deeds, the March 1659 trial process and the link to washing clothes at St Ninian’s Well. This is legal evidence about accusation and confession, not neutral folklore collection.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://witches.hca.ed.ac.uk/trial/T/LA/1130" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: witches.hca.ed.ac.uk">[witches.hca.ed.ac.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">witches.hca.ed.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on ed.ac.uk.</span></span></span>

**Third, there is modern memorial retelling.** Heritage trails, local history blogs and modern witchcraft maps now retell Bessie’s story as part of Stirling’s women’s history, social history and dark heritage. The Women in Stirling Trail uses the Tolbooth stop to connect Bessie, Magdalena Blair and Isobel Bennet with the wider Scottish pattern of witchcraft accusation, and points readers towards the University of Edinburgh’s interactive mapping work.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.yourstirling.com/women-in-stirling-trail/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: yourstirling.com">[Your Stirling]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">yourstirling.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Your Stirling Women in Stirling Trail</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Your Stirling Women in Stirling Trail</span></span></span>

This distinction changes the tone of the story. It is tempting to market witch-trial places as haunted curiosities, but the stronger reading is more human and more unsettling. A woman associated with healing was drawn into a system that could turn care, washing, charm-making and neighbourly reputation into capital danger.

## Why the Stirling cases still matter in Stirlingshire folklore

Stirling’s witch-trial material belongs in Stirlingshire folklore because it captures a recurring county pattern: charged places at [thresholds]({{ 'thresholds/' | relative_url }}). The castle rock, old bridge, Tolbooth and Wellgreen all sit in a landscape where authority, movement, water and memory overlap. St Ninian’s Well is especially powerful because it is modest. It is not a grand ruin, but a small urban survival where the sound of running water can still make the past feel close in modern local accounts.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://stirlingarchaeology.substack.com/p/stirlings-five-biggest-secrets" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: stirlingarchaeology.substack.com">[stirlingarchaeology.substack.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">stirlingarchaeology.substack.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Stirling&#x27;s Five Biggest Secret Gems</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Stirling&#x27;s Five Biggest Secret Gems</span></span></span>

The case also helps correct a common misunderstanding about “witches” in local legend. Many accused people were not accused because they resembled later fantasy witches. They were healers, charmers, quarrelsome neighbours, vulnerable widows, women with reputations, or people drawn into chains of accusation. Historic Environment Scotland notes that the 1563 Witchcraft Act brought the death penalty for offences and that Scottish witch trials often used confession, neighbour testimony and invasive procedures such as pricking; it also says the fate of many accused people remains unknown and that the Survey estimates about two-thirds were executed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://blog.historicenvironment.scot/2022/06/the-witchcraft-act-and-its-impact-in-scotland/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: blog.historicenvironment.scot">[Historic Environment Scotland Blog]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">blog.historicenvironment.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on historicenvironment.scot.</span></span></span>

For readers of folklore, this is where the subject becomes ethically tricky. Witch-trial records preserve beliefs in charms, healing wells, disease transfer, curses and supernatural danger. But they preserve them under pressure. A charm recorded in a trial is not the same as a tale told freely at a fireside. It may be exaggerated, mistranslated into legal language, reshaped by ministers and officials, or extracted through fear.

That does not make the folklore worthless. It makes it more important to read carefully. Bessie Stevenson’s story lets modern Stirlingshire remember both sides at once: the old belief that water, cloth and ritual action could heal, and the historical fact that such belief could be used to punish real people. In that sense, St Ninian’s Well is not simply a “witches’ well”. It is a healing place whose memory was scarred by a witch trial.

## What remains at Wellgreen today

Today, the visitor looking for this story is not searching for a dramatic execution site or a preserved seventeenth-century courtroom scene. The key landscape anchors are Stirling’s Tolbooth area and St Ninian’s Well at Wellgreen Place. The current Tolbooth is a public arts and events venue, while heritage interpretation remembers that earlier Tolbooth buildings functioned as places of imprisonment, trial and civic authority.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.yourstirling.com/women-in-stirling-trail/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: yourstirling.com">[Your Stirling]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">yourstirling.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Your Stirling Women in Stirling Trail</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Your Stirling Women in Stirling Trail</span></span></span>

St Ninian’s Well itself is easy to miss. Official records locate it precisely, but its power as folklore lies partly in that hidden quality: a small structure in the modern city, tied to water that once served everyday life and to a story in which everyday healing became criminal evidence. The Trove record’s description of remaining walls, earlier chambers and the square recess where the spring rises gives the site a physical weight that purely legendary witch stories often lack.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.trove.scot/place/46210" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: trove.scot">[Trove Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">trove.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on trove.scot.</span></span></span>

The most careful modern retelling is therefore not “Bessie Stevenson was a witch who used a magic well”. It is: Bessie Stevenson was one of the real people accused in Stirling’s seventeenth-century witchcraft proceedings; the surviving record links her to folk healing at St Ninian’s Well; and the case shows how a healing tradition attached to a local water source could be transformed, under early modern law and fear, into a charge of witchcraft.
