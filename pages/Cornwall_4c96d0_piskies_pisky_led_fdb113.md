## Introduction

To be pisky-led in Cornish tradition is to lose ordinary direction: a path that should be familiar seems to shift, a traveller circles the same field, a light or sound draws someone into danger, or time itself appears to slip. The piskies are [Cornwall]({{ 'where-cornwalls-landscape-turns-into/' | relative_url }})’s best-known fairy beings, but the important point is not simply that they are “mischievous”. The pisky-led tale explains a particular kind of bewilderment in a particular landscape: moors, lanes, cliffs, woods, farm tracks and old stone country where bad weather, darkness and local memory could make a short journey feel uncanny.



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/Cornwall_4c96d0_piskies_pisky_led_fdb113-overview.webp" | relative_url }}" alt="Overview image for Piskies" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

The tradition is strongest in nineteenth-century collections and later retellings, especially around Robert Hunt, William Bottrell, M. A. Courtney and T. Q. Couch. It also survives because the phrase still works. To say someone has been pisky-led is half joke, half folk diagnosis: a way of admitting that Cornwall’s old places can still unsettle confidence, even when the modern reader no longer takes the piskies literally.

## What being pisky-led means

Being pisky-led is not just “getting lost”. In the old accounts, the lost person often knows where the path ought to be but cannot reach it. M. A. Courtney’s nineteenth-century account says a mischievous pisky might lead benighted people over hedges and ditches, or round and round the same field, while the path remained visible but somehow unusable; the remedy she records is to turn stockings, or a pocket, inside out. She also preserves the dry local joke that “Pisky led is often whiskey led,” showing that even in the collecting period the belief could be treated with humour as well as fear.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Folk-Lore_Journal/Volume_5/Cornish_Folk-Lore_%28pp._177-220%29" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: en.wikisource.org">[Wikisource]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">en.wikisource.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Folk-Lore Journal/Volume 5/Cornish Folk-Lore (pp.</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Folk-Lore Journal/Volume 5/Cornish Folk-Lore (pp.</span></span></span>

That mixture matters. Pisky-leading was a supernatural explanation for a real experience: night walking, fog, drink, fatigue, poor light, marshy ground, unfamiliar lanes and the sudden panic of disorientation. It did not need a grand vision of fairyland. A person might simply go out after dark and return late, muddied, embarrassed and convinced that something had interfered with their senses.

Robert Hunt’s *Popular Romances of the West of England* gives the most compact version of the mechanism. He describes the Cornish pisky as an unsociable, mischievous sprite whose favourite trick is to entice people into bogs by appearing like a cottage light or a man carrying a lantern. Hunt adds that a traveller who lost his way might say he was piskay-led, and that turning a garment inside out was believed to break the charm.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://sacred-texts.com/neu/eng/prwe/prwe023.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sacred-texts.com">[Internet Sacred Text Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sacred-texts.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on sacred-texts.com.</span></span></span>

So the “problem” of being pisky-led has three parts:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **A false guide:** a light, sound, figure, path or impulse that seems trustworthy.
* **A broken sense of place:** the victim cannot move through the landscape normally, even when familiar markers are nearby.
* **A counter-charm or interruption:** turned clothing, outside voices, daylight, prayer, common sense or sheer accident returns the person to ordinary direction.

</div>

This is why pisky-leading belongs so naturally to Cornwall. It turns the county’s physical texture into story: field boundaries, boggy patches, cliff paths, moorland tracks, mine country, wind-bent lanes and ancient stones become places where the human sense of direction is not quite sovereign.

## Why piskies lead people astray

Pisky-leading is often described as mischief, but the tales are rarely random. They tend to punish arrogance, curiosity, greed, carelessness or failure to respect a place. In that sense, piskies are not only pranksters; they are agents of local warning.

Sometimes the offence is simply being abroad at the wrong time. The piskies belong to night, moonlight, lonely places and thresholds. A person travelling after dark is already crossing into their territory. Sometimes the offence is more moral: spying, mocking, taking fairy goods, interrupting fairy work, or trying to profit from the little people. Courtney’s account says piskies may help with threshing or household tasks, but if acknowledged too openly or rewarded with clothes they leave; if mischievous, they lure people into exhausting dances of misdirection.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Folk-Lore_Journal/Volume_5/Cornish_Folk-Lore_%28pp._177-220%29" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: en.wikisource.org">[Wikisource]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">en.wikisource.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Folk-Lore Journal/Volume 5/Cornish Folk-Lore (pp.</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Folk-Lore Journal/Volume 5/Cornish Folk-Lore (pp.</span></span></span>

Hunt’s wider fairy section makes the same point in a sharper form. He distinguishes the Cornish pisky from lighter Devon traditions, calling the Cornish version more cunning and less harmless, and he places piskies alongside spriggans, knockers, buccas and browneys rather than treating all “fairies” as one soft category.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://sacred-texts.com/neu/eng/prwe/prwe023.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sacred-texts.com">[Internet Sacred Text Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sacred-texts.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on sacred-texts.com.</span></span></span> That is not a scientific taxonomy, and later scholarship is cautious about taking such divisions too literally, but it shows how Victorian collectors heard Cornish fairy lore: not as a nursery decoration, but as a set of beings with power over homes, animals, work, mines and dangerous places.

The lead-astray motif also has a practical side. It gives memorable shape to local advice: do not follow strange lights over wet ground; do not wander alone at night; do not assume familiar terrain is safe in fog; do not boast that you are immune to old beliefs; do not treat ancient places as empty. The pisky story turns caution into entertainment, which is one reason it travels so well.

## Piskies, spriggans and shifting names

Modern readers often want a neat answer: are piskies the same as pixies, fairies, spriggans or knockers? Cornish sources resist that neatness. Hunt listed five varieties of fairy beings in Cornwall — Small People, spriggans, piskies or pigseys, buccas or knockers, and browneys — but his own descriptions show overlap in behaviour and setting.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://sacred-texts.com/neu/eng/prwe/prwe023.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sacred-texts.com">[Internet Sacred Text Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sacred-texts.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on sacred-texts.com.</span></span></span>

Courtney is even clearer about local variation. She says that in West Cornwall the pisky is often a solitary ragged little being, while in the east the name could be applied more broadly to other small people. She also records named figures from Polperro tradition, including Jack o’ the lantern and Joan the Wad, in a rhyme asking for light home in bad weather.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Folk-Lore_Journal/Volume_5/Cornish_Folk-Lore_%28pp._177-220%29" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: en.wikisource.org">[Wikisource]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">en.wikisource.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Folk-Lore Journal/Volume 5/Cornish Folk-Lore (pp.</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Folk-Lore Journal/Volume 5/Cornish Folk-Lore (pp.</span></span></span>

This matters because pisky-leading sits at the edge of several traditions. It can look like a will-o’-the-wisp story when the pisky appears as a misleading light. It can become a fairy-abduction story when the lost person enters an underground or otherworldly realm. It can touch spriggan lore when ancient stones, treasure, theft or punishment are involved. It can even resemble household fairy lore when the pisky is a helper who turns troublesome if treated wrongly.

Ronald M. James’s modern study of Cornish folklore treats this slipperiness as part of the evidence rather than a problem to be tidied away. The University of Exeter Press describes his work as showing that Cornwall has a distinct body of oral tradition even when its legends and folktales resemble those elsewhere; the book’s contents include chapters on piskies, spriggans and bucca, and on piskies in migratory legends.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.exeterpress.co.uk/products/the-folklore-of-cornwall" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: exeterpress.co.uk">[University of Exeter Press]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">exeterpress.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Folklore of Cornwall – University of Exeter Press...</span></span></span> A Journal of Folklore Research review notes that James works mainly from nineteenth-century collections, especially Hunt and Bottrell, and examines how Cornish forms of fairy beings vary by name, function and narrative pattern.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://scholarworks.iu.edu/journals/index.php/jfrr/article/view/39894" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: scholarworks.iu.edu">[IUScholarWorks]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">scholarworks.iu.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Scholar Works</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Elissa R. Henken - Review of Ronald M. James, The Folklore of Cornwall: The Oral Tradition of a Celtic Nation &#124; Journal of Folklore Resea...</span></span></span>

The safest reading, then, is this: “pisky” is not a rigid species label. It is a Cornish name used in a living oral world where stories shifted by parish, teller, dialect, collector and purpose. Being pisky-led is one of the clearest mechanisms attached to that name.

## The landscape that makes pisky-leading believable

Pisky-leading needs a landscape where a person can plausibly lose certainty. Cornwall supplies that in abundance, especially in the west: granite moors, prehistoric stones, narrow lanes, old churchtown routes, mine workings, marshy ground, wind and sudden mist. The tales do not float above the county; they cling to routes and landmarks.

One of the most evocative examples is the William Noy tradition associated with Treverven and the St Buryan area. A modern local-history account summarises Bottrell’s tale as the story of William Noy of Pendrea travelling on horseback from St Buryan Churchtown towards Burnewhall Farm, a route that would have taken him past Treverven Standing Stone. The account is careful to stress why the story feels unusually grounded: named people, farms and places give the fairy episode a real topography.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cornishbirdblog.com/treverven-standing-stone-being-piskie-led/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cornishbirdblog.com">[The Cornish Bird]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cornishbirdblog.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Cornish Bird Treverven Standing Stone &amp; being Piskie-Led</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Cornish Bird Treverven Standing Stone &amp; being Piskie-Led</span></span></span>

The landscape itself supports that kind of storytelling. Historic England’s National Heritage List records many protected prehistoric monuments in Cornwall, and its entries for standing stones and alignments show how visible these ancient features remain in the county’s fields and uplands.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://historicengland.org.uk/listing/the-list/list-entry/1021223" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: historicengland.org.uk">[Historic England]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">historicengland.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on historicengland.org.uk.</span></span></span> Around St Buryan, nearby stone circles, menhirs, crosses, wells and old tracks create exactly the kind of memory-rich terrain where folklore can attach itself to a journey.

In pisky-led stories, such places are not simply “spooky”. They act as hinges between ordinary and extraordinary geography. A stone can be a landmark, a meeting point, a warning, a fairy threshold or a later storyteller’s anchor. That is why the motif is so durable: even when belief fades, the landscape still gives the story somewhere to stand.

## When pisky-leading becomes fairyland

Not every pisky-led tale ends in terror. Some become stories of enchantment, chosen children and glimpses of another world. Cornwall Heritage Trust’s version of “The Pisky-Led Boy” tells of Peder Prynne of St Allen parish, who follows strange music into a wood, vanishes for three weeks, and is eventually found asleep on ferns. He says he has been with the piskies, who treated him kindly, and describes a hidden realm of water, music, riches and food.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cornwallheritagetrust.org/learn/resources/stories-and-rhymes/pisky-led-boy/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cornwallheritagetrust.org">[Cornwall Heritage Trust]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cornwallheritagetrust.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cornwallheritagetrust.org.</span></span></span>

That story is gentler than the bog-and-ditch version, but the mechanism is the same. A sound draws the child onwards. The ordinary wood thickens and darkens. He feels he should turn back, but the music becomes irresistible. By the time he returns, the village has experienced his absence as a crisis, while he has experienced it as a visit.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cornwallheritagetrust.org/learn/resources/stories-and-rhymes/pisky-led-boy/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cornwallheritagetrust.org">[Cornwall Heritage Trust]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cornwallheritagetrust.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cornwallheritagetrust.org.</span></span></span>

This kind of story does two things at once. It preserves the danger of being led away, especially for children, but it also keeps alive the older fairy idea that some people may be favoured rather than harmed. The difference often lies in character. Peder is innocent, dutiful and imaginative; the tale’s ending suggests that only those with purity and second sight receive kindness from the little people.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cornwallheritagetrust.org/learn/resources/stories-and-rhymes/pisky-led-boy/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cornwallheritagetrust.org">[Cornwall Heritage Trust]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cornwallheritagetrust.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cornwallheritagetrust.org.</span></span></span>

That is very different from the comic adult who is lured by wine, treasure or a prank. Pisky-leading can therefore be a punishment, a warning, a test, a joke or a strange blessing. What unites these forms is the loss of human control over direction.

## How old and well-attested is the tradition?

The strongest evidence for Cornish pisky-leading comes from nineteenth-century and early twentieth-century collecting, rather than from medieval documents. That does not mean the belief was invented then. It means that this is when antiquarians, local historians and folklore collectors wrote it down in quantity.

Hunt’s *Popular Romances* was first published in the nineteenth century and later editions present a wide range of Cornish drolls, traditions and superstitions. Its index alone shows how heavily fairy material features in the collection, including piskies, spriggans, fairy tools, changelings, fairy fairs and fairy revels.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://sacred-texts.com/neu/eng/prwe/index.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sacred-texts.com">[Internet Sacred Text Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sacred-texts.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on sacred-texts.com.</span></span></span> Courtney’s 1887 article in *The Folk-Lore Journal* adds another layer: she writes as someone reporting what “old folk” said, while also naming Bottrell and Couch as important sources for the tales.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Folk-Lore_Journal/Volume_5/Cornish_Folk-Lore_%28pp._177-220%29" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: en.wikisource.org">[Wikisource]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">en.wikisource.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Folk-Lore Journal/Volume 5/Cornish Folk-Lore (pp.</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Folk-Lore Journal/Volume 5/Cornish Folk-Lore (pp.</span></span></span>

The evidence is therefore good for a living or recently living nineteenth-century belief culture, but it should be handled carefully. Victorian collectors often rewrote, polished, moralised or romanticised what they heard. A modern review of James’s *The Folklore of Cornwall* stresses exactly this difficulty: Cornish material is relatively limited, much was recorded through nineteenth-century collectors, and assessing what is distinctively Cornish requires caution about romantic rewriting and borrowed motifs.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://scholarworks.iu.edu/journals/index.php/jfrr/article/view/39894" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: scholarworks.iu.edu">[IUScholarWorks]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">scholarworks.iu.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Scholar Works</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Elissa R. Henken - Review of Ronald M. James, The Folklore of Cornwall: The Oral Tradition of a Celtic Nation &#124; Journal of Folklore Resea...</span></span></span>

That caution makes the tradition more interesting, not less. Pisky-leading is not a single ancient doctrine passed down unchanged. It is a cluster of related explanations, jokes, warnings and stories that collectors caught at a moment when older rural storytelling was under pressure from schooling, print culture, transport, tourism and changing work.

## Cornwall, Devon and the question of difference

Piskies are not confined neatly to Cornwall. Pixy or pisky traditions are part of the wider south-western folklore of Cornwall and Devon, with echoes across other counties. The question is not whether Cornwall has the only such stories, but whether Cornish versions developed a recognisable local flavour.

Hunt thought they did. He contrasted the Cornish pisky with the Devon pixy, presenting the Cornish being as darker, more cunning and more dangerous.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://sacred-texts.com/neu/eng/prwe/prwe023.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sacred-texts.com">[Internet Sacred Text Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sacred-texts.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on sacred-texts.com.</span></span></span> Modern scholarship is more measured. Bernard Deacon’s discussion of Ronald James’s comparison of Devon and Cornish pixy narratives notes that the two counties share a common basis: little people, often knee-high, dancing at night in out-of-the-way places, sometimes helpful and sometimes harmful. But James finds subtle narrative differences, especially when far west Cornwall is compared with Devon.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://bernarddeacon.com/recent-articles-on-cornwall/what-can-piskey-tales-tell-us-about-cornish-difference/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: bernarddeacon.com">[Cornish studies resources]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">bernarddeacon.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on bernarddeacon.com.</span></span></span>

That distinction is important for a Cornwall page. It avoids two mistakes: treating piskies as generic “British fairies”, or pretending that every pisky motif is uniquely Cornish. The better view is that Cornwall shares a south-western fairy language but gives it local accents through place names, droll-teller style, mining and farming settings, and the cultural boundary of the Tamar.

Deacon’s summary of James also points to a possible west Cornwall pattern shaped by geography and the legacy of linguistic difference. Far west Cornwall was not sealed off from neighbouring traditions, but it had enough social and linguistic distinctiveness for stories to develop different emphases.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://bernarddeacon.com/recent-articles-on-cornwall/what-can-piskey-tales-tell-us-about-cornish-difference/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: bernarddeacon.com">[Cornish studies resources]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">bernarddeacon.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on bernarddeacon.com.</span></span></span> Pisky-leading therefore belongs both to a shared south-western motif and to Cornwall’s own sense of being a place where the land can resist outsiders, sceptics and overconfident travellers.

## Why the old charm turns clothing inside out

The inside-out charm is one of the most memorable details because it is so simple. No priest, book, specialist or elaborate ritual is needed. A traveller turns a coat, stocking or pocket inside out and the delusion breaks. Hunt records the belief that no pisky could harm a man if his coat were inside out, and that night travellers might wear a jacket or cloak that way as protection.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://sacred-texts.com/neu/eng/prwe/prwe023.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sacred-texts.com">[Internet Sacred Text Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sacred-texts.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on sacred-texts.com.</span></span></span> Courtney records the same logic in more domestic form: turning stockings or a pocket inside out ends the bewilderment.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Folk-Lore_Journal/Volume_5/Cornish_Folk-Lore_%28pp._177-220%29" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: en.wikisource.org">[Wikisource]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">en.wikisource.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Folk-Lore Journal/Volume 5/Cornish Folk-Lore (pp.</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Folk-Lore Journal/Volume 5/Cornish Folk-Lore (pp.</span></span></span>

The charm works symbolically because pisky-leading is a reversal of ordinary perception. The world has turned wrong: the visible path cannot be reached, the field will not end, the guiding light is false. Turning a garment inside out answers inversion with inversion. It is a small act of re-ordering, a way for the bewildered person to declare that the rules have changed back.

It also makes the story easy to remember. A good folk remedy is portable, cheap and vivid. Whether or not anyone believed it literally, the charm gave a frightened traveller something to do. In that sense it is both magic and psychology: a ritual pause, a reset, a moment to stop stumbling and regain control.

## How pisky stories still shape Cornwall

Today, piskies live in several Cornwalls at once. They appear in heritage storytelling, children’s resources, local humour, tourist souvenirs, craft names, walking lore and modern retellings. Cornwall Heritage Trust’s “Pisky-Led Boy” shows how the motif can be presented for family audiences while still preserving the core elements: music, wandering, disappearance, fairy hospitality and return.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cornwallheritagetrust.org/learn/resources/stories-and-rhymes/pisky-led-boy/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cornwallheritagetrust.org">[Cornwall Heritage Trust]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cornwallheritagetrust.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cornwallheritagetrust.org.</span></span></span>

Yet the older tales are stranger than much modern branding suggests. They are not only cute little figures in pointed hats. They explain accidents, bad luck, animal distress, broken journeys, vanishings, household disorder and uneasy contact with places that feel older than the people passing through them. Hunt records piskies riding horses at night and leaving them distressed, with knotted manes interpreted as fairy stirrups.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://sacred-texts.com/neu/eng/prwe/prwe028.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sacred-texts.com">[Internet Sacred Text Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sacred-texts.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on sacred-texts.com.</span></span></span> Courtney records piskies who help with chores, punish dirty servants, mislead fishermen and mock those they have tricked.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Folk-Lore_Journal/Volume_5/Cornish_Folk-Lore_%28pp._177-220%29" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: en.wikisource.org">[Wikisource]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">en.wikisource.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Folk-Lore Journal/Volume 5/Cornish Folk-Lore (pp.</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Folk-Lore Journal/Volume 5/Cornish Folk-Lore (pp.</span></span></span>

That range is why piskies remain useful for Cornwall’s folklore identity. They are small enough to be comic, but powerful enough to make the landscape feel alive. They connect domestic interiors with wild ground, children’s stories with adult caution, ancient stones with parish gossip, and old belief with modern jokes about getting lost.

The phrase “pisky-led” survives because it names an experience people still recognise. Anyone who has taken a wrong path on a Cornish moor, misread a lane in mist, followed a misleading light, or felt suddenly unsure in a place that looked simple on the map can understand the old explanation. The pisky may no longer be a literal guide in many minds, but the feeling of being led astray remains one of Cornwall’s most enduring forms of enchantment.

    var select = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-select]');
    if (select && available.indexOf(marketId) >= 0) select.value = marketId;
    var selectedText = marketId;
    if (select && select.selectedIndex >= 0) selectedText = select.options[select.selectedIndex].text;
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-selected-market-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = selectedText;
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-selected-market-flag], [data-ebay-trigger-market-flag]')).forEach(function (flag) {
      flag.className = flagClass(marketId);
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-trigger-market-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = selectedText;
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-domain-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = domainLabel(marketId);
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-option]')).forEach(function (button) {
      button.setAttribute('aria-selected', button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-option') === marketId ? 'true' : 'false');
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      var active = button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button') === marketId;
      button.setAttribute('aria-pressed', active ? 'true' : 'false');
    });
    section.setAttribute('data-ebay-selected-market', marketId);
    if (persist) {
      try { if (window.localStorage) localStorage.setItem('phoenix-ebay-market', marketId); } catch (err) {}
    }
    closeMenu(section);
  }
  sections.forEach(function (section) {
    if (section.getAttribute('data-ebay-localized-init') === '1') return;
    section.setAttribute('data-ebay-localized-init', '1');
    var available = availableMarkets(section);
    var marketId = defaultMarket;
    if (window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation && window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation.inferMarket) {
      marketId = window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation.inferMarket(available, {
        storageKey: 'phoenix-ebay-market',
        defaultMarket: defaultMarket
      });
    } else if (available.indexOf(defaultMarket)< 0) {
      marketId = available[0] || defaultMarket;
    }
    var select = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-select]');
    if (select) {
      select.addEventListener('change', function () {
        applyMarket(section, select.value, true);
      });
    }
    var trigger = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-trigger]');
    var menu = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-menu]');
    if (trigger && menu) {
      trigger.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
        event.stopPropagation();
        var expanded = trigger.getAttribute('aria-expanded') === 'true';
        menu.hidden = expanded;
        trigger.setAttribute('aria-expanded', expanded ? 'false' : 'true');
        var picker = trigger.closest('.fr-ebay-market-picker');
        if (picker) picker.classList.toggle('fr-ebay-market-picker--open', !expanded);
      });
      Array.prototype.slice.call(menu.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-option]')).forEach(function (button) {
        button.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
          event.stopPropagation();
          applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-option'), true);
        });
      });
      document.addEventListener('click', function () { closeMenu(section); });
    }
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      button.addEventListener('click', function () {
        applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button'), true);
      });
    });
    applyMarket(section, marketId, false);
  });
})();
