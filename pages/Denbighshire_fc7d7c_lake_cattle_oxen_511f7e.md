## Introduction

Cerrig-y-drudion’s lake-cattle legend is one of the most memorable animal traditions in old [Denbighshire]({{ 'where-denbighshires-legends-still/' | relative_url }}): a miraculous freckled cow gives endless milk until human greed breaks the gift, then vanishes into a lake with her two long-horned offspring. A second local version turns those same oxen into supernatural workers, dragging a troubling spirit from Cerrig-y-drudion church and into the water. The story matters because it ties together the things that make this part of Denbighshire folklore so distinctive: upland pasture, water, cattle wealth, church fear, witchcraft, named landscape features and the sense that a lake can swallow more than it shows. The main collector was the cleric and antiquary Elias Owen, whose *Welsh Folk-Lore* was first recognised as a prize essay at the 1887 National Eisteddfod and published in 1896.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.library.wales/discover-learn/digital-exhibitions/books/history-books/welsh-folk-lore-a-collection-of-the-folk-tales-and-legends-of-north-wales" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: library.wales">[National Library of Wales]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">library.wales</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on library.wales.</span></span></span>



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/Denbighshire_fc7d7c_lake_cattle_oxen_511f7e-overview.webp" | relative_url }}" alt="Overview image for Lake Cattle" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

The place also needs a little map care. Cerrigydrudion is now within Conwy County Borough, but the Gazetteer of British Place Names lists it as a community in the historic county of Denbighshire; that older county setting is the one in which Owen and other antiquarian sources preserve the tale.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://gazetteer.org.uk/place/Cerrigydrudion_C%2C_Denbighshire_317054" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gazetteer.org.uk">[Gazetteer]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gazetteer.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Gazetteer Cerrigydrudion C, Denbighshire &#124; Gazetteer of British Place Names</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Gazetteer Cerrigydrudion C, Denbighshire &#124; Gazetteer of British Place Names</span></span></span>

## The freckled cow and the broken gift

The oldest useful way into the story is not the lake, but the cow. In Owen’s account, the freckled cow belongs to a wider family of Welsh and borderland tales about a wonderful dairy animal that gives milk freely to all who need it. The gift is generous but not limitless in a moral sense: it depends on decent behaviour. When a witch or malicious milker abuses the gift by milking the cow into a sieve or riddle, the abundance ends. Owen records that the cow then went towards a lake four miles away, bellowing as she went, followed by her two children, the two long-horned oxen, until all three disappeared into Llyn Dau Ychain, the Lake of the Two Oxen, in the parish of Cerrig-y-drudion.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/files/20096/20096-h/20096-h.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Project Gutenberg Welsh Folk-Lore</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Project Gutenberg Welsh Folk-Lore</span></span></span>

A valuable earlier printed witness appears in *Archaeologia Cambrensis*, where the informant is named: Thomas Jones of Cefn Bannog, a small mountain farm near the well associated with the cow. He told the collector that the cow gave milk willingly and copiously until she was milked into a riddle; then she left the country, and two of her offspring went to Llyn Dau Ychain, bellowing as if calling to one another before entering the lake and vanishing. This matters because it makes the tale feel less like a floating “Welsh myth” and more like a local tradition attached to a named farm, named well, named lake and named informant.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/stream/archaeologiacam64assogoog/archaeologiacam64assogoog_djvu.txt" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on archive.org.</span></span></span>

The story also carries a plain moral that would have made immediate sense in a cattle-keeping upland community. Milk is not merely food in this tale; it is a test of social restraint. The magical animal can feed everyone, but the spell is broken by waste, greed or spite. Owen’s source in *Archaeologia Cambrensis* makes that lesson explicit, saying that the thoughtless milker’s behaviour warned local people not to waste even what they did not need.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/stream/archaeologiacam64assogoog/archaeologiacam64assogoog_djvu.txt" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on archive.org.</span></span></span>

What gives the Cerrig-y-drudion version its special force is the way the “gift broken by greed” motif is pinned to the ground. The tradition names the cow’s well, the cow’s crib, her pasture and the ridge around Cefn Bannog and Waen Bannog. The *Archaeologia Cambrensis* account says these names gave the tradition “a reality” it would not otherwise possess, because the story could be walked: from well, to cow-house, to pasture, to lake.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/stream/archaeologiacam64assogoog/archaeologiacam64assogoog_djvu.txt" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on archive.org.</span></span></span>

## The oxen that dragged away a spirit

Owen did not leave the two oxen as mere companions in the cow’s disappearance. He also recorded a second Cerrig-y-drudion story from Mr Lloyd of Llanfihangel Glyn Myfyr, in which the oxen become the only power strong enough to remove a spirit from the parish church. In this version, the freckled cow’s two calves grow into mighty long-horned oxen. A wicked spirit troubles Cerrig-y-drudion church so badly that people fear passing the building even by day, seeing the figure at the windows. A conjuror is consulted and declares that only the two long-horned oxen can dislodge it.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/files/20096/20096-h/20096-h.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Project Gutenberg Welsh Folk-Lore</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Project Gutenberg Welsh Folk-Lore</span></span></span>

The details are strikingly physical. The spirit is secured to a sledge, the oxen are yoked to it, and they drag the struggling load towards a lake on Hiraethog Mountain. As they go, the sledge cuts furrows into the land, while the oxen’s hooves leave marks in the rocks. When the spirit still refuses to yield at the lake, oxen, sledge and spirit are driven into the water together. Owen gives this as the explanation for the lake’s name: the Lake of the Two Oxen.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/files/20096/20096-h/20096-h.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Project Gutenberg Welsh Folk-Lore</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Project Gutenberg Welsh Folk-Lore</span></span></span>

This is not simply a “monster in a lake” tale. It joins three kinds of belief that often overlap in Denbighshire and North Welsh folklore. First, it is a cattle legend, because the supernatural strength belongs to animals descended from the miraculous cow. Secondly, it is a church-haunting story, with a frightened parish and a polluted sacred building. Thirdly, it is a landscape-origin tale, explaining furrows, hoof-marks and a lake-name through a dramatic supernatural event. That combination is what makes the Cerrig-y-drudion case more than a variant of a common fairy-cow story.

The church named in the tradition is not an incidental backdrop. St Mary Magdalene at Cerrigydrudion has served the parish for at least 700 years, with the National Churches Trust noting a documented reference in the 1254 Norwich Taxation and later building history tied to drovers, worshippers and local families.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nationalchurchestrust.org/church/st-mary-magdalene-cerrigydrudion" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nationalchurchestrust.org">[National Churches Trust]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nationalchurchestrust.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nationalchurchestrust.org.</span></span></span> Owen’s tale turns that long-standing parish centre into a threatened threshold: the familiar church becomes frightening until the community uses extraordinary animal power to drive the disturbance back into the upland water.

## Why the lake itself has partly vanished

Modern visitors looking for the lake in a simple way may be disappointed, because the physical landscape has changed. The Clwyd-Powys Archaeological Trust’s historic landscape material for Mynydd Hiraethog says a small natural lake called Llyn Dau-ychain once lay in the area now occupied by Alwen Reservoir. The same source notes that Alwen Reservoir was built between 1911 and 1916, and that major reservoirs on Mynydd Hiraethog submerged earlier prehistoric sites and abandoned fields and farms of medieval and post-medieval origin.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://heneb.org.uk/archive/cpat/projects/longer/histland/hiraeth/mhlakes.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: heneb.org.uk">[Heneb]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">heneb.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Clwyd-Powys Archaeological Trust - Projects - Historic Landscapes - Mynydd Hiraethog - Lakes, Reservoirs and Pools...</span></span></span>

That modern change adds an extra layer to the folklore. In the legend, cattle and spirit vanish beneath older water; in the twentieth-century landscape, the older lake-site itself was absorbed into a reservoir system. Natural Resources Wales describes Alwen Reservoir as a dam and reservoir constructed in the early twentieth century to provide water for Birkenhead, with present-day trails passing through forest, moorland and reservoir scenery.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://naturalresources.wales/days-out/places-to-visit/north-east-wales/alwen/?lang=en" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: naturalresources.wales">[Natural Resources Wales]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">naturalresources.wales</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on naturalresources.wales.</span></span></span>

This does not prove the legend is “about” reservoir-building, because Owen’s material predates the Alwen works. It does, however, affect how the tale is encountered today. The old lake is no longer a neat, visible folklore site in the way a standing stone or churchyard might be. Instead, the story survives as a memory layered into the altered Hiraethog landscape: old lake, reservoir, forestry, trails, information panels and local retellings.

Cerrigydrudion’s own community description fits that setting well. The village lies near the A5 and close to Alwen Reservoir and Llyn Brenig, while the moorland of Hiraethog to the north is described locally as historically rich in Welsh culture and steeped in folklore.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cerrigydrudion.org.uk/en/home.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cerrigydrudion.org.uk">[Cerrigydrudion Community Council]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cerrigydrudion.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cerrigydrudion.org.uk.</span></span></span> The cattle story belongs exactly to that border between lived rural place and legendary upland.

## Lake legends across the Welsh borderlands

Owen himself treated the freckled cow as both local and comparative. After giving the Cerrig-y-drudion disappearance, he immediately pointed to related stories: the White Cow of Mitchell’s Fold in Shropshire, and similar Dun Cow traditions in Warwickshire and Lancashire. In the Shropshire version he summarised, a fairy cow feeds people in famine until a witch milks her into a riddle and the cow disappears or is turned into stone.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/files/20096/20096-h/20096-h.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Project Gutenberg Welsh Folk-Lore</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Project Gutenberg Welsh Folk-Lore</span></span></span>

That comparison is useful, but it should not flatten the Denbighshire story into a generic “magic cow” motif. The local version has its own texture. It links the cow to Cefn Bannog and Ffynnon y Fuwch Frech, gives her two named long-horned offspring, sends them to a particular lake in the parish of Cerrig-y-drudion, and then gives those oxen a second career as exorcising animals. The Shropshire comparison helps readers see the shared borderland pattern; the Cerrig-y-drudion details explain why this page belongs in Denbighshire.

There is also a wider Welsh pattern of lake cattle and lake dwellers. Sir John Rhys, in *Celtic Folklore: Welsh and Manx*, wrote that many Welsh lakes and tarns were believed to have fairy inhabitants who owned cattle, and that those cattle sometimes came ashore and mixed with ordinary herds. He placed such lake traditions alongside famous stories like Llyn y Fan Fach, where a lake woman brings cattle from the water.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/files/55989/55989-h/55989-h.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Project Gutenberg Celtic Folklore: Welsh and Manx (Volume 2 of 2</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Project Gutenberg Celtic Folklore: Welsh and Manx (Volume 2 of 2</span></span></span> Cerrig-y-drudion’s tale reverses the direction: the miraculous cattle do not simply emerge from water; they return to it when the human relationship fails.

Rhys also discussed traditions of the long-horned oxen in Cardiganshire, where landscape features such as a furrow and a preserved horn were linked to legendary oxen. He cautiously suggested that some of these wild ox legends might preserve a distant memory of the aurochs, the extinct wild ox, though that is interpretation rather than proof.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/files/55989/55989-h/55989-h.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Project Gutenberg Celtic Folklore: Welsh and Manx (Volume 2 of 2</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Project Gutenberg Celtic Folklore: Welsh and Manx (Volume 2 of 2</span></span></span> For Cerrig-y-drudion, the safer point is that long-horned oxen were powerful legendary figures in Welsh tradition, capable of explaining scars in the land, huge strength and the movement of dangerous beings.

## How old and how well-attested is the tradition?

The Cerrig-y-drudion cattle tradition is reasonably well-attested for a local folklore case, but it is not an ancient text with a continuous medieval record. Its strongest evidence is nineteenth-century antiquarian collection, especially the *Archaeologia Cambrensis* material naming Thomas Jones of Cefn Bannog and Owen’s later *Welsh Folk-Lore*. The National Library of Wales identifies Owen as a cleric and antiquary and confirms the publication history of *Welsh Folk-Lore*: prize essay at the 1887 National Eisteddfod, published as a single volume in 1896.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.library.wales/discover-learn/digital-exhibitions/books/history-books/welsh-folk-lore-a-collection-of-the-folk-tales-and-legends-of-north-wales" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: library.wales">[National Library of Wales]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">library.wales</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on library.wales.</span></span></span>

Owen’s strength as a source is that he preserved parish-level traditions, named informants or intermediaries when he could, and noticed conflicts between versions. He explicitly acknowledged a conflicting tradition at nearby Llanrwst, where a large bone at Gwydir was popularly said to be the rib of the Dun Cow killed by the Earl of Warwick, even though the Cerrig-y-drudion tradition buried the celebrated cattle in Llyn Dau Ychain. Owen did not force the stories into a single tidy explanation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/files/20096/20096-h/20096-h.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Project Gutenberg Welsh Folk-Lore</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Project Gutenberg Welsh Folk-Lore</span></span></span>

That honesty is important for modern readers. Folklore does not usually behave like a single authorised plot. The same miraculous cow may be localised in more than one place; a lake may collect more than one supernatural story; a named ox may explain a furrow in one county and a haunted church in another. The contradictions do not necessarily make the traditions worthless. They show how memorable motifs travel and settle, while local names, rocks, wells and churches give them fresh authority.

There is even another Llyn Dau Ychain tale in Joseph Jacobs’s *Welsh Fairy Book*, where Sion Rhobert, a Denbighshire harper, sees a brilliantly lit palace by the lake where he had never seen a house before.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://sacred-texts.com/neu/celt/wfb/wfb56.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sacred-texts.com">[Internet Sacred Text Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sacred-texts.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on sacred-texts.com.</span></span></span> That separate fairy-palace story is not the same as the cattle legend, but it reinforces the lake’s role as a place where the ordinary upland route could suddenly open into an uncanny world.

## Why this small story matters in Denbighshire folklore

Cerrig-y-drudion’s lake cattle matter because they show Denbighshire folklore working at ground level. The tale is not built around a king, a battlefield or a famous saint. It is built around milk, cattle, a mountain farm, a parish church, a local lake and the moral danger of misusing a shared gift. That makes it especially revealing for a county tradition shaped by upland grazing, parish life, drovers’ routes and waterlogged moorland edges.

The story also gives Denbighshire a strong version of a broader Welsh theme: water as the border between human life and an older, stranger order. In some Welsh lake tales, cattle emerge from enchanted water and enrich a farm. At Cerrig-y-drudion, the direction is darker. The cow leaves because the human world has failed the terms of the gift; the oxen vanish because they have carried a dangerous spirit to its final confinement. Water is not just scenery. It is the place where broken bargains and troublesome powers are taken out of sight.

For today’s reader, the most memorable image may be the double disappearance: the freckled cow and her bellowing offspring sinking beneath Llyn Dau Ychain, and later the oxen themselves dragging a spirit into the same kind of watery oblivion. The modern reservoir landscape has changed how the place is seen, but it has not erased the pattern of the story. Around Cerrig-y-drudion, the old Denbighshire imagination still turns cattle, churches and lakes into a single folklore map: a map where generosity can be wasted, fear can be yoked and driven away, and the water keeps what the land can no longer hold.
