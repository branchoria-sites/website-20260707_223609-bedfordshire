## Introduction

Brecknockshire, also called Breconshire, is one of the thirteen historic counties of Wales. It is centred on Brecon and the upper Usk, bordered by Radnorshire, Herefordshire, Monmouthshire, Glamorgan, Carmarthenshire and Cardiganshire, and much of its southern upland identity is now bound up with Bannau Brycheiniog National Park. The modern map can be confusing: many places a visitor thinks of as “the Brecon Beacons” now fall within Powys or neighbouring local authority areas, while historic-county folklore still follows older county, parish, lordship and landscape lines. DataMapWales notes that Brecknockshire was one of the counties created when the Marcher lordships were abolished under the Laws in Wales Acts, while the Wikimedia/Wikishire historic-county mapping treats Brecknockshire as a distinct Welsh historic county.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://datamap.gov.wales/layers/geonode%3Ahistoric_counties_bng_rcahmw_ply" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: datamap.gov.wales">[gov.wales]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">datamap.gov.wales</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Data Map Wales Historic County Boundaries of Wales</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Data Map Wales Historic County Boundaries of Wales</span></span></span>

## Where Brecknockshire folklore lives on the map

The county’s folklore makes most sense if it is read through terrain. Brecknockshire is inland, upland and transitional: the River Usk runs through its central valley; the Wye marks part of the northern border; the Black Mountains and the Bannau rise to the south and south-west; and old roads and passes connect it with Herefordshire, Monmouthshire, Carmarthenshire and Glamorgan. The Association of British Counties describes Brecknockshire as a mountainous rural inland county, and the Gazetteer of British Place Names places its identity around the Wye, Usk, Builth Wells, Hay-on-Wye, Llanwrtyd Wells, Mynydd Epynt and the Bannau landscape.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://abcounties.com/counties/county-profiles/brecknockshire/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: abcounties.com">[Association of British Counties]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">abcounties.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Association of British Counties Brecknockshire</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Association of British Counties Brecknockshire</span></span></span>

That matters because many traditions do not stop at modern boundaries. The famous Lady of the Lake story belongs most directly to Llyn y Fan Fach, just over the historic Carmarthenshire side of the Black Mountain, but the surrounding ridge, Fan Brycheiniog, Llyn y Fan Fawr and the Bannau walking landscape draw the tale into Brecknockshire’s folklore orbit. Likewise, Clydach Gorge lies in the borderlands of old Brecknockshire and Monmouthshire, and its fairy and witch associations are often told as part of the wider Bannau/Brecon Beacons imaginative landscape. The National Park Authority’s 2023 public identity shift to Bannau Brycheiniog also reinforces the older Welsh association of the peaks with Brychan’s kingdom, even while the authority has clarified that the legal authority name itself did not change.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://beacons-npa.gov.uk/the-authority/press-and-news/archive/april-2023/brecon-beacons-national-park-reclaims-its-welsh-name/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: beacons-npa.gov.uk">[Brecon Beacons National Park]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">beacons-npa.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on beacons-npa.gov.uk.</span></span></span>

For a folklore reader, the useful rule is this: keep Brecknockshire as the centre, but expect the stories to move along ridges, drove roads, parish networks, river valleys and tourist trails. Older belief culture rarely respected the neat lines that later administrators drew.

## Fairies, mountain roads and gifts that cannot be trusted

Welsh fairy tradition is one of the strongest lenses for reading Brecknockshire. Wirt Sikes’s *British Goblins* was published in 1880 and remains one of the most widely cited English-language antiquarian collections of Welsh fairy lore. The Public Domain Review identifies Sikes as United States consul to Cardiff from 1876 to 1883 and summarises the book as a study of Welsh mythology and legend, especially the fairy people often called the “fair folk” or “fair family”.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://publicdomainreview.org/collection/british-goblins-welsh-folk-lore-fairy-mythology-legends-and-traditions-1880/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: publicdomainreview.org">[The Public Domain Review]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">publicdomainreview.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">british goblins welsh folk lore fairy mythology legends and traditions 1880</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">british goblins welsh folk lore fairy mythology legends and traditions 1880</span></span></span>

One Breconshire detail in Sikes is especially memorable: fairy bread. In the full text of *British Goblins*, Sikes records a Breconshire legend in which the fair folk give loaves of bread to peasants, but the loaves turn into toadstools the next morning unless they are eaten in darkness and silence.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/stream/britishgoblinswe00sikerich/britishgoblinswe00sikerich_djvu.txt" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on archive.org.</span></span></span> The story is small, but it shows a central rule of fairy folklore: fairy generosity is conditional. Gifts from the otherworld often come with silence, secrecy, timing or taboo attached. Break the rule and the blessing becomes worthless, comic or dangerous.

Sikes also divides Welsh fairies into types: lake and stream women, household beings, mine fairies and mountain spirits. That classification is not a tidy native taxonomy so much as a Victorian attempt to organise living traditions, but it helps explain why Brecknockshire has more mountain and road folklore than parlour fairies. The county’s lonely uplands are the natural home of beings that mislead travellers, appear at dusk, or turn a familiar track into frightening ground.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://books.google.com/books/about/British_Goblins.html?id=_iXaAAAAMAAJ" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: books.google.com">[Google Books]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">books.google.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on google.com.</span></span></span>

The most Brecknockshire-flavoured fairy figure is the mountain hag or wandering female spirit known in Welsh tradition as a gwyll. Later summaries of Sikes preserve the story of an “Old Woman of the Mountain” encountered on the Black Mountain in Breconshire: a man tries to follow what he thinks is an old woman, is led into a marsh, hears her cackling laugh, and drives her away with a knife, a common protective object in fairy belief.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gwyllion" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wikipedia.org.</span></span></span> Whether one treats this as fairy lore, ghost lore or cautionary hill story, the setting is crucial. The tale belongs to mist, bog, poor visibility and the real danger of losing one’s way on upland ground.

## The Lady of the Lake and Brecknockshire’s western edge

The Lady of Llyn y Fan Fach is not strictly a Brecknockshire-only legend, because the lake is usually placed in Carmarthenshire on the northern margin of the Black Mountain. Yet no account of Brecknockshire folklore feels complete without explaining it, because the story sits immediately beside the county’s western upland edge and is constantly retold through Bannau Brycheiniog walking culture. Fan Brycheiniog, whose summit lies in Brecknockshire, overlooks the neighbouring lake landscape and gives the mountain group one of its strongest county connections.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fan_Brycheiniog" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Fan Brycheiniog</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Fan Brycheiniog</span></span></span>

The story’s common shape is simple and powerful. A young man sees a beautiful woman rise from the lake. She agrees to marry him, but only on a condition: if he strikes her three times, she will return to the water. The marriage prospers for a time, with magical cattle and children, but the taboo is eventually broken. She disappears back into the lake, taking her animals with her. Their sons become associated with the Physicians of Myddfai, legendary healers whose medical knowledge is tied to their fairy mother. A scholarly treatment by R. Gwyndaf describes the Llyn y Fan Fach story as a lake legend about a fairy maiden who marries a local farmer and founds the line of the Physicians of Myddfai, while also noting that it belongs to a wider class of migratory legends found beyond Wales.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://isfnr.org/files/Printed.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: isfnr.org">[Folk Narrative Research Society]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">isfnr.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on isfnr.org.</span></span></span>

For readers of Brecknockshire folklore, the important point is not county ownership but landscape function. The tale turns a remote glacial lake into a doorway between ordinary farming life and an otherworld of beauty, cattle, healing and taboo. It also explains why the Myddfai healing tradition feels both practical and magical: medicine is imagined as knowledge received from a lake woman, then passed down into human hands. Modern tourism keeps that connection alive. Discover Carmarthenshire’s walking material explicitly links Llyn y Fan Fach with the Lady of the Lake and the Physicians of Myddfai, while recent walking journalism still presents the lake as a place where terrain and legend are inseparable.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.discovercarmarthenshire.com/media/2433/llyn-y-fan-eng.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: discovercarmarthenshire.com">[Discover Carmarthenshire]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">discovercarmarthenshire.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Discover Carmarthenshire Llyn y Fan Fach</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Discover Carmarthenshire Llyn y Fan Fach</span></span></span>

## Stones that walk, drink and remember

Brecknockshire’s prehistoric monuments carry a different kind of folklore: not fairy marriage, but animation. Maen Llia, the great standing stone near the road between Ystradfellte and Heol Senni, is one of the county’s most striking examples. Coflein, the Royal Commission’s online record for the historic environment of Wales, lists Maen Llia as a standing stone with Cadw scheduling records under reference BR017.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://coflein.gov.uk/en/archives/6059835/sites" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: coflein.gov.uk">[Coflein]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">coflein.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on coflein.gov.uk.</span></span></span> Other heritage summaries describe it as a 3.7 metre, roughly twelve-foot stone, likely Late Neolithic or Bronze Age in date, standing in an exposed moorland setting.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.themodernantiquarian.com/site/104/maen-llia" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: themodernantiquarian.com">[The Modern Antiquarian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">themodernantiquarian.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on themodernantiquarian.com.</span></span></span>

The folklore says the stone moves. One version claims that when a cock crows, Maen Llia goes down to drink at the river; another says it goes to the Afon Llia on special occasions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.themodernantiquarian.com/post/36763/folklore/maen-llia" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: themodernantiquarian.com">[The Modern Antiquarian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">themodernantiquarian.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on themodernantiquarian.com.</span></span></span> This is a common motif in British standing-stone lore: stones walk, drink, dance, turn round, or reveal treasure at certain times. The tale does not have to be read as a literal belief held uniformly by everyone. It is a way of giving agency to a monument that already feels out of the ordinary: too large, too isolated and too old to fit comfortably into everyday explanation.

There may also be a natural cue behind the story. Modern local interpretations suggest that the “drinking” legend could be connected with the stone’s long shadow reaching towards water when the sun is low.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.komoot.com/highlight/4390519" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: komoot.com">[komoot]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">komoot.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on komoot.com.</span></span></span> That does not disprove the folklore; it helps explain why the image stuck. A stone that casts a moving shadow across moorland can look, in the right season and light, as if it is travelling. Folklore often begins in this meeting point between observation and imagination.

## Saints, wells and the sacred landscape

Compared with some Welsh counties, Brecknockshire is less famous for one nationally celebrated holy well, but saintly and well traditions still shape its religious folklore. The county’s church sites, dedications and local legends belong to a broader Welsh pattern in which springs, wells and early saints mark the landscape. Cadw’s interpretation material for early medieval Welsh sacred places warns that holy wells near church sites often have little surviving evidence for early medieval use, and that dating saintly place-name traditions can be difficult.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cadw.gov.wales/sites/default/files/2019-04/InterpplanCelticSaints_EN.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cadw.gov.wales">[Cadw]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cadw.gov.wales</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Cadw TOUCHSTONE Interpretation plan</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cadw TOUCHSTONE Interpretation plan</span></span></span> That caution is useful: not every old well-name proves an ancient cult, and not every later story preserves an unbroken early medieval memory.

Even so, Brecknockshire’s local religious folklore is rich in claimed connections. Llanigon, near Hay-on-Wye, preserves traditions around St Eigon, sometimes linked in local tradition with Caratacus and even St Paul crossing Gospel Pass. Such traditions are historically difficult, but they show how border parishes used saintly genealogy to place themselves inside a very deep Christian past.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Llanigon" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wikipedia.org.</span></span></span>

The wider Welsh well tradition also helps explain how Brecknockshire readers should approach local springs. Holy wells across Britain and Wales were often associated with healing, offerings, divination, baptism, saints and seasonal visiting, but the strength of documentation varies greatly from site to site. Modern well researchers repeatedly stress the need to separate recorded custom, local memory, place-name evidence and later romantic retelling.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://britishholywells.co.uk/wales/wales.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: britishholywells.co.uk">[britishholywells.co.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">britishholywells.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on britishholywells.co.uk.</span></span></span> In Brecknockshire, that means the best evidence may be parish history, local archives, field-names and oral testimony rather than a famous national shrine.

## Village folklore: charms, sayings and parish memory

Not all Brecknockshire folklore is dramatic. Some of the most valuable material is ordinary village lore: sayings, charms, weather beliefs, domestic customs and small stories preserved by local people. A key source is M. E. Hartland and E. B. Thomas’s “Breconshire Village Folklore”, published in *Folklore* in 1913. JSTOR’s record identifies it as a Breconshire article, and search snippets note that much of the material came from Miss E. B. Thomas of Llanthomas, Llanigon, with tales and sayings connected to the parish.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.jstor.org/stable/1255651" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: jstor.org">[JSTOR]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">jstor.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Breconshire Village Folklore</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Breconshire Village Folklore</span></span></span>

This kind of source matters because it catches folklore before it becomes a tourist brand. It is not mainly about grand mythic beings; it is about what people said, feared, repeated, sang or did. Later scholarship on charms even cites Hartland’s Breconshire material in discussions of oral healing traditions, showing that county-level folklore can contribute to wider studies of how spoken charms moved through Europe and Britain.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ledonline.it/Il-Segno-le-Lettere/allegati/996-7-language-of-magic_07.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ledonline.it">[Led Online]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ledonline.it</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Led Online A Written Charm in Oral Tradition: “Peter Sat on a Marble</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Led Online A Written Charm in Oral Tradition: “Peter Sat on a Marble</span></span></span>

The Brecknock Society’s modern oral archive shows that this local-memory work continues. The society says it has recorded about 125 voices over ten years, mainly local people speaking about Breconshire, in both Welsh and English.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.brecknocksociety.wales/oral-archive-project" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: brecknocksociety.wales">[Brecknock Society and Museum Friends]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">brecknocksociety.wales</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on brecknocksociety.wales.</span></span></span> That is important for modern folklore because living memory is not just a source for “old stories”; it records how people today remember farms, chapels, schools, markets, roads, family sayings, seasonal practices and uncanny experiences. County folklore is always partly an archive of everyday speech.

## Ghosts, gorges and modern retellings

Brecknockshire’s ghost tradition today is strongly shaped by tourism, heritage events and the wider appetite for haunted Wales. Craig-y-Nos Castle, at Penycae in Powys and close to the old Brecknockshire/Glamorgan borderlands, is one of the best-known modern examples. Visit Wales describes it as a former estate of the opera singer Adelina Patti and says it is known as one of the most haunted places in Wales.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.visitwales.com/inspire-me/short-breaks/haunted-wales" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: visitwales.com">[Visit Wales]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">visitwales.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on visitwales.com.</span></span></span> Land of Legends gives the same basic heritage framing: Patti owned the castle, added a theatre, and the site is now associated with ghostly reports and tours.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.landoflegends.wales/location/craig-y-nos-castle" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: landoflegends.wales">[Land of Legends]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">landoflegends.wales</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on landoflegends.wales.</span></span></span>

This is not the same kind of evidence as a nineteenth-century oral tale. Craig-y-Nos folklore is a modern haunted-house tradition built from layered history: celebrity, performance, illness, architecture, night-time events and paranormal tourism. That does not make it worthless; it means it should be labelled honestly. The story’s power comes from the castle’s documented past and from the way visitors retell experiences in a theatrical setting.

Clydach Gorge offers another kind of modern retelling. The Abergavenny Chronicle reported local guide traditions linking the gorge with fairies, goblins and witches, and with the romantic claim that Shakespeare found inspiration there for *A Midsummer Night’s Dream*.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.abergavennychronicle.com/news/fairies-witches-spirits-and-shakespeare-a-guide-to-the-folklore-of-the-clydach-gorge-71603" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: abergavennychronicle.com">[Abergavenny Chronicle]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">abergavennychronicle.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Abergavenny Chroniclea guide to the folklore of the Clydach Gorge</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Abergavenny Chroniclea guide to the folklore of the Clydach Gorge</span></span></span> The National Park Authority’s archive likewise describes Clydach Gorge as filled with tales of fairies and otherworldly creatures and notes the Shakespeare association as part of local storytelling.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://beacons-npa.gov.uk/the-authority/press-and-news/archive/2010-2/november-2010/ghostly-gorge-tales-by-the-fireside/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: beacons-npa.gov.uk">[Brecon Beacons National Park]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">beacons-npa.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on beacons-npa.gov.uk.</span></span></span> The Shakespeare claim should be treated cautiously, as a tradition rather than a proven literary fact, but it reveals something real about the place: the gorge has long been experienced as a dramatic, otherworldly landscape.

## Customs and calendar folklore

Brecknockshire sits within the broader seasonal custom world of south and mid Wales. The Mari Lwyd, the grey-mare wassailing custom using a horse’s skull or horse figure, is not uniquely Brecknockshire, but it is part of the cultural neighbourhood in which Breconshire winter customs are understood. Wales.com describes the Mari as traditionally taken around a village between Christmas and Twelfth Night, and notes regional variants including Ystradgynlais in the Swansea Valleys, historically close to the Brecknockshire border world.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.wales.com/about/history-and-heritage/welsh-traditions-myths-and-legends/mari-lwyd" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wales.com">[Wales]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wales.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Mari Lwyd</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Mari Lwyd</span></span></span> Amgueddfa Cymru, the national museum, also identifies the Mari Lwyd as one of Wales’s best-known Christmas customs, involving wassail-singing groups carrying the horse figure from door to door.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://museum.wales/articles/1187/Christmas-Traditions-The-Mari-Lwyd/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: museum.wales">[Museum Wales]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">museum.wales</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on museum.wales.</span></span></span>

The point is not to claim every Welsh custom as Brecknockshire property. It is to place the county within a living Welsh custom zone where winter visiting, song contests, door rituals, mumming-like characters, wassailing and local performance have repeatedly crossed parish and county lines. Modern revivals and events may not reproduce an unbroken Brecknockshire practice, but they show how old forms continue to be reimagined.

## What is well attested, and what should be treated carefully?

Brecknockshire folklore is strongest where named places, named collectors and repeated records meet. The Black Mountain fairy and “Old Woman” material has a clear antiquarian source in Sikes and later Welsh fairy scholarship. The Breconshire fairy-bread story is explicitly attached to the county in *British Goblins*. Maen Llia has firm archaeological standing and a persistent local legend. Hartland and Thomas’s 1913 village folklore gives parish-level evidence from Llanigon. The Lady of Llyn y Fan Fach is very well attested, though it belongs most precisely to the Carmarthenshire side of the western Bannau and should be explained as a cross-border landscape legend rather than forced wholly into Brecknockshire.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/stream/britishgoblinswe00sikerich/britishgoblinswe00sikerich_djvu.txt" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[archive.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on archive.org.</span></span></span>

More caution is needed with claims that sound attractive but rest mainly on later tourism or local romance. Shakespeare at Clydach Gorge is a good example: it is a genuine tradition about the gorge, and worth mentioning, but not a secure biographical fact about Shakespeare. Haunted Craig-y-Nos is similarly valuable as modern ghost culture, but its stories should be separated from the documented history of Adelina Patti and the building’s later uses.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.abergavennychronicle.com/news/fairies-witches-spirits-and-shakespeare-a-guide-to-the-folklore-of-the-clydach-gorge-71603" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: abergavennychronicle.com">[abergavennychronicle.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">abergavennychronicle.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Abergavenny Chroniclea guide to the folklore of the Clydach Gorge</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Abergavenny Chroniclea guide to the folklore of the Clydach Gorge</span></span></span>

The county’s folklore therefore works best when presented in layers: old oral and antiquarian material; parish customs and charms; archaeological sites with attached legends; saintly and well traditions with uneven documentation; and modern heritage retellings. Brecknockshire’s identity is not built from one famous monster or one neat myth cycle. It is built from the feeling that the upland road, the lake edge, the standing stone, the gorge and the village churchyard all remember more than they explain.
