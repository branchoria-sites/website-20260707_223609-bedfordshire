## Introduction

The witches and seers of [Argyllshire]({{ 'the-strange-stories-behind-argyllshires/' | relative_url }}) belong to one of the most distinctive parts of Highland belief: a world where unusual knowledge, healing skill, curses, warnings and visions were understood through local traditions rather than modern categories. Stories collected in Argyll and the western Highlands describe witches not only as figures of fear, but also as people associated with practical magic, charms, weather lore and knowledge of hidden causes of misfortune. Second sight, meanwhile, was remembered as a troubling and sometimes respected ability to perceive events beyond ordinary human senses.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/files/58894/58894-h/58894-h.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">58894 h</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Project GutenbergThe Project Gutenberg eBook of Witchcraft &amp; Second Sight...15 Feb 2019 — The Project Gutenberg eBook, Witchcraft &amp; Seco...</span></span></span>

These traditions are especially important because they show how Argyll communities explained uncertainty. A failed crop, sick animal, sudden illness or unexplained death could be interpreted through ideas of bewitchment, harmful influence or supernatural warning. At the same time, many people known in tradition as wise practitioners or seers occupied a more complicated place: they could be feared, consulted and respected. Much of what survives today comes from oral accounts recorded in the nineteenth century, especially the work of John Gregorson Campbell, a Gaelic-speaking minister connected with Tiree and Argyll who collected stories of witchcraft, charms and second sight from Highland communities.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/files/58894/58894-h/58894-h.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">58894 h</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Project GutenbergThe Project Gutenberg eBook of Witchcraft &amp; Second Sight...15 Feb 2019 — The Project Gutenberg eBook, Witchcraft &amp; Seco...</span></span></span>

## Cunning folk and magical traditions

### Witches were feared, but not always simply villains

In Argyll folklore, the word “witch” covers a wide range of traditional figures. Some stories describe harmful practitioners blamed for illness, bad luck or storms, while others preserve memories of people who possessed useful knowledge: healing methods, protective charms, cures for livestock problems or ways of dealing with perceived supernatural threats. The boundary between a feared witch and a respected healer could be unclear, because the same knowledge that might protect a household could also be viewed as dangerous if used against neighbours.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/files/58894/58894-h/58894-h.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">58894 h</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Project GutenbergThe Project Gutenberg eBook of Witchcraft &amp; Second Sight...15 Feb 2019 — The Project Gutenberg eBook, Witchcraft &amp; Seco...</span></span></span>

John Gregorson Campbell’s collections are among the most valuable records of these beliefs. His *Witchcraft and Second Sight in the Highlands and Islands of Scotland*, published after his death, gathered traditional accounts rather than presenting witchcraft as proven fact. The collection includes stories about magical practices, weather-working, charms, apparitions and community memories from Gaelic-speaking areas, including the western Highlands and islands closely connected with Argyll.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/files/58894/58894-h/58894-h.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">58894 h</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Project GutenbergThe Project Gutenberg eBook of Witchcraft &amp; Second Sight...15 Feb 2019 — The Project Gutenberg eBook, Witchcraft &amp; Seco...</span></span></span>

A recurring theme in these traditions is that misfortune often had a human explanation. If a cow stopped giving milk, butter failed to form, a child became suddenly ill or a household suffered repeated bad luck, people might look for a hidden cause. Belief in the evil eye and harmful magic formed part of a wider Highland understanding that unseen forces could affect everyday life. Objects and rituals intended to protect against harm were also part of this tradition, with museums preserving examples of Scottish charms and protective objects connected with healing and safeguarding.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nms.ac.uk/discover-catalogue/from-amulets-to-elf-bolts-10-scottish-charms" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nms.ac.uk">[National Museums Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nms.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Museums Scotland From amulets to elf bolts: 11 Scottish Charms</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">witchcraft was deeply rooted. ### Island memories of witches and powerful women

Argyll’s islands preserved some of the strongest witch traditions. On Mull, later storytelling developed around groups of powerful women sometimes described as witches who were consulted by local people and clan leaders. These tales, including stories of the “witches of Mull”, belong to the folklore tradition rather than verified historical accounts, but they reveal an important pattern: the witch could be imagined as a person with unusual authority rather than merely a figure of evil.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.countrylife.co.uk/travel/the-witches-of-mull-247999" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: countrylife.co.uk">[Country Life]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">countrylife.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">untry Life The Witches of Mull, and other spine-chilling stories from</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">It&#x27;s said that not merely one witch or two made the island her home, but a whole race of them.Read more...</span></span></span>

Another famous Argyll landscape connection is the legend of the Cailleach of Ben Cruachan, a powerful old woman associated with the mountain above Loch Awe. In later retellings she appears as a landscape-shaping supernatural figure whose actions explain the creation of places such as Loch Awe. ## Second sight in Highland belief

### What people meant by second sight

Second sight was one of the best-known supernatural beliefs associated with the Highlands and islands. It was traditionally understood as an ability to perceive events, people or dangers that ordinary sight could not reveal. John Gregorson Campbell recorded traditions of this kind through accounts of visions, apparitions and warnings, describing a belief system in which certain individuals were thought to have access to knowledge beyond everyday experience.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/files/58894/58894-h/58894-h.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">58894 h</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Project GutenbergThe Project Gutenberg eBook of Witchcraft &amp; Second Sight...15 Feb 2019 — The Project Gutenberg eBook, Witchcraft &amp; Seco...</span></span></span>

The idea was not simply that someone could predict the future. Traditional accounts often involved seeing a person’s image before an event, witnessing a sign connected with death or disaster, or receiving a warning that only became understandable later. Modern writers sometimes use “seer” and “person with second sight” interchangeably, but Highland tradition could distinguish between someone naturally affected by visions and someone regarded as skilled in interpreting or controlling such experiences.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.scottishbeacon.com/news/sports-culture/scottish-folklore-seers-and-second-sight/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: scottishbeacon.com">[scottishbeacon.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">scottishbeacon.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">scottish folklore seers and second sight</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">A seer is able to control the visions, while for a person...Read more...</span></span></span>

Second sight also became closely linked with the landscape of the western Highlands. Remote glens, islands and sea routes created environments where storms, shipwrecks, illness and sudden loss were familiar dangers. Stories of visions before deaths at sea or warnings before disasters reflected the concerns of communities whose lives depended on uncertain conditions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/files/58894/58894-h/58894-h.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">58894 h</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Project GutenbergThe Project Gutenberg eBook of Witchcraft &amp; Second Sight...15 Feb 2019 — The Project Gutenberg eBook, Witchcraft &amp; Seco...</span></span></span>

### Seers, prophecy and the making of local memory

The reputation of Highland seers grew through storytelling, and some figures became famous beyond their original communities. However, the line between historical person, remembered character and later legend is often difficult to draw. Traditions were reshaped as they passed from one generation to another, and collectors such as Campbell recorded versions already influenced by memory and local interpretation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/files/58894/58894-h/58894-h.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">58894 h</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Project GutenbergThe Project Gutenberg eBook of Witchcraft &amp; Second Sight...15 Feb 2019 — The Project Gutenberg eBook, Witchcraft &amp; Seco...</span></span></span>

This does not make the traditions unimportant. Instead, second sight offers a window into how Argyll communities understood knowledge, uncertainty and reputation. A person believed to possess unusual insight could become a guardian of community memory, with stories preserving their warnings, sayings and encounters long after the original events were forgotten.

## Charms, healing and community memory

### Everyday magic in Argyll life

Traditional Argyll beliefs about magic were often practical rather than theatrical. Charms, prayers, protective actions and healing customs were part of ordinary responses to sickness, danger and misfortune. The same household might combine Christian practice with older folk traditions, reflecting the mixed religious and cultural environment of the Highlands.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nms.ac.uk/discover-catalogue/from-amulets-to-elf-bolts-10-scottish-charms" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nms.ac.uk">[National Museums Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nms.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Museums Scotland From amulets to elf bolts: 11 Scottish Charms</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">witchcraft was deeply rooted. Campbell’s collections preserve many examples of this world of everyday belief: magical cures, protective sayings, stories of enchanted knowledge and accounts of people attempting to counter harmful influences. ### From oral tradition to modern folklore

The surviving image of Argyll witches and seers has been shaped heavily by collectors, historians and modern tourism. Nineteenth-century folklorists preserved valuable oral material, but their work also reflected the interests of their own period. A story written down in a book was not a direct recording of the past alone; it was also the result of conversations, selection and interpretation by the collector.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://booksfromscotland.com/book/the-gaelic-otherworld-rev-john-gregorson-campbells-superstitions-of-the-highlands-and-the-islands-of-scotland-and-witchcraft-and-second-sight-in-the-highlands-and-islands/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: booksfromscotland.com">[Books from Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">booksfromscotland.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Books from ScotlandThe Gaelic Otherworld: Rev.John Gregorson Campbell&#x27;s...1 May 2008 — John Gregorson Campbell&#x27;s Superstitions of the Hi...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 2008</span></span></span>

Today, witches and second sight remain part of Argyllshire’s cultural identity through local storytelling, heritage interpretation and interest in Gaelic traditions. The enduring appeal of these traditions lies in that mixture of fear, wisdom, imagination and place.
