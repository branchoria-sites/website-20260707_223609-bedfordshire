## Introduction

Geographically, Shetland is the UK’s northernmost historic county area in the project’s historic-counties frame. It is also a modern island council area, so the historic and present-day boundaries mostly align in everyday public understanding. The important difference is cultural rather than administrative: Shetland’s folklore belongs to islands that were under Norse rule for centuries before becoming part of Scotland in the late fifteenth century. Shetland.org summarises that Vikings arrived around 850 AD, that Norse rule lasted for nearly 600 years, and that Shetland passed under Scottish administration in 1469; the same source stresses that Shetland’s language, music, foodways and festivals still carry this northern inheritance.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.shetland.org/blog/norse-influence-shetland-culture" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: shetland.org">[Shetland.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">shetland.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Norse influence on Shetland culture &#124; Shetland.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Norse influence on Shetland culture &#124; Shetland.org</span></span></span>

## Why Shetland folklore feels different

Shetland’s folklore is often best understood as Northern Isles folklore: related to Orkney, linked to Scandinavia, but deeply local in its wording, places and social memory. The island dialect preserves traces of older speech worlds. Shetland ForWirds describes the dialect as an amalgam of Norse, Lowland Scots and English, reflecting successive periods of influence, while Shetland.org notes that most place names have roots in the Old Norse spoken in the islands until roughly 300 years ago.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.shetlanddialect.org.uk/historical" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: shetlanddialect.org.uk">[shetlanddialect.org.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">shetlanddialect.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on shetlanddialect.org.uk.</span></span></span><span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.shetland.org/blog/norse-influence-shetland-culture" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: shetland.org">[Shetland.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">shetland.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Norse influence on Shetland culture &#124; Shetland.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Norse influence on Shetland culture &#124; Shetland.org</span></span></span>

That linguistic background matters because Shetland’s supernatural beings often come with local names, not just standard folklore labels. A trow is not simply a fairy with a Shetland accent; it belongs to a body of island stories about hill people, hidden dwellings, music, theft, luck, danger and uneasy neighbourliness. A njuggel is not merely a kelpie renamed; Shetland Museum and Archives presents it as a water-horse tradition with Scandinavian associations and its own local behaviour.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.shetlandmuseumandarchives.org.uk/blog/the-most-deceptive-of-creatures-the-njuggel" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: shetlandmuseumandarchives.org.uk">[Shetland Museum &amp; Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">shetlandmuseumandarchives.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on shetlandmuseumandarchives.org.uk.</span></span></span>

John Spence’s 1899 *Shetland Folk-Lore* remains a major printed landmark because it was explicitly gathered from older island informants. In his preface, Spence says he had spent nearly forty years collecting sayings and superstitions “from the lips of the old folk”, and he describes winter fireside storytelling about trows, witches, spirits and apparitions as a former kind of everyday literature.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Shetland_Folk-Lore" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: en.wikisource.org">[Wikisource]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">en.wikisource.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Shetland Folk-Lore</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Shetland Folk-Lore</span></span></span> The Folklore Society’s *County Folklore* volume for Orkney and Shetland, published shortly afterwards, confirms the breadth of Spence’s material: fishing superstitions, trows, selkies, witches, Finns, festival customs, medical magic, charms and proverbs.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/stream/countyfolklore03folkuoft/countyfolklore03folkuoft_djvu.txt" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Internet Archive Full text of &quot;County folklore</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Internet Archive Full text of &quot;County folklore</span></span></span>

## Trows: Shetland’s hill people

The trows are the creatures most strongly associated with Shetland folklore. They are usually imagined as hidden people of the hills, mounds and moorland: smaller than humans in many tellings, mischievous or dangerous, active at night, fond of music, and liable to interfere with people, animals, children or household goods. Shetland.org calls trows among the most common and best-loved figures in the islands’ folklore and describes them as hill-dwelling “little people” who come out at night and may turn to stone if caught by the rising sun.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.shetland.org/blog/shetland-folklore" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: shetland.org">[Shetland.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">shetland.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on shetland.org.</span></span></span>

The landscape is central to the belief. Trows are linked to the inland heathery peatlands as well as to mounds and knolls. Shetland Museum and Archives even includes a “trowie knowe” in its Customs and Folklore gallery, presenting it as the dwelling-place of Shetland’s little people.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.shetlandmuseumandarchives.org.uk/visit/galleries/customs-and-folklore" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: shetlandmuseumandarchives.org.uk">[Shetland Museum &amp; Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">shetlandmuseumandarchives.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Shetland Museum &amp; Archives Customs and Folklore &#124; Shetland Museum &amp; Archives</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Shetland Museum &amp; Archives Customs and Folklore &#124; Shetland Museum &amp; Archives</span></span></span> This is important: the trow is not just a character in a story, but a way of making the island landscape feel inhabited by older, unseen neighbours.

Trow stories often explain unease around liminal places and times. The old moor road after dark, the mound beside the track, the tune heard where no fiddler should be, the unexplained illness of a cow, the missing child or the strange visitor at night can all be pulled into a trow-shaped explanation. Shetland’s musical culture also keeps the association alive: Shetland.org connects local music with fiddle traditions and “trowie reels”, while the wider trow tradition includes stories of tunes learned from supernatural musicians.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.shetland.org/blog/norse-influence-shetland-culture" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: shetland.org">[Shetland.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">shetland.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Norse influence on Shetland culture &#124; Shetland.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Norse influence on Shetland culture &#124; Shetland.org</span></span></span>

The trow also gives Shetland an obvious internal link to Orkney folklore. Both island groups have trow traditions, but Shetland’s versions are strongly coloured by its dialect, settlement pattern and peatland geography. For a UK folklore map, trows are one of the clearest ways to connect Shetland with Orkney while still keeping Shetland’s voice distinct.

## Sea-people, selkies and the dangerous edge of the shore

Shetland folklore is as maritime as it is moorland. Selkies, mermaids, sea-trows and Finns belong to a world where the boundary between human and non-human life is imagined through seals, skins, skerries and deep water. These stories are not just romantic sea-fairy tales. They reflect a community whose livelihood and danger were tied to fishing, seal-hunting, weather and the constant possibility of loss at sea.

Selkie stories are especially powerful because they turn the familiar seal into a being with a hidden human life. In the common Northern Isles pattern, a seal-being removes its skin to take human form; if a human steals that skin, the selkie can be trapped on land, often in an unhappy marriage, until the skin is recovered. Search summaries for the Shetland tradition point to versions localised in Unst and Papa Stour, including the famous motif of the stolen sealskin and the sea-being’s eventual return.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Selkie" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wikipedia.org.</span></span></span><span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://shetlandwithlaurie.com/the-blog/shetland-folklore-series-creatures-from-the-sea-selkies-mermaids-beasts-serpents" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: shetlandwithlaurie.com">[Shetland With Laurie]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">shetlandwithlaurie.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on shetlandwithlaurie.com.</span></span></span>

The Papa Stour material is particularly revealing because it places the story among real skerries and seal-hunting. A modern Shetland folklore account retells a story from the Ve Skerries in which a stranded hunter is rescued by a selkie on condition that he helps return the skin of the selkie’s wife.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://shetlandwithlaurie.com/the-blog/shetland-folklore-series-creatures-from-the-sea-selkies-mermaids-beasts-serpents" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: shetlandwithlaurie.com">[Shetland With Laurie]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">shetlandwithlaurie.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on shetlandwithlaurie.com.</span></span></span> The moral tension is obvious: the human depends on the mercy of the very kind of being he has been exploiting.

Mermaid and giant stories also attach themselves to the northern seascape. One of the best-known Unst legends concerns the giants Herman and Saxa, associated with Hermaness and Saxa Vord, who are drawn into fatal rivalry by a mermaid. Shetland.org describes the mermaid luring the two quarrelling giants into the sea, while Shetland Amenity Trust place-name material records Herma and Saxi as reputed Unst giants whose names are linked with Hermaness, Saxavord, Herman’s Ha and Saxa’s Baa.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.shetland.org/blog/shetland-folklore" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: shetland.org">[Shetland.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">shetland.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on shetland.org.</span></span></span><span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.shetlandamenity.org/place-names-of-the-week-personal-names" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: shetlandamenity.org">[Shetland Amenity Trust]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">shetlandamenity.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Shetland Amenity Trust Place Names of the Week</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Shetland Amenity Trust Place Names of the Week</span></span></span>

## Njuggels and water warnings

The njuggel, also spelled in several local forms, is Shetland’s feared water-horse. It resembles the wider Scottish kelpie tradition in that it appears as a horse and is associated with water, but Shetland Museum and Archives is careful to give it a local explanation. In an interview with Dr Ian Tait, the museum describes the njuggel as a deceptive creature of freshwater places that enticed people to climb on its back before galloping to the deepest part of a loch.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.shetlandmuseumandarchives.org.uk/blog/the-most-deceptive-of-creatures-the-njuggel" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: shetlandmuseumandarchives.org.uk">[Shetland Museum &amp; Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">shetlandmuseumandarchives.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on shetlandmuseumandarchives.org.uk.</span></span></span>

The same museum account makes the folklore feel practical rather than merely decorative. In older Shetland, people crossed open moorland without modern roads, often at dusk or by moonlight, while transporting peat, milling grain, tending animals or moving between settlements. A stray horse near water was therefore plausible enough to be tempting. The njuggel story turns that everyday risk into a memorable warning: be wary near lochs, burns, mills and marshy ground, especially in winter darkness.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.shetlandmuseumandarchives.org.uk/blog/the-most-deceptive-of-creatures-the-njuggel" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: shetlandmuseumandarchives.org.uk">[Shetland Museum &amp; Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">shetlandmuseumandarchives.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on shetlandmuseumandarchives.org.uk.</span></span></span>

Place names help show how embedded the belief was. Shetland Museum and Archives points to names such as Njuggleswater in Tingwall, Njogershun in Northmavine and Nuckrawater in Whalsay as evidence of the tradition’s spread across the islands.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.shetlandmuseumandarchives.org.uk/blog/the-most-deceptive-of-creatures-the-njuggel" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: shetlandmuseumandarchives.org.uk">[Shetland Museum &amp; Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">shetlandmuseumandarchives.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on shetlandmuseumandarchives.org.uk.</span></span></span> This is one of the strongest signs of old folklore: it is not confined to one literary tale, but has left marks on the map.

## Witches, charms and fear in seventeenth-century Shetland

Shetland witchcraft tradition has two layers that should not be confused. One is folklore: stories of witches, curses, second sight, charms, healing, protection and dealings with trows. The other is legal history: real people accused, tried and sometimes executed under early modern witchcraft law. The two overlap because trial records often preserve what officials believed, feared or claimed about local supernatural practice.

Shetland Museum and Archives states that the first major witch trial in Shetland took place in 1616, after the fall of Earl Patrick Stewart and the arrival of a new administration at Scalloway. The museum suggests there were probably fewer than ten witch-burnings in Shetland, while stressing that even that number was “ten too many”.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.shetlandmuseumandarchives.org.uk/blog/shetland-witches" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: shetlandmuseumandarchives.org.uk">[Shetland Museum &amp; Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">shetlandmuseumandarchives.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Shetland Museum &amp; Archives Witchcraft in Shetland &#124; Shetland Museum &amp; Archives</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Shetland Museum &amp; Archives Witchcraft in Shetland &#124; Shetland Museum &amp; Archives</span></span></span> This comparatively small number should not make the subject seem minor. For a small island society, even a handful of executions left a deep moral and imaginative mark.

The 1616 cases show how Shetland witchcraft accusations could blend demonic language, sea danger and trow belief. The museum’s account of Catherine Johnson says she was accused not only of relations with the devil but also of talking with trows in the kirkyards of Hillswick and Stenness. Jonka Dynneis was linked with a husband in danger offshore and later with a curse said to have caused a man to perish at sea.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.shetlandmuseumandarchives.org.uk/blog/shetland-witches" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: shetlandmuseumandarchives.org.uk">[Shetland Museum &amp; Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">shetlandmuseumandarchives.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Shetland Museum &amp; Archives Witchcraft in Shetland &#124; Shetland Museum &amp; Archives</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Shetland Museum &amp; Archives Witchcraft in Shetland &#124; Shetland Museum &amp; Archives</span></span></span> These details matter because they show how Shetland’s sea-risk, churchyards, trows and witchcraft could all be drawn into one explanatory net.

Modern Shetland is also rethinking this history publicly. A 2024 Shetland News report recorded a new memorial at Gallow Hill in Scalloway to commemorate people killed during Shetland’s seventeenth-century witchcraft trials, including Barbara Tulloch and her daughter Helen among the last recorded executions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.shetnews.co.uk/2024/04/07/new-memorial-scalloway-remembers-victims/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: shetnews.co.uk">[Shetland News]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">shetnews.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on shetnews.co.uk.</span></span></span> That modern act of remembrance changes how the folklore is read: not as spooky entertainment alone, but as part of a history of accusation, gender, fear and power.

## Stones, giants and story-shaped places

Shetland’s prehistoric and natural landscape is thick with later stories. Standing stones, cairns, headlands, skerries and lochs are often explained through giants, trows or old supernatural events. This does not mean the stories reveal the archaeological purpose of the monuments. Rather, they show how later Shetlanders made ancient or striking places meaningful.

Standing stones are a good example. A NorthLink Ferries guide notes that many Shetland standing stones may be around 5,000 years old and that almost all are now associated with entertaining folk tales.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.northlinkferries.co.uk/shetland-blog/shetland-standing-stones/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: northlinkferries.co.uk">[NorthLink Ferries]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">northlinkferries.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on northlinkferries.co.uk.</span></span></span> Shetland.org’s trow account gives one popular explanation: some standing stones are said to be trows caught out by sunrise and turned to stone.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.shetland.org/blog/shetland-folklore" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: shetland.org">[Shetland.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">shetland.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on shetland.org.</span></span></span> This is a familiar folklore mechanism across Britain, where stones become petrified dancers, giants, witches or sinners, but Shetland gives the motif its own trowie form.

Giant legends work similarly. Petta Water has been described in local retelling as a giant’s footprint, while the Unst giants Herman and Saxa explain dramatic coastal features around Hermaness and Saxa Vord.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://shetlandwithlaurie.com/the-blog/shetland-folklore-series-a-land-of-giants" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: shetlandwithlaurie.com">[Shetland With Laurie]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">shetlandwithlaurie.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Shetland With Laurie Shetland folklore series: A land of Giants</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Shetland With Laurie Shetland folklore series: A land of Giants</span></span></span> These stories do not ask the reader to believe that giants literally shaped the land. Their value is interpretive: they turn topography into memory, and memory into story.

## Winter customs: skeklers and Up Helly Aa

Shetland’s customary folklore is not confined to old supernatural beings. Winter performance is one of its most visible living traditions, especially through guizing, straw disguise and fire festivals.

The skekler tradition is especially striking. Shetland Museum and Archives describes skeklers as groups of guizers who visited houses late in the year dressed in straw suits and face-covering hats. It was considered bad luck to turn them away; householders welcomed them in, and the visitors played music and danced in exchange for food and drink.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.shetlandmuseumandarchives.org.uk/blog/keep-a-look-out-for-skeklers-this-halloween" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: shetlandmuseumandarchives.org.uk">[Shetland Museum &amp; Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">shetlandmuseumandarchives.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on shetlandmuseumandarchives.org.uk.</span></span></span> The museum’s Customs and Folklore gallery also lists a skeklers costume as a highlight, showing how a once-ephemeral house-visiting custom has become part of curated heritage.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.shetlandmuseumandarchives.org.uk/visit/galleries/customs-and-folklore" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: shetlandmuseumandarchives.org.uk">[Shetland Museum &amp; Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">shetlandmuseumandarchives.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Shetland Museum &amp; Archives Customs and Folklore &#124; Shetland Museum &amp; Archives</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Shetland Museum &amp; Archives Customs and Folklore &#124; Shetland Museum &amp; Archives</span></span></span>

Up Helly Aa is more famous, but it needs careful handling. It is often marketed through Viking imagery, torches and a burning galley, yet it is not an unbroken pagan survival from the Viking Age. The official Up Helly Aa history says that around 1870 a group of young Lerwick men introduced new ideas into older seasonal proceedings: the name, more elaborate guizing and a torchlight procession. Viking themes began appearing from 1877, the galley arrived in the late 1880s, the “Guizer Jarl” in 1906, and the annual Jarl Squad only after the First World War.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.uphellyaa.org/about-up-helly-aa/history/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: uphellyaa.org">[uphellyaa.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">uphellyaa.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">History of Up Helly Aa &#124; Up Helly Aa</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">History of Up Helly Aa &#124; Up Helly Aa</span></span></span>

That does not make Up Helly Aa “fake”. It makes it a modern tradition built from older materials: Yule timing, guizing, local satire, fire, civic performance and Shetland’s Norse identity. This is exactly how folklore often works. A custom can be relatively recent in its present form while still expressing older cultural memory and community values.

## Where the old sources end and modern retellings begin

Shetland folklore survives through several channels, and they do not all carry the same kind of evidence. The oldest printed collections, such as Spence’s *Shetland Folk-Lore* and the Folklore Society’s *County Folklore* volume, are essential because they preserve material gathered from older oral tradition. They are not neutral recordings in a modern academic sense, but they are close to the world in which many stories were still circulating by fireside, in dialect and through local memory.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Shetland_Folk-Lore" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: en.wikisource.org">[Wikisource]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">en.wikisource.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Shetland Folk-Lore</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Shetland Folk-Lore</span></span></span><span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/stream/countyfolklore03folkuoft/countyfolklore03folkuoft_djvu.txt" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Internet Archive Full text of &quot;County folklore</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Internet Archive Full text of &quot;County folklore</span></span></span>

Museums and archives add another layer. Shetland Museum and Archives interprets folklore through exhibitions, objects, reconstructed settings, blog essays and public workshops, while its gallery framing reminds readers that belief culture often leaves fewer objects than farming, fishing or domestic life.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.shetlandmuseumandarchives.org.uk/visit/galleries/customs-and-folklore" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: shetlandmuseumandarchives.org.uk">[Shetland Museum &amp; Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">shetlandmuseumandarchives.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Shetland Museum &amp; Archives Customs and Folklore &#124; Shetland Museum &amp; Archives</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Shetland Museum &amp; Archives Customs and Folklore &#124; Shetland Museum &amp; Archives</span></span></span> The wider Scottish oral record is also accessible through Tobar an Dualchais, the University of Edinburgh-linked resource containing more than 40,000 oral recordings from the 1930s onwards, including stories, songs, music, poetry and factual information searchable by topic and contributor.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://gaelic.ed.ac.uk/gaelic-collections/tobar-an-dualchais-kist-o-riches" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gaelic.ed.ac.uk">[Gaelic at the University of Edinburgh]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gaelic.ed.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on ed.ac.uk.</span></span></span>

Modern retellings on tourism sites, local blogs, podcasts, school resources, guided walks and festival interpretation keep the stories public. They are valuable, but they should be read as retellings unless they point back to older collectors, oral recordings, archives or named local tradition-bearers. This distinction is especially important with selkies, trows and Up Helly Aa, because all three are popular outside Shetland and can easily be flattened into generic fantasy or tourist imagery.

## What Shetland adds to the UK folklore map

Shetland matters because it complicates any simple idea of “British folklore”. Its stories are Scottish, but not Highland Gaelic; Norse-influenced, but not simply Scandinavian; island-based, but not isolated from wider North Atlantic tradition. Its folklore repeatedly returns to the practical realities of island life: the danger of water, the darkness of winter, the loneliness of moorland travel, the uncertainty of fishing, the importance of music, the pressure of small communities and the presence of older languages in place names.

The county’s most memorable figures each belong to a particular environment. Trows belong to knowes, hills and night music. Selkies belong to seals, skins, skerries and longing for the sea. [Njuggels]({{ 'njuggels/' | relative_url }}) belong to lochs, burns, mills and unsafe journeys. Witches belong both to story and to court records, where fear could become fatal. Skeklers and Up Helly Aa belong to winter performance, disguise and community self-invention.

For readers following folklore across UK counties and mapped areas, Shetland is therefore not just another northern outpost. It is one of the clearest examples of how historic identity, language, landscape and legend can reinforce one another. Its folklore is not best approached as a cabinet of monsters, but as a living map: hills with hidden people, stones with old explanations, waters with dangerous invitations, and winter streets still brightened by costume, music and fire.
