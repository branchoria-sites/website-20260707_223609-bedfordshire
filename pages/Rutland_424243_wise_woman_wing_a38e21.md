## Introduction

Amelia Woodcock, remembered as the Wise Woman of Wing, is one of [Rutland]({{ 'why-rutlands-folklore-is-much-in-little/' | relative_url }})’s most revealing nineteenth-century folklore figures because her story sits exactly on the line between healing, commerce and witchcraft suspicion. She was not a storybook witch in the old trial-and-broomstick sense. The strongest evidence presents her as a village healer and herbal practitioner whose reputation grew so large that patients came to Wing, and later Oakham, from well beyond the immediate parish. Contemporary and near-contemporary accounts describe crowds, carriages, lodging houses, bottles of medicine, chemists’ supplies, sceptical observers and people who believed intensely that she could cure them.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/stream/notesqueries55unse/notesqueries55unse_djvu.txt" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Internet Archive Full text of &quot;Notes and queries</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Internet Archive Full text of &quot;Notes and queries</span></span></span>



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/Rutland_424243_wise_woman_wing_a38e21-overview.webp" | relative_url }}" alt="Overview image for Wise Woman" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

Her importance to Rutland folklore is not that she proves supernatural healing was “real”, but that she shows how such belief worked in ordinary county life. In a small village, a woman with practical knowledge of wounds, herbs and salves could become a local institution; the same reputation that brought respect and income could also attract suspicion. Modern Wing heritage material still remembers her cottage site in City Yard, and even preserves the darker claim that she was wrongly accused of witchcraft.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.rutlandhistory.org/walks/winga4.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: rutlandhistory.org">[rutlandhistory.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">rutlandhistory.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Wing Village Walk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wing Village Walk</span></span></span>

## Amelia Woodcock’s reputation as a healer

Amelia Woodcock was born Amelia Dexter, probably about 1816 at Barleythorpe. A Rutland Local History and Record Society account traces her through Hambleton, her marriage in January 1842 to Matthew Woodcock, and family moves through Egleton and Wing. By the mid-1840s she was living in Wing, where four of her children were born between 1845 and 1854; another child was baptised at Egleton in 1857.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.rutlandhistory.org/HRW/chapter-030" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: rutlandhistory.org">[rutlandhistory.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">rutlandhistory.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Heritage of Rutland Water</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Heritage of Rutland Water</span></span></span>

It was in Wing that she became known as the Wise Woman of Wing. The phrase “wise woman” matters. In English folk usage it did not simply mean “witch”. It usually pointed to someone believed to possess special practical, herbal or magical knowledge: a person consulted for illness, misfortune, suspected bewitchment, lost goods or other hard-to-solve troubles. Owen Davies’s work on English cunning-folk describes these practitioners as small-scale but valued providers of popular magic, often dealing with everyday problems rather than grand occult ritual.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://books.google.com/books/about/Popular_Magic_Cunning_folk_in_English_Hi.html?id=LHpeAAAACAAJ" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: books.google.com">[Google Books]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">books.google.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Books Popular Magic: Cunning-folk in English History</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Books Popular Magic: Cunning-folk in English History</span></span></span>

The Rutland evidence suggests Amelia’s earliest reputation was medical rather than spectacularly magical. The local history account says villagers first visited her for ailments because she was “clever in the cure of slight wounds”, and that stories of her ability to treat the sick spread through the surrounding district. By 1851, the number of people who believed in her healing powers had become extraordinary for a village setting.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.rutlandhistory.org/HRW/chapter-030" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: rutlandhistory.org">[rutlandhistory.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">rutlandhistory.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Heritage of Rutland Water</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Heritage of Rutland Water</span></span></span>

The 1876 account in *Notes and Queries*, written by Cuthbert Bede, is more sceptical but extremely valuable because it preserves how her reputation was remembered within living memory. Bede said she had no hospital-nursing experience and no formal medical training, yet had “rapidly established” a reputation for treating diseases, including serious conditions such as cancer. He also records the familiar pattern by which herbal practice became mass practice: at first she gathered herbs from fields and woods, but once her fame grew she had no time to gather them herself and turned to drugs ordered from a chemist.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/stream/notesqueries55unse/notesqueries55unse_djvu.txt" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Internet Archive Full text of &quot;Notes and queries</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Internet Archive Full text of &quot;Notes and queries</span></span></span>

That shift is important. It keeps Amelia out of the simple “old herb woman in a cottage” stereotype. She belonged to the nineteenth-century medical marketplace as well as to folklore. She used reputation, remedies, supply chains and patient traffic. Her practice seems to have mixed village herbalism, proprietary medicine, personal authority and the power of expectation.

## Wing became a healing destination

The most striking feature of Amelia Woodcock’s story is scale. This was not just a neighbourly woman giving salve to people in the next cottage. The sources describe Wing as temporarily reshaped by the traffic of patients.

Cuthbert Bede wrote that although Amelia remained in a humble Wing cottage, she was visited daily by people who came in their own carriages. He had also heard that medical men came to consult her. Patients were taken in order, “without distinction of rank”, and so many arrived that some had to lodge in the village or nearby until she could see them.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/stream/notesqueries55unse/notesqueries55unse_djvu.txt" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Internet Archive Full text of &quot;Notes and queries</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Internet Archive Full text of &quot;Notes and queries</span></span></span>

The Rutland Local History and Record Society account makes the same point in more local terms. It says hundreds of ailing people were drawn to the once quiet village, and that the constant flow of patients benefited publicans, shopkeepers and cottagers who could accommodate them. This is one of the most vivid details in the whole tradition: belief in healing did not float above village life; it changed the village economy.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.rutlandhistory.org/HRW/chapter-030" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: rutlandhistory.org">[rutlandhistory.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">rutlandhistory.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Heritage of Rutland Water</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Heritage of Rutland Water</span></span></span>

Modern Wing walking material preserves the place-memory attached to this traffic. City Yard, near Top Street and Wing New Hall, is said to take its name from the well-to-do people who came in carriages from nearby cities to consult Amelia. The same walking guide identifies the site of her now-demolished cottage as one of a former row behind City Yard House.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.rutlandhistory.org/walks/winga4.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: rutlandhistory.org">[rutlandhistory.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">rutlandhistory.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Wing Village Walk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wing Village Walk</span></span></span>

That etymology should be treated carefully. Local explanations of place-names can be late or romantic. Even a modern local-history blog that went looking for the Wise Woman of Wing noted the appeal of the City Yard story while also expressing caution about popular etymology.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://liberalengland.blogspot.com/2011/07/in-search-of-wise-woman-of-wing.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: liberalengland.blogspot.com">[Liberal England]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">liberalengland.blogspot.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">in search of wise woman of wing</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">in search of wise woman of wing</span></span></span> But as folklore, the association is still valuable. Whether or not the name began with Amelia’s visitors, Wing now uses City Yard as the physical anchor for remembering her.

## Visitors, newspapers and the village economy

Amelia’s fame was large enough to enter the newspapers. A Rutland Local History and Record Society chapter quotes the *Stamford Mercury* for 10 September 1852, which carried two accounts of her life and activities at Wing, one from an Oakham correspondent and another from Uppingham. The chapter also quotes a later *Stamford Mercury* obituary from 29 May 1863, which said that her Wing house became too small, that she bought a house at Oakham, and that she established an omnibus to meet the railway trains.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.rutlandhistory.org/HRW/chapter-030" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: rutlandhistory.org">[rutlandhistory.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">rutlandhistory.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Heritage of Rutland Water</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Heritage of Rutland Water</span></span></span>

The railway detail changes how the story should be read. Wing was close to the Midland Railway between Stamford and Oakham, and Bede’s account explicitly locates the village in relation to that railway.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/stream/notesqueries55unse/notesqueries55unse_djvu.txt" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Internet Archive Full text of &quot;Notes and queries</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Internet Archive Full text of &quot;Notes and queries</span></span></span> Amelia’s fame therefore belonged to a modernising county as much as to an older rural world. Rail travel, newspapers and chemists helped a “wise woman” reputation travel faster and farther.

The reported omnibus is especially telling. It suggests a practical business system around the healer: patients arriving by train, transport meeting them, consultations managed by order, lodgings filling up, bottles and jars of medicine issued in quantity. This was folk healing, but it was not small in operation.

The evidence also shows how her practice could become entertainment, scandal and warning. The Rutland history chapter quotes an 1852 *Stamford Mercury* story about a man sent to collect a bottle of her medicine who sampled it on the way home, found it not unpleasant, drank more, and was discovered “dead drunk” by the roadside. The joke depends on public curiosity about the Wise Woman’s “compound” and hints that some mixtures may have been strongly alcoholic or at least stimulating.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.rutlandhistory.org/HRW/chapter-030" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: rutlandhistory.org">[rutlandhistory.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">rutlandhistory.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Heritage of Rutland Water</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Heritage of Rutland Water</span></span></span>

A more serious newspaper report from 17 February 1854 concerned Oliver Epery, a twenty-year-old patient from Earith in Huntingdonshire, who died suddenly while staying in Mrs Woodcock’s house. The inquest found no evidence of an immediate cause and returned a verdict of natural causes.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.rutlandhistory.org/HRW/chapter-030" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: rutlandhistory.org">[rutlandhistory.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">rutlandhistory.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Heritage of Rutland Water</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Heritage of Rutland Water</span></span></span> This episode does not prove wrongdoing, but it shows the risk surrounding any popular healer with very ill patients: if people arrived desperate, some would inevitably die while under care.

## What did her medicines look like?

The surviving descriptions point to a mixture of herbs, salves, commercial drugs and large-volume household medicine. Amelia’s practice began with plants gathered from local fields and woods. The Rutland account suggests remedies associated with familiar folk-herbal plants such as betony, dandelion, herb robert, celandine and field poppy, while rightly phrasing some of this as what she “could have used” rather than as a complete prescription book.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.rutlandhistory.org/HRW/chapter-030" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: rutlandhistory.org">[rutlandhistory.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">rutlandhistory.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Heritage of Rutland Water</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Heritage of Rutland Water</span></span></span>

The stronger evidence comes from Bede’s *Notes and Queries* account, which includes orders she sent to a chemist. The writing is difficult and the spelling irregular, but Bede identifies ingredients such as quinine, sal volatile, aniseed and Spanish juice. He also says the chemist supplied cart-loads, and sometimes van-loads, of drugs.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/stream/notesqueries55unse/notesqueries55unse_djvu.txt" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Internet Archive Full text of &quot;Notes and queries</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Internet Archive Full text of &quot;Notes and queries</span></span></span>

That chemist story is one of the best windows into Amelia’s reputation. According to Bede, the chemist first visited her hoping for business, but she mistook him for a patient, diagnosed him with an “ulcerated liver”, sold him a bottle, and only later learned he was a supplier. He then became one of her regular sources of drugs.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/stream/notesqueries55unse/notesqueries55unse_djvu.txt" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Internet Archive Full text of &quot;Notes and queries</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Internet Archive Full text of &quot;Notes and queries</span></span></span> The anecdote is comic and sceptical, but it also shows how nineteenth-century folk medicine could overlap with formal trade.

Davies’s study of cunning-folk in the nineteenth-century medical marketplace helps explain this overlap. He notes that herbalism was a major part of cunning-folk medical activity, but that the boundaries were complex: “herbalists” ranged from humble gatherers of simples to medical botanists, occult practitioners and respectable high-street sellers. Many such practitioners did not appear clearly in trade directories, and some people who looked respectable may still have offered magical diagnosis or cures.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/C39904711CB1F582D472DBE54ECF1BAA/S0025727300064711a.pdf/cunningfolk_in_the_medical_marketplace_during_the_nineteenth_century.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridge.org">[Cambridge University Press &amp; Assessment]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">University Press &amp; Assessment</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">University Press &amp; Assessment</span></span></span>

Amelia fits that blurred world. She was not a licensed doctor, and Bede did not think she had formal medical education. But she was not merely a back-lane superstition either. She bought supplies, distributed medicines, dealt with large numbers of patients and became known across class lines.

## Why cunning folk were both trusted and feared

Cunning-folk were trusted because they offered help where ordinary medicine, money and social status did not guarantee relief. They were feared because their authority looked like hidden knowledge. The same person who could “know” an illness, cure a wound or make a mixture might also be suspected of using uncanny power.

Davies distinguishes charmers from cunning-folk: charmers cured particular ailments and usually did not diagnose broadly, deal with witchcraft or take payment, whereas cunning-folk offered a wider range of services. From a medical point of view, he argues, their most important role was often the prevention, identification and curing of witchcraft.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/C39904711CB1F582D472DBE54ECF1BAA/S0025727300064711a.pdf/cunningfolk_in_the_medical_marketplace_during_the_nineteenth_century.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridge.org">[Cambridge University Press &amp; Assessment]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">University Press &amp; Assessment</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">University Press &amp; Assessment</span></span></span> Amelia’s surviving Rutland material is more strongly medical than anti-witchcraft, but the label “wise woman”, the claims of special cure and the later accusation tradition place her close to that wider cunning-folk world.

The nineteenth century did not simply abandon witchcraft belief after the old witch trials ended. Davies notes that although the 1736 Witchcraft Act encouraged educated people to treat witchcraft as a thing of the past, popular belief persisted among the wider population.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/C39904711CB1F582D472DBE54ECF1BAA/S0025727300064711a.pdf/cunningfolk_in_the_medical_marketplace_during_the_nineteenth_century.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridge.org">[Cambridge University Press &amp; Assessment]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">University Press &amp; Assessment</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">University Press &amp; Assessment</span></span></span> That context matters for Rutland. Amelia lived long after the age of formal English witch trials, but villagers, patients and newspapers still had a vocabulary for suspicious power.

The Rutland history chapter says some people thought Amelia was either a charlatan or a witch, though it concludes that she seems to have been guilty of no more than notable knowledge of herbal medicines and salves.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.rutlandhistory.org/HRW/chapter-030" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: rutlandhistory.org">[rutlandhistory.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">rutlandhistory.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Heritage of Rutland Water</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Heritage of Rutland Water</span></span></span> The modern Wing village walk goes further, saying she committed suicide after being wrongly accused of witchcraft.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.rutlandhistory.org/walks/winga4.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: rutlandhistory.org">[rutlandhistory.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">rutlandhistory.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Wing Village Walk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wing Village Walk</span></span></span> That claim is part of today’s local retelling, but it needs careful handling because other accounts record her death at Mount Pleasant in 1863 and her burial with her husband Matthew at Hambleton, without establishing the suicide story as securely as her healing reputation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.rutlandhistory.org/HRW/chapter-030" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: rutlandhistory.org">[rutlandhistory.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">rutlandhistory.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Heritage of Rutland Water</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Heritage of Rutland Water</span></span></span>

So was Wing’s Wise Woman really a witch? In evidence-based terms, no clear source proves that she practised harmful witchcraft, or that she was prosecuted as a witch, or that she should be understood as a witch in the sensational sense. The better reading is that she was a successful wise woman or folk healer whose reputation was powerful enough to attract both faith and suspicion.

## The class tension in her story

One reason Amelia Woodcock’s story is so memorable is that it unsettles class expectations. She was the wife of a working man, described in the sources as a labouring man or stonemason, yet she attracted wealthy patients and possibly medical men.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/stream/notesqueries55unse/notesqueries55unse_djvu.txt" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Internet Archive Full text of &quot;Notes and queries</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Internet Archive Full text of &quot;Notes and queries</span></span></span>

That reversal is central to the folklore. A humble cottage became a place where rank was temporarily rearranged. Bede says patients were seen in turn without distinction of rank.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/stream/notesqueries55unse/notesqueries55unse_djvu.txt" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Internet Archive Full text of &quot;Notes and queries</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Internet Archive Full text of &quot;Notes and queries</span></span></span> For a rural healer, this was part of the authority: the Wise Woman’s power did not come from a university, a title or a professional licence, but from reputation and results believed by her clients.

At the same time, class shaped how people wrote about her. Sceptical observers often framed her through “quackery”, odd spelling, strange surroundings or credulous patients. Bede’s account preserves useful details, but it is not neutral; it enjoys the comedy of the chemist’s false diagnosis and the peculiarity of her written orders.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/stream/notesqueries55unse/notesqueries55unse_djvu.txt" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Internet Archive Full text of &quot;Notes and queries</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Internet Archive Full text of &quot;Notes and queries</span></span></span> Another *Notes and Queries* correspondent, however, insisted that Bede’s account was “in no wise exaggerated” and that her fame had spread far and wide, while adding that simple medicines, moderate regimen and belief in supernatural powers probably all contributed to her reputation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/stream/notesqueries55unse/notesqueries55unse_djvu.txt" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Internet Archive Full text of &quot;Notes and queries</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Internet Archive Full text of &quot;Notes and queries</span></span></span>

That second testimony is unusually balanced. It does not ask the reader to choose between “miracle worker” and “fraud”. It allows for practical remedies, changes in diet or routine, patient confidence, theatrical presence and supernatural belief all working together. For a public folklore page, that is probably the fairest interpretation.

## The “weird-like” Wise Woman in memory

The most folkloric portrait of Amelia comes from the 1876 follow-up in *Notes and Queries*. A woman recalled being taken with two invalid sisters to consult the Wise Woman nearly twenty years earlier. They travelled about thirty miles, found many patients waiting, and learned that some had lodged overnight in or near Wing to secure an early audience. After waiting for hours, they gained access.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/stream/notesqueries55unse/notesqueries55unse_djvu.txt" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Internet Archive Full text of &quot;Notes and queries</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Internet Archive Full text of &quot;Notes and queries</span></span></span>

Her description of Amelia is vivid: thin, bony, “weird-like”, with a sharp voice and a dark, searching countenance. A pot boiled on the fire; Amelia added something to it and muttered words the visitor could not understand. Then she suddenly declared that all of them were consumptive and would be dead within three years. The medicines they took away had no noticeable effect; one sister died ten years later, but not of consumption, while the narrator and another sister survived.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/stream/notesqueries55unse/notesqueries55unse_djvu.txt" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Internet Archive Full text of &quot;Notes and queries</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Internet Archive Full text of &quot;Notes and queries</span></span></span>

This recollection may tell us as much about atmosphere as medicine. The boiling pot, the muttered words, the sudden diagnosis and the frightened visitors are exactly the details by which a healer becomes legendary. They also show why patients might feel awe even when the cure failed. Amelia’s practice was not only pharmacological. It was performative: the room, the queue, the reputation, the direct speech and the confidence of diagnosis all mattered.

For Rutland folklore, this is the moment where the Wise Woman becomes more than a local medical curiosity. She becomes a figure people could picture and retell: the healer in the cottage, surrounded by anxious strangers, dispensing bottles and certainty.

## Oakham, death and the afterlife of the legend

Amelia’s career did not remain fixed in Wing. The Rutland Local History and Record Society account says she was living at Mount Pleasant, Oakham, by 1857, and the quoted *Stamford Mercury* obituary says the move followed the Wing house becoming too small. The same obituary remembered her as retiring in disposition but astonishingly busy.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.rutlandhistory.org/HRW/chapter-030" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: rutlandhistory.org">[rutlandhistory.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">rutlandhistory.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Heritage of Rutland Water</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Heritage of Rutland Water</span></span></span>

She died at Mount Pleasant on 24 May 1863, aged forty-seven. Her husband Matthew lived until 1877, and both were buried at Hambleton. The Rutland account records the inscription on their headstone in Hambleton churchyard, naming Amelia as Matthew’s wife and giving her date of death and age.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.rutlandhistory.org/HRW/chapter-030" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: rutlandhistory.org">[rutlandhistory.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">rutlandhistory.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Heritage of Rutland Water</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Heritage of Rutland Water</span></span></span>

That grave matters because it grounds a story that could otherwise drift into pure legend. Amelia is not a vague “old witch” of folklore. She was a documented woman moving between Barleythorpe, Hambleton, Egleton, Wing and Oakham; a wife and mother; a healer with a businesslike flow of patients; and a person whose memory was later shaped by both local pride and suspicion.

The modern survival of the story is also very Rutland. Wing walking leaflets, village-history material, local blogs, county heritage writing and folklore podcasts keep her in circulation as one of the county’s distinctive figures.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.rutlandhistory.org/walks/winga4.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: rutlandhistory.org">[rutlandhistory.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">rutlandhistory.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Wing Village Walk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wing Village Walk</span></span></span> She belongs alongside Oakham’s [horseshoes]({{ 'horseshoes/' | relative_url }}), Wing’s maze and Rutland’s village customs as a reminder that folklore is not only monsters and ghosts. Sometimes it is a queue outside a cottage, a bottle of medicine, a rumour of witchcraft and a community trying to decide whether unusual knowledge is a gift, a trick or something more troubling.

## Why the Wise Woman of Wing matters to Rutland

Amelia Woodcock matters because her story gives Rutland a human-scale example of nineteenth-century belief culture. In a county often represented by churches, stone villages, estate landscapes and ceremonial customs, she brings in the domestic and bodily side of folklore: illness, pain, fear, hope, herbs, gossip, payment, class and reputation.

She also shows how old belief survived in modern conditions. Her fame spread through word of mouth, but also through newspapers. She gathered or used herbs, but also bought drugs from chemists. She lived in a village cottage, but later used railway-connected transport and dealt with patients from beyond Rutland. She was remembered as a healer, but also shadowed by accusations of witchcraft.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/stream/notesqueries55unse/notesqueries55unse_djvu.txt" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[archive.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Internet Archive Full text of &quot;Notes and queries</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Internet Archive Full text of &quot;Notes and queries</span></span></span>

The best way to read her is not as a confirmed witch, and not simply as a fraud. She was a wise woman in the practical English sense: a local healer whose perceived knowledge became socially powerful. Some patients probably found comfort, routine, mild remedies or placebo benefit. Some were disappointed. Some may have been too ill for anyone to help. Around all of them grew the story that Wing had a woman who could cure what others could not.

That is why Amelia Woodcock still belongs on Rutland’s folklore map. Her legend is small enough to stand in a cottage yard, but large enough to reveal a whole county world of healing belief, rural economy and uneasy respect for those who seemed to know more than ordinary people should.

  function encodeQuery(value) {
    return encodeURIComponent(String(value || '').replace(/\s+/g, ' ').trim()).replace(/%20/g, '+');
  }
  function buildUrl(query, reference, marketId) {
    var cfg = markets[marketId] || markets[defaultMarket];
    if (!cfg) return '#';
    var template = String(cfg.url_template || 'https://www.ebay.com/sch/i.html?_nkw={query}');
    var url = template.replace('{query}', encodeQuery(query || '')).replace('{query_plain}', String(query || ''));
    var joiner = url.indexOf('?') >= 0 ? '&' : '?';
    var params = [];
    if (cfg.mkrid) params.push('mkrid=' + encodeURIComponent(cfg.mkrid));
    if (cfg.campaign_id) params.push('campid=' + encodeURIComponent(cfg.campaign_id));
    params.push('mkevt=1');
    params.push('mkcid=1');
    params.push('toolid=' + encodeURIComponent(cfg.tool_id || '10001'));
    if (reference) params.push('customid=' + encodeURIComponent(reference).slice(0, 256));
    return url + joiner + params.join('&');
  }

        .map(function (option) { return option.value; })
        .filter(function (marketId) { return !!markets[marketId]; });
    }
    return Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]'))

      .filter(function (marketId) { return !!markets[marketId]; });
  }
  function applyMarket(section, marketId, persist) {
    var available = availableMarkets(section);
    if (available.indexOf(marketId)< 0) marketId = available[0] || defaultMarket;
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-localized-link]')).forEach(function (link) {

      var reference = link.getAttribute('data-ebay-reference') || '';
      link.setAttribute('href', buildUrl(query, reference, marketId));
    });
    var select = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-select]');
    if (select && available.indexOf(marketId) >= 0) select.value = marketId;
    var selectedText = marketId;
    if (select && select.selectedIndex >= 0) selectedText = select.options[select.selectedIndex].text;
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-selected-market-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = selectedText;
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-selected-market-flag], [data-ebay-trigger-market-flag]')).forEach(function (flag) {
      flag.className = flagClass(marketId);
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-trigger-market-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = selectedText;
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-domain-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = domainLabel(marketId);
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-option]')).forEach(function (button) {
      button.setAttribute('aria-selected', button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-option') === marketId ? 'true' : 'false');
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      var active = button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button') === marketId;
      button.setAttribute('aria-pressed', active ? 'true' : 'false');
    });
    section.setAttribute('data-ebay-selected-market', marketId);
    if (persist) {
      try { if (window.localStorage) localStorage.setItem('phoenix-ebay-market', marketId); } catch (err) {}
    }
    closeMenu(section);
  }
  sections.forEach(function (section) {
    if (section.getAttribute('data-ebay-localized-init') === '1') return;
    section.setAttribute('data-ebay-localized-init', '1');
    var available = availableMarkets(section);
    var marketId = defaultMarket;
    if (window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation && window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation.inferMarket) {
      marketId = window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation.inferMarket(available, {
        storageKey: 'phoenix-ebay-market',
        defaultMarket: defaultMarket
      });
    } else if (available.indexOf(defaultMarket)< 0) {
      marketId = available[0] || defaultMarket;
    }
    var select = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-select]');

      select.addEventListener('change', function () {
        applyMarket(section, select.value, true);

    var menu = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-menu]');

      trigger.addEventListener('click', function (event) {

        var expanded = trigger.getAttribute('aria-expanded') === 'true';

        button.addEventListener('click', function (event) {

          applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-option'), true);
        });
      });
      document.addEventListener('click', function () { closeMenu(section); });
    }

      button.addEventListener('click', function () {
        applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button'), true);
      });
    });
    applyMarket(section, marketId, false);
