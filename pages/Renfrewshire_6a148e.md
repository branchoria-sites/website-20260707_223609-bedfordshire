## Introduction

For this project, Renfrewshire is best understood in its historic county sense: the old County of Renfrew on the south bank of the Clyde, stretching from the Glasgow edge to the Clyde coast. That older county is broader than today’s Renfrewshire Council area, because the historic county also takes in areas now governed as Inverclyde and East Renfrewshire, while some places near Glasgow have shifted administratively over time. Wikishire describes the county as a small but populous shire on the Clyde, with Paisley as its largest town and Renfrew as the county town; modern summaries likewise note that the traditional county was larger than the present council area.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://wikishire.co.uk/wiki/Renfrewshire" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wikishire.co.uk">[Wikishire]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wikishire.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wikishire.co.uk.</span></span></span>

## What makes Renfrewshire folklore distinctive?

Renfrewshire’s folklore sits at the meeting point of medieval Strathclyde, west-coast Christianity, Clyde industry and small-town memory. Instead of a remote Highland world of second sight and fairy cattle, the county’s best-attested traditions often cling to churches, weaving communities, old stones, gallows sites and civic monuments. That makes its folklore feel close to ordinary public space: a crossroads in Paisley, a stone in a hotel enclosure, a cross now kept inside Paisley Abbey, or a statue of an old village piper.

The historic county also has an important boundary problem for readers. A story from Greenock, Gourock, Pollokshaws, Newton Mearns, Eastwood, Barrhead or Paisley may all belong naturally to Renfrewshire in the older county framework, even though modern council names divide the area. This matters because saint cults, witch accusations, weaving networks, parish boundaries and oral tradition do not always follow present-day administrative lines. The old county stretched from the Glasgow suburbs and Paisley westwards to Port Glasgow, Greenock and Gourock, with the Clyde forming a powerful northern edge.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://wikishire.co.uk/wiki/Renfrewshire" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wikishire.co.uk">[Wikishire]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wikishire.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wikishire.co.uk.</span></span></span>

That geography helps explain the character of the lore. Renfrewshire is a county of routes: rivers, ferries, roads, pilgrimage tracks, industrial streets and parish paths. Its traditions are often about movement across those routes: saints arriving from Ireland, a miraculous stone crossing the sea, accused witches being taken to trial, weavers carrying songs, and later visitors walking Paisley’s witch-trial trail.

## The Paisley witches: Renfrewshire’s central dark legend

The best-known Renfrewshire tradition is the Paisley, Bargarran or Renfrewshire witch hunt of 1696–97. It began with Christian Shaw, the eleven-year-old daughter of John Shaw of Bargarran near Erskine. She accused a household servant, Katherine Campbell, after a quarrel over milk; the case widened into claims of torment, possession, strange objects being produced from the mouth, and a growing list of alleged witches. Glasgow Libraries’ Bargarran collection summarises the core of the case: Christian alleged in 1696 that she had been bewitched by a female servant, many locals were implicated, and seven people were executed in Paisley on 10 June 1697.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://libcat.csglasgow.org/web/arena/bargarran-witches" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: libcat.csglasgow.org">[Glasgow Libraries]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">libcat.csglasgow.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Glasgow Libraries The Bargarran Witches Collection</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Glasgow Libraries The Bargarran Witches Collection</span></span></span>

The case was not just a vague legend. It is heavily documented by Scottish witch-trial standards. The University of Edinburgh’s Survey of Scottish Witchcraft is the major digital resource for early modern Scottish witch-hunting, and its records include the Renfrewshire cases and the Privy Council commission that investigated Christian Shaw’s accusations.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://witches.hca.ed.ac.uk/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: witches.hca.ed.ac.uk">[witches.hca.ed.ac.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">witches.hca.ed.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on ed.ac.uk.</span></span></span> Paisley’s Enchanted Threads, a local history and oral-history project, gives the names and short biographies of the victims and notes that more than thirty people were suspected or accused during the first months of 1697 before the eventual trial focused on seven.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.paisleysenchantedthreads.co.uk/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: paisleysenchantedthreads.co.uk">[paisleysenchantedthreads.co.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">paisleysenchantedthreads.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on paisleysenchantedthreads.co.uk.</span></span></span>

The people remembered as the main victims were Margaret Lang, John Lindsay, James Lindsay, John Lindsay in Barloch, Katherine Campbell, Margaret Fulton and Agnes Naismith; John Reid, another accused man, died in prison before the executions. The executions took place at Gallow Green in Paisley, and the dead became attached to Maxwellton Cross in local memory. Paisley’s Enchanted Threads describes the 1697 event as the last major witch hunt and mass execution of witches in Western Europe, a wording also repeated in local remembrance and tourism material.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.paisleysenchantedthreads.co.uk/about/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: paisleysenchantedthreads.co.uk">[paisleysenchantedthreads.co.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">paisleysenchantedthreads.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on paisleysenchantedthreads.co.uk.</span></span></span>

What makes the story folkloric, rather than merely legal history, is what happened after it. Agnes Naismith’s supposed dying curse became a Paisley legend, with later misfortunes sometimes blamed on the witch-trial wrongs. A horseshoe at Maxwellton Cross was said to hold down or avert the curse; modern accounts describe how the horseshoe was lost during twentieth-century roadworks, later replaced, and folded into regeneration-era retellings of Paisley’s identity.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.scottishcommunityalliance.org.uk/briefings/paisley-lays-ghost-to-rest" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: scottishcommunityalliance.org.uk">[Scottish Community Alliance]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">scottishcommunityalliance.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Scottish Community Alliance Paisley lays ghost to rest</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Scottish Community Alliance Paisley lays ghost to rest</span></span></span>

The tale also has an uncomfortable double legacy. Christian Shaw is remembered both as the child whose evidence helped condemn innocent people and as a later figure in Renfrewshire’s thread industry. Paisley’s Enchanted Threads records modern community interest in her later life, while textile-history summaries credit Shaw of Bargarran with beginning fine linen-thread production at Bargarran House in 1722.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.paisleysenchantedthreads.co.uk/news/christian-shaws-signature-discovered/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: paisleysenchantedthreads.co.uk">[paisleysenchantedthreads.co.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">paisleysenchantedthreads.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on paisleysenchantedthreads.co.uk.</span></span></span> That overlap is part of why the story still grips Paisley: the witch hunt is not sealed off from civic history, industry or local pride. It sits uneasily inside them.

## Saints, stones and healing power

Renfrewshire’s saint legends are older in flavour than the witch stories, but they are often preserved through medieval church tradition rather than continuous oral folklore. St Mirin, Paisley’s patron saint, is remembered as an Irish monk and missionary associated with the town’s early Christian origins. Paisley’s own visitor material openly frames the earliest story as “myth and mystery”, then tells of Mirin’s journey from Ireland, his mission in Scotland, St Mirin’s Croft, and a shrine that became a centre of pilgrimage.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://paisley.is/stories-from-renfrewshire/monks-missionaries-and-pilgrims/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: paisley.is">[Paisley]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">paisley.is</span><span class="citation-popover-title">​Monks. missionaries and pilgrims</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">​Monks. missionaries and pilgrims</span></span></span>

St Mirin’s legend matters because it gives Paisley a sacred origin story. The saint is commemorated in Paisley Abbey, St Mirin’s Cathedral, St Mirren Football Club, street names and civic memory. The Diocese of Paisley continues to present him as the patron saint of Paisley, while acknowledging the legendary nature of many miracle stories attached to him.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://rcdop.org.uk/news/the-significance-of-st-mirin" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: rcdop.org.uk">[Diocese of Paisley]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">rcdop.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">the significance of st mirin</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">the significance of st mirin</span></span></span> For folklore readers, Mirin is less a “mythic creature” than a local founder-saint: a figure through whom the town explains its religious prestige and long memory.

St Conval’s tradition is even more dramatically folkloric. Inchinnan and Renfrew preserve the story of a saint who crossed the sea from Ireland on a stone. Historic Environment Scotland’s record for the old Inchinnan church site states that a later legend, written down in the sixteenth-century Aberdeen Breviary, tells how St Conval stood beside the Irish Sea seeking God’s guidance and the stone beneath him broke loose and carried him to Inchinnan.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://portal.historicenvironment.scot/apex/f?p=1505%3A300%3A%3A%3A%3A%3AVIEWTYPE%2CVIEWREF%3Adesignation%2CSM2792" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: portal.historicenvironment.scot">[portal.historicenvironment.scot]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">portal.historicenvironment.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on historicenvironment.scot.</span></span></span>

The stone itself became part of a healing landscape. Gilbert Márkus’s study of St Conval and Inchinnan explains that medieval readings associated the saint with a famous stone on which he was said to have sailed from Ireland, and that water collected in the stone was used in healing practices; the study also stresses that such texts tell us more about the later cult of the saint than about the historical man.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://eprints.gla.ac.uk/253885/1/253885.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: eprints.gla.ac.uk">[Enlighten Publications]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">eprints.gla.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Enlighten Publications</span></span></span> That distinction is important: the story is not evidence that a saint rode a rock across the sea, but it is strong evidence that medieval and later Renfrewshire communities imagined stones, water, saints and healing as deeply connected.

## Old stones: from Strathclyde crosses to “druid” memories

Renfrewshire’s legendary stones show how folklore often grows around objects whose original purpose has become obscure. The Barochan Cross is one of the county’s most important early medieval monuments. Historic Environment Scotland dates it to roughly AD 900–1100 and describes it as one of only three complete crosses surviving from the kingdom of Strathclyde, richly carved on all four sides and now displayed in Paisley Abbey.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.historicenvironment.scot/visit/all/barochan-cross/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: historicenvironment.scot">[Historic Environment Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">historicenvironment.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-title">barochan cross</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">barochan cross</span></span></span>

Although Barochan Cross is primarily an early Christian monument, it sits in the same imaginative landscape as saint legends, pilgrimage roads and heroic Strathclyde memory. Local-history accounts describe its earlier position near Houston and Langbank, its move to Corslie Hill, its popularity as a day-trip destination, and its eventual removal for conservation before being placed in Paisley Abbey in 1981.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://rlhf.info/barochan-cross/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: rlhf.info">[rlhf.info]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">rlhf.info</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on rlhf.info.</span></span></span> The cross is a good example of how a sacred object can change roles: from religious marker to antiquarian curiosity, local outing, heritage exhibit and possible internal-link anchor for early medieval Strathclyde folklore.

The Clochoderick Stone near Kilbarchan has a looser, more legendary aura. The Northern Antiquarian records traditions that it was known as a rocking stone, linked with “druids”, justice-giving and the burial-place of the Strathclyde king Ryderch Hael, while also noting older place-name references and the nearby St Bride’s Burn.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.thenorthernantiquarian.org/2019/05/17/clochoderick-stone/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: thenorthernantiquarian.org">[The Northern Antiquarian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">thenorthernantiquarian.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">clochoderick stone</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">clochoderick stone</span></span></span> This is exactly the kind of tradition that needs careful wording. The stone’s folklore is real as folklore, but “druid” labels in Scottish antiquarian writing often tell us more about later attempts to explain mysterious stones than about demonstrable ancient ritual.

St Conval’s Chariot and the Argyll Stone near Renfrew add another layer. Local learning material places St Conval’s Chariot in the grounds of the Normandy Hotel on Inchinnan Road, describing it as a round-topped boulder within railings beside the Argyll Stone.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://myinchinnan.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2019/08/Stories-Stones-and-Saints-Learning-Resource-_compressed.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: myinchinnan.org.uk">[myinchinnan.org.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">myinchinnan.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Stories, Stones and Saints</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Stories, Stones and Saints</span></span></span> These stones turn a roadside corner into a compact memory-site: saintly travel, healing water, medieval devotion and later political legend gathered in one enclosure.

## Fairies, kelpies and ghosts: present, but not the county’s strongest evidence

Readers often ask whether Renfrewshire has fairy hills, black dogs or water monsters like other parts of Scotland. The honest answer is that these motifs are not as strongly or famously attested here as the witch hunt, saint cults and legendary stones. Renfrewshire Local History Forum notes that the Gryffe valley has had tales of fairies, kelpies, ghosts, tricksters and con-men, while also admitting that many of these stories are no longer widely told.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://rlhf.info/renfrewshire-folklore/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: rlhf.info">[rlhf.info]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">rlhf.info</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on rlhf.info.</span></span></span>

That does not make the material worthless. It tells us something valuable about modern folklore loss. Renfrewshire almost certainly shared wider Lowland Scottish motifs: water spirits near burns and rivers, fairy or otherworldly associations around old stones, ghost stories in old buildings, and local uncanny tales around roads and graveyards. But when sources are thin, the responsible approach is to treat such motifs as regional background unless a named Renfrewshire place or collector preserves a clearer version.

Black-dog folklore is a good example. Scotland has spectral-hound traditions, including the Highland otherworldly hound often called the cù sìth, and wider British black-dog legends are well documented.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.atlasobscura.com/articles/cu-sith-scottish-folklore-black-dog" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: atlasobscura.com">[Atlas Obscura]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">atlasobscura.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Atlas Obscura Death as a Dog: The Cù Sith of Scotland</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Atlas Obscura Death as a Dog: The Cù Sith of Scotland</span></span></span> However, live source evidence for a distinctive Renfrewshire black dog is weak compared with, for example, East Anglia’s Black Shuck or Lancashire and Yorkshire’s spectral dogs. On a Renfrewshire page, black dogs are best mentioned as a comparison point, not presented as a major county tradition unless a stronger local source is found.

Paisley ghost stories are easier to trace, though they are often modern and tourism-facing. Local and paranormal writing has gathered haunted-place claims around Paisley Abbey and former hospital buildings, including tales of unexplained noises, apparitions and Lady Glen-Coats.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://paisley.org.uk/news/exploring-the-mysteries-and-legends-of-paisley-abbey" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: paisley.org.uk">[Paisley.org.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">paisley.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">exploring the mysteries and legends of paisley abbey</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">exploring the mysteries and legends of paisley abbey</span></span></span> These accounts are part of modern folklore and visitor culture, but they are not as historically grounded as the witch-trial material. They matter because they show how older places continue to generate stories, especially in a town already associated with witchcraft memory.

## Weavers, pipers and living custom

Renfrewshire folklore is not only supernatural. Paisley and Kilbarchan preserve strong traditions of music, weaving and local performance. Habbie Simpson, the piper of Kilbarchan, is one of the county’s great folk figures. He is remembered through Robert Sempill’s “Lament for Habbie Simpson”, the verse form later associated with Robert Burns, and through a statue on Kilbarchan’s Steeple Building. Modern accounts note that Kilbarchan residents are still informally known as “Habbies”, and that during Lilias Day celebrations the piper traditionally dresses as Habbie Simpson.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Habbie_Simpson" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Habbie Simpson</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Habbie Simpson</span></span></span>

That makes Habbie a bridge between literary history and custom. He is not a supernatural being, but he belongs firmly in Renfrewshire’s traditional culture: a named local performer turned communal emblem. His memory also connects naturally with folk music, village pageantry and Scots verse.

Paisley’s weaving tradition carries a similar cultural charge. The town’s shawls, thread mills and Paisley pattern belong to industrial history, but they also shape identity, song and memory. Robert Tannahill, the Paisley “weaver poet”, wrote songs that fed into Scottish musical tradition, and the modern Tannahill Weavers took their name from him and from Paisley’s weaving community.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://projects.handsupfortrad.scot/hall-of-fame/the-tannahill-weavers/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: projects.handsupfortrad.scot">[Hands Up for Trad]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">projects.handsupfortrad.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on handsupfortrad.scot.</span></span></span> In Renfrewshire, folklore is therefore not just about strange beings; it is also about how working communities turned labour, music and local pride into remembered tradition.

## How Renfrewshire retells its folklore today

Modern Renfrewshire retells its folklore through memorials, walking routes, local-history websites, heritage projects, churches, museums and festivals. The Paisley witch hunt has become the most visible example. Paisley’s Enchanted Threads was explicitly created to collect community stories about the Renfrewshire Witch Hunt of 1697, working with oral-history methods and local volunteers to record how people learned, interpreted and preserved the story.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.paisleysenchantedthreads.co.uk/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: paisleysenchantedthreads.co.uk">[paisleysenchantedthreads.co.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">paisleysenchantedthreads.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on paisleysenchantedthreads.co.uk.</span></span></span>

This modern retelling has changed the emphasis. Older versions often centred the curse, the horseshoe and the uncanny power of the dead. More recent heritage work tends to foreground injustice, gender, law, fear and remembrance. The supernatural frame has not disappeared, but the victims are increasingly presented as wronged people rather than as glamorous “witches”. That shift is important for a public-facing folklore page: the story can be mysterious and memorable without turning executions into entertainment.

The saints and stones are retold differently. St Mirin remains part of living religious and civic identity, while St Conval’s Chariot survives as a small, curious heritage site where saint legend, healing folklore and early medieval Christianity meet. Barochan Cross, protected indoors at Paisley Abbey, has moved from landscape monument to curated artefact. These changes do not kill folklore; they alter how people encounter it. A visitor now meets some Renfrewshire legends through plaques, websites, tours and conservation displays rather than fireside storytelling.

## What to remember about Renfrewshire folklore

Renfrewshire’s folklore is strongest when read through place. Paisley gives the county its great witch-trial memory, St Mirin’s sacred origin story and much of its weaving culture. Inchinnan and Renfrew hold the stone-riding legend of St Conval. Kilbarchan gives the county Habbie Simpson, a folk musician whose memory still enters custom. Houston, Barochan and Kilbarchan preserve older stone landscapes where early Christian monuments and antiquarian legends overlap.

The county’s legendary identity is therefore not a simple catalogue of fairies, witches, ghosts and saints. It is a layered map of memory: medieval devotion, early modern fear, industrial pride, village performance and modern attempts to repair or reinterpret the past. Its most powerful stories survive because they are attached to things people can still point to: a cross, a croft, a stone, a road junction, an abbey, a festival figure, and the uneasy public memory of a witch hunt that Renfrewshire has never quite stopped retelling.
