## Introduction

The Stone of Mannan is the small, weathered boulder set high on a larger stone beside Clackmannan Tolbooth and the Mercat Cross on Main Street. It matters because it turns one town-square object into a founding emblem for the whole of [Clackmannanshire]({{ 'where-do-clackmannanshires-legends-begin/' | relative_url }}): a possible prehistoric or pre-Christian stone, a place-name puzzle, a memory of an early district called Manau, and a much-loved Bruce legend about a lost glove. Historic Environment Scotland records the monument under several names — Clach Manau, Mannan’s Stone and Bruce’s Stone — and treats it as nationally important because of its association with the origins of both town and shire.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://portal.historicenvironment.scot/apex/f?p=1505%3A300%3A%3A%3A%3A%3AVIEWTYPE%2CVIEWREF%3Adesignation%2CLB1947" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: portal.historicenvironment.scot">[Historic Environment Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">portal.historicenvironment.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Historic Environment ScotlandClackmannan Tolbooth, Mercat Cross and Clackmannan Stone, Main Street, Clackmannan (LB1947)...</span></span></span>



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/Clackmannanshire_a9d2bc_stone_of_mannan_6d1d35-overview.webp" | relative_url }}" alt="Overview image for Mannan Stone" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

The safest way to read the tradition is not to force one neat answer. The stone’s physical history is partly documented, its early meaning is uncertain, and its popular legends have clearly changed over time. That mixture is exactly why it is such a good folklore landmark for “the Wee County”: Clackmannan’s name, civic pride and heroic storytelling all gather round a plain lump of whinstone that has become anything but ordinary.

## What the Stone of Mannan is

The object people usually point to as the Stone of Mannan is not the whole tall monument. The older part is the smaller capstone: a broken, rounded whinstone boulder, bound with iron rods, now mounted on a much larger irregular block. Historic Environment Scotland says the capstone is considered by some sources to date from the third or second millennium BC, while other accounts more cautiously call it pre-Christian; the supporting block was brought from Abbey Craig and erected in 1833.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://portal.historicenvironment.scot/apex/f?p=1505%3A300%3A%3A%3A%3A%3AVIEWTYPE%2CVIEWREF%3Adesignation%2CLB1947" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: portal.historicenvironment.scot">[Historic Environment Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">portal.historicenvironment.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Historic Environment ScotlandClackmannan Tolbooth, Mercat Cross and Clackmannan Stone, Main Street, Clackmannan (LB1947)...</span></span></span>

That 1833 display matters. The present monument looks like an ancient standing stone at first glance, but its current form is a nineteenth-century arrangement: an old boulder lifted up and made prominent in the town centre. According to Historic Environment Scotland, the stone is believed to have been originally located at Lookabootye Brae, closer to the River Forth, and may then have been kept at Clackmannan Tower before being moved to its present position.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://portal.historicenvironment.scot/apex/f?p=1505%3A300%3A%3A%3A%3A%3AVIEWTYPE%2CVIEWREF%3Adesignation%2CLB1947" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: portal.historicenvironment.scot">[Historic Environment Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">portal.historicenvironment.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Historic Environment ScotlandClackmannan Tolbooth, Mercat Cross and Clackmannan Stone, Main Street, Clackmannan (LB1947)...</span></span></span>

The setting is part of the story. The stone stands beside the late-sixteenth-century tolbooth belfry and the Mercat Cross, whose capital bears weathered Bruce arms. Clackmannanshire Council’s visitor information presents the three as a linked civic group: the Tolbooth, the cross and the ancient standing stone of Mannan, all gathered in the old county town’s centre.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.clacks.gov.uk/visiting/clackmannan/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: clacks.gov.uk">[Clacks]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">clacks.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Clacks Tolbooth Cross and the Stone of Mannan</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Clacks Tolbooth Cross and the Stone of Mannan</span></span></span>

This is why the stone feels larger than its size. It is not only an antiquity, and not only a curiosity. It sits where law, market rights, local government, family power and old story meet. The stone’s folklore has survived partly because it is so visible: it is not hidden in a field, but placed where everyday town life has repeatedly had to look at it, explain it and claim it.

## Mannan, Manau and Bruce traditions

The name “Clackmannan” is usually explained through the stone, but the second half of the name has produced several rival stories. In modern visitor retellings, one strand links the stone with a sea-god called Mannau or Mannan; another links it with Robert the Bruce’s lost glove, supposedly called a “mannan”. Clackmannanshire Council gives both versions side by side, describing the stone as associated with “the Sea God Mannau” and with Bruce’s glove.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.clacks.gov.uk/visiting/clackmannan/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: clacks.gov.uk">[Clacks]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">clacks.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Clacks Tolbooth Cross and the Stone of Mannan</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Clacks Tolbooth Cross and the Stone of Mannan</span></span></span>

Historic Environment Scotland gives a more cautious and useful split. One interpretation is that the name refers to a Celtic god Manau and that the stone was connected with pagan worship. Another suggestion is that Manau refers not to a god but to a late Iron Age territory covering the wider area of Stirlingshire and Clackmannanshire.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://portal.historicenvironment.scot/apex/f?p=1505%3A300%3A%3A%3A%3A%3AVIEWTYPE%2CVIEWREF%3Adesignation%2CLB1947" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: portal.historicenvironment.scot">[Historic Environment Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">portal.historicenvironment.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Historic Environment ScotlandClackmannan Tolbooth, Mercat Cross and Clackmannan Stone, Main Street, Clackmannan (LB1947)...</span></span></span>

That distinction changes how the folklore is understood. A “god of the stone” story makes the object feel like a pagan survival. A “territory of Manau” reading makes it a political or ethnic memory: the stone of a district, people or gathering-place rather than necessarily a shrine to a named deity. Either way, the stone preserves the sense that Clackmannan’s identity reaches back beyond the medieval burgh and the Bruce family.

The Bruce legend is more colourful, but less credible as an origin for the place-name. In the nineteenth-century version recorded by Historic Environment Scotland, King Robert Bruce was staying at Clackmannan, stopped at the stone while travelling, left his glove on it, and sent a servant back to the “clack” to fetch his “mannan”. The servant’s reported reply — that he would “look about” there — also explains Lookabootye Brae in the same story-cycle.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://portal.historicenvironment.scot/apex/f?p=1505%3A300%3A%3A%3A%3A%3AVIEWTYPE%2CVIEWREF%3Adesignation%2CLB1947" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: portal.historicenvironment.scot">[Historic Environment Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">portal.historicenvironment.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Historic Environment ScotlandClackmannan Tolbooth, Mercat Cross and Clackmannan Stone, Main Street, Clackmannan (LB1947)...</span></span></span>

As etymology, that tale fails. Historic Environment Scotland notes that Clackmannan is recorded from the twelfth century onwards, so a story set around Robert the Bruce cannot explain the original name.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://portal.historicenvironment.scot/apex/f?p=1505%3A300%3A%3A%3A%3A%3AVIEWTYPE%2CVIEWREF%3Adesignation%2CLB1947" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: portal.historicenvironment.scot">[Historic Environment Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">portal.historicenvironment.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Historic Environment ScotlandClackmannan Tolbooth, Mercat Cross and Clackmannan Stone, Main Street, Clackmannan (LB1947)...</span></span></span> As folklore, however, it works beautifully. It ties the old stone to the county’s most prestigious heroic association, turns a puzzling place-name into a memorable anecdote, and gives the landscape a spoken catchphrase: “look about ye”.

## Why the stone defines the Wee County

Clackmannanshire is small enough that a single town-square monument can carry unusual weight. Historic Environment Scotland states that the county name appears to derive from the stone, and that in the early nineteenth century it was described as a symbol of town power, comparable to the Clachnacudden of Inverness, with local privileges imagined to depend in some way on its existence.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://portal.historicenvironment.scot/apex/f?p=1505%3A300%3A%3A%3A%3A%3AVIEWTYPE%2CVIEWREF%3Adesignation%2CLB1947" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: portal.historicenvironment.scot">[Historic Environment Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">portal.historicenvironment.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Historic Environment ScotlandClackmannan Tolbooth, Mercat Cross and Clackmannan Stone, Main Street, Clackmannan (LB1947)...</span></span></span>

That is a striking form of civic folklore. The stone was not merely “old”; it was treated as if the community’s standing, rights and continuity were bound up with it. Even when the details are uncertain, the pattern is familiar across Scottish local tradition: a stone becomes the visible proof that a place has depth, authority and a story older than written charters.

The Third Statistical Account of Clackmannan parish, republished locally, shows how strongly this feeling persisted. It calls the Stone of Mannan unique, describes it as sacred to the spirit of a local deity, and says it was erected on its whinstone plinth in 1833 to keep it safe from “curio-hunters”. It also remarks that local sentiment remained strong regarding the stone.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.clackmannanshire.scot/index.php/history/third-statistical-account-clackmannan-parish" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: clackmannanshire.scot">[clackmannanshire.scot]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">clackmannanshire.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Third Statistical Account</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Third Statistical Account</span></span></span>

That protective instinct is important. The 1833 mounting can be read as antiquarian display, but also as rescue. The stone was made monumental so that it could not easily be chipped away, lost, stolen or ignored. In folklore terms, it was turned from a remembered object into a public emblem.

The surrounding buildings reinforce that emblematic role. The Tolbooth represented civic authority; the Mercat Cross represented market privilege and public proclamation; the Bruce arms on the cross tied the town to one of Scotland’s great historical families. Placing the Stone of Mannan among them made a layered statement: Clackmannan was not just an administrative place, but a place whose authority reached back into legend.

## How antiquarians reshaped the story

The Stone of Mannan is a good example of how local folklore is preserved and reshaped by antiquarians, ministers, surveyors and heritage bodies. The object may be old, but the story most visitors now meet is filtered through nineteenth- and twentieth-century interpretation.

The Ordnance Survey Name Books are especially revealing because they show how place-name traditions could vary even in the Victorian period. Scotland’s People gives Lookaboutye Brae in Clackmannan as an example: the 1861–62 Name Book recorded a tradition that the name came from one of the Scottish kings, identified there as James III, stopping during a hunt and saying it was a fine place to “look about ye”. Scotland’s People then notes that modern online retellings often attach the phrase instead to Robert the Bruce losing a glove.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.scotlandspeople.gov.uk/help-and-support/guides/ordnance-survey-name-books" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: scotlandspeople.gov.uk">[scotlandspeople.gov.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">scotlandspeople.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Ordnance Survey Name Books &#124; Scotland&#x27;s People</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Ordnance Survey Name Books &#124; Scotland&#x27;s People</span></span></span>

That shift matters because it shows the Bruce story was not the only available explanation. The same memorable phrase could be moved from one king to another, and from a landscape-view anecdote to a glove-retrieval legend. Folklore often behaves like that: a good phrase survives, while the named hero changes to suit local prestige or popular memory.

The Third Statistical Account also shows the blend of tradition and historical correction. It repeats the Bruce-and-Douglas hunting story, in which Bruce leaves his glove in the cleft of the stone and sends Douglas back with the instruction to look about at the stone for his “mannan”. But it immediately adds that this cannot explain the town-name, because the name goes back to the time of David I.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.clackmannanshire.scot/index.php/history/third-statistical-account-clackmannan-parish" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: clackmannanshire.scot">[clackmannanshire.scot]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">clackmannanshire.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Third Statistical Account</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Third Statistical Account</span></span></span>

That is the central tension in the Stone of Mannan’s legend. The story is too late to be true as an origin, but too locally meaningful to dismiss as worthless. It tells us less about the actual naming of Clackmannan and more about how later residents wanted the name to feel: ancient, royal, witty, rooted in a recognisable slope and attached to a physical stone still standing in public view.

Modern heritage interpretation continues this balancing act. Historic Environment Scotland keeps the competing names and explanations together: possible prehistoric or pre-Christian origin, Manau as deity or territory, Bruce’s Stone as legend, and the caution that the Bruce tale cannot account for a twelfth-century place-name.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://portal.historicenvironment.scot/apex/f?p=1505%3A300%3A%3A%3A%3A%3AVIEWTYPE%2CVIEWREF%3Adesignation%2CLB1947" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: portal.historicenvironment.scot">[Historic Environment Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">portal.historicenvironment.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Historic Environment ScotlandClackmannan Tolbooth, Mercat Cross and Clackmannan Stone, Main Street, Clackmannan (LB1947)...</span></span></span> The result is not a debunking, but a clearer way to enjoy the tradition without mistaking every retelling for early evidence.

## What readers should remember

The Stone of Mannan defines Clackmannan because it is both modest and overloaded. Physically, it is a battered whinstone boulder on a nineteenth-century support. Culturally, it carries the name of the town and county, a possible memory of Manau, a claim of pagan sacredness, a Bruce legend, and a civic role beside the Tolbooth and Mercat Cross.

The strongest evidence supports four careful conclusions. First, the stone is an old and nationally recognised monument, though its exact original date and function remain uncertain. Second, its present form and position were shaped in 1833, when the boulder was raised and preserved in the town centre. Third, the Bruce glove tale is a later origin legend rather than a reliable explanation of the place-name. Fourth, the legend still matters because it shows how Clackmannanshire has turned a single stone into a compact symbol of local identity.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://portal.historicenvironment.scot/apex/f?p=1505%3A300%3A%3A%3A%3A%3AVIEWTYPE%2CVIEWREF%3Adesignation%2CLB1947" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: portal.historicenvironment.scot">[Historic Environment Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">portal.historicenvironment.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Historic Environment ScotlandClackmannan Tolbooth, Mercat Cross and Clackmannan Stone, Main Street, Clackmannan (LB1947)...</span></span></span>

For folklore, that is the point. The Stone of Mannan is not valuable because every story about it can be proved. It is valuable because the stories cluster so tightly around one visible object: ancient territory, royal memory, local wordplay, civic pride and the habit of asking, whenever the county’s name is spoken, what kind of stone could have given a whole shire its name.
