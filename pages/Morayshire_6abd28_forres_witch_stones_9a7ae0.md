## Introduction

Forres became [Morayshire]({{ 'where-morayshire-turns-history-into/' | relative_url }})’s “witch town” not because one clean historical record proves it was a major execution centre, but because three kinds of memory have settled onto the same small landscape: Shakespeare’s *Macbeth*, the local Witches’ Stone near Cluny Hill, and the great carved monument known as Sueno’s Stone. The result is one of the strongest legend clusters in historic Morayshire: a place where visitors can stand beside real stones while hearing stories of prophecy, punishment, battle and old fear. The careful reading is more interesting than the sensational one. Macbeth’s witches belong first to literature and performance; the Witches’ Stone belongs to local memorial folklore whose exact historical basis is disputed; Sueno’s Stone is a genuine early medieval masterpiece later drawn into Macbeth legend. Together they show how Forres turns stone, drama and difficult witch-hunt memory into a haunted local identity.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.historicenvironment.scot/visit/all/suenos-stone/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: historicenvironment.scot">[historicenvironment.scot]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">historicenvironment.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Historic Environment Scotland Sueno&#x27;s Stone &#124; Historic Scotland</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Historic Environment Scotland Sueno&#x27;s Stone &#124; Historic Scotland</span></span></span>

## Macbeth fixed Forres in the supernatural imagination

Shakespeare did not invent Forres, but *Macbeth* gave the town a permanent place in the English-speaking imagination. In the play’s opening movement, the action is repeatedly placed around Forres: a camp near Forres, a heath near Forres, and Forres as Duncan’s palace. The Folger Shakespeare Library summarises the drama as a Scottish tragedy driven by witchcraft, prophecy and murder, with the “Weïrd Sisters” greeting Macbeth and Banquo after battle and predicting Macbeth’s rise to kingship.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.opensourceshakespeare.org/views/plays/play_view.php?Act=1&amp;Scope=act&amp;WorkID=macbeth&amp;msg=pl&amp;pleasewait=1" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: opensourceshakespeare.org">[Open Source Shakespeare]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">opensourceshakespeare.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on opensourceshakespeare.org.</span></span></span>

That matters for folklore because Shakespeare’s stage geography behaves differently from archaeology. A named place in a famous play can pull later stories towards it, even when the play is not good evidence for an eleventh-century event. The Royal Shakespeare Company notes that Shakespeare drew heavily on Raphael Holinshed’s *Chronicles* of 1587, but also reshaped the material, including politically useful changes to Banquo and his descendants. Forres, then, enters modern legend through a chain: medieval Scottish royal story, early modern chronicle, Shakespearean tragedy, then local and tourist retelling.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.rsc.org.uk/macbeth/about-the-play/dates-and-sources" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: rsc.org.uk">[Royal Society of Chemistry]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">rsc.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Dates and Sources &#124; Macbeth &#124; Royal Shakespeare Company &#124; Royal Shakespeare Company...</span></span></span>

This helps explain Macbeth’s Hillock, near Brodie between Forres and Nairn. Modern local tradition identifies it as the place where Macbeth met the three witches, and the site is now actively presented as part of the area’s visitor landscape. The tradition is valuable as folklore, but it should not be treated as a securely documented eleventh-century meeting-place. A recent academic abstract on Forres’s witch reputation describes Macbeth’s Hillock as a local landmark commemorating the famous meeting, while arguing that the meeting belongs to literary imagination rather than recoverable historical event.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.macbethshillock.co.uk/history" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: macbethshillock.co.uk">[macbethshillock]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">macbethshillock.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on macbethshillock.co.uk.</span></span></span>

The important distinction is simple: Forres’s Macbeth memory is real, but that does not mean Macbeth met witches there. The memory is real because people have named, visited, marketed, argued over and retold the place as Macbeth country. It is part of Morayshire’s folklore map because the story has attached itself to the land.

## Sueno’s Stone is older than the Macbeth legend

Sueno’s Stone gives Forres something far more solid than stage tradition: a major early medieval monument that can be studied, protected and visited. Historic Environment Scotland describes it as a gigantic Pictish cross-slab about seven metres tall, carved from local sandstone, and the tallest and most complex piece of early medieval sculpture in Scotland. Its carvings include a rare narrative battle scene and a scene long interpreted as royal inauguration.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.historicenvironment.scot/visit/all/suenos-stone/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: historicenvironment.scot">[Historic Environment Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">historicenvironment.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Historic Environment Scotland Sueno&#x27;s Stone &#124; Historic Scotland</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Historic Environment Scotland Sueno&#x27;s Stone &#124; Historic Scotland</span></span></span>

The official designation record places the stone in Rafford parish at grid reference NJ 04651 59534 and describes a late first-millennium cross-slab standing under a glass canopy. One face carries a ring-headed cross with interlace; another is divided into panels of horsemen, foot combat, a besieged stronghold, headless corpses and defeated forces. The same record is cautious about older romantic explanations: although the stone has traditionally been associated with Swein Forkbeard, Historic Environment Scotland says there appears to be no evidence supporting that link, and the original purpose, patron and exact circumstances of erection remain uncertain.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://portal.historicenvironment.scot/apex/f?p=1505%3A300%3A%3A%3A%3A%3AVIEWTYPE%2CVIEWREF%3Adesignation%2CSM90292" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: portal.historicenvironment.scot">[Historic Environment Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">portal.historicenvironment.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on historicenvironment.scot.</span></span></span>

That uncertainty is exactly why Sueno’s Stone became so available to legend. A huge carved stone, standing near Forres, showing battle and death, with an unclear original story, invites later explanation. Local Macbeth tradition has sometimes claimed that this was the crossroads where Macbeth encountered the witches, or that the witches were trapped within the stone. Historic interpretation, however, has to put the order the right way round: the stone is genuinely early medieval; the Macbeth-witch association is a later legendary overlay shaped by Shakespeare’s fame.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.historicenvironment.scot/visit/all/suenos-stone/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: historicenvironment.scot">[historicenvironment.scot]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">historicenvironment.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Historic Environment Scotland Sueno&#x27;s Stone &#124; Historic Scotland</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Historic Environment Scotland Sueno&#x27;s Stone &#124; Historic Scotland</span></span></span>

For Morayshire folklore, Sueno’s Stone is therefore doing two jobs at once. It is not merely a “mysterious stone” for spooky retellings. It is a nationally important monument whose carvings preserve a serious early medieval visual language, and it is also a landmark around which later people have built stories of prophecy, imprisonment and supernatural danger. The power of the place comes from that double life.

## The Witches’ Stone turns local horror into a landmark

The Witches’ Stone in Forres is a smaller object with a darker modern message. The Historic Environment Record for the Witches Stones, Forres, describes a surviving granite stone, about three feet square and one foot high, clasped by an iron band. Its plaque says that witches were rolled from Cluny Hill in spiked barrels and burned where the barrels stopped, and that the stone marks one such burning. The same record adds that this surviving stone is said to be the only remaining one of three, with a second boulder recorded in the garden of Trafalgar Place and a third reportedly broken up in 1802.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://her.aberdeenshire.gov.uk/Monument/MAB3401/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: her.aberdeenshire.gov.uk">[Aberdeen Historic Environment Record]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">her.aberdeenshire.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on aberdeenshire.gov.uk.</span></span></span>

Local visitor material preserves the same grim tradition. Forres Heritage presents the stone as a reminder of the town’s “sordid past” and says it marks the place where a woman believed to be a witch died after being thrown from Cluny Hill. Visit Forres repeats the plaque wording and frames the stone as a daily reminder of witch punishment in former centuries.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://forresheritage.co.uk/witches-stone/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: forresheritage.co.uk">[Forres Heritage]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">forresheritage.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on forresheritage.co.uk.</span></span></span>

The problem is that memorial folklore and trial history do not line up neatly. Julian Goodare, one of the leading historians associated with the Survey of Scottish Witchcraft, has specifically warned that the Forres plaque’s spiked-barrel story is not backed by known historical documentation, and that there is no documentary record of witches from Forres in the form claimed by the monument. He also notes that Forres’s Macbeth fame has encouraged confusion between Shakespeare’s witches and remembered witch-hunt victims.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.thebottleimp.org.uk/2013/11/remembering-scottish-witches/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: thebottleimp.org.uk">[The Bottle Imp]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">thebottleimp.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Bottle Imp Remembering Scottish Witches</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Bottle Imp Remembering Scottish Witches</span></span></span>

That does not make the Witches’ Stone meaningless. It changes what kind of evidence it is. It may not prove that accused witches were executed in the exact theatrical manner described on the plaque. It does prove that Forres has publicly chosen to remember witch persecution through a physical stone, a shocking story and a named place. As folklore, the stone is less a courtroom document than a local memory object: a place where fear of witchcraft, sympathy for victims, macabre storytelling and tourist curiosity all meet.

## What the witch-trial evidence can and cannot prove

The wider Scottish witch-hunt was very real. The University of Edinburgh’s Survey of Scottish Witchcraft was created to record people known to have been accused of witchcraft in Scotland between 1563 and 1736, and its dataset was designed to examine accusation, belief, trial procedure, chronology and geography. Its public FAQ says the Witchcraft Act was in force from 1563 to 1736, with major waves of accusation in 1590–91, 1597, 1628–30, 1649 and 1661–62.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://datashare.ed.ac.uk/handle/10283/45" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: datashare.ed.ac.uk">[Edinburgh DataShare]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">datashare.ed.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on ed.ac.uk.</span></span></span>

This matters because some modern local retellings blur scale. Visit Forres says “more than 4000 witches were killed in Moray during the 16th century”, but the stronger national evidence points to nearly 4,000 known accused people across Scotland, not executions in Moray alone. The University of Edinburgh dataset description gives the national scope as Scotland-wide accused witches between 1563 and 1736, while the FAQ places Grampian, including Aberdeen, at 7% of named accused witches, far below the Lothians.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://visitforres.scot/listing/the-witches-stone/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: visitforres.scot">[visitforres.scot]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">visitforres.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-title">the witches stone</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">the witches stone</span></span></span>

The better historical conclusion is not that Forres was innocent of witch belief, nor that its stone should be dismissed. It is that Forres’s reputation as a witch town rests on a mixture of evidence types: national witch-hunt history, local memorial tradition, Shakespearean association and later storytelling. A 2025 article in *Northern Scotland*, “Forres: A Hotbed of Witches?”, directly treats this reputation as a historical problem, noting Forres’s fame in *Macbeth*, Macbeth’s Hillock, and the Witches’ Stone as linked elements in the town’s witch identity.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.grafiati.com/es/literature-selections/missing-women-in-fiction/journal/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: grafiati.com">[Grafiati]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">grafiati.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Artículos de revistas: &quot;Missing women in fiction&quot; – Grafiati</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Artículos de revistas: &quot;Missing women in fiction&quot; – Grafiati</span></span></span>

For readers, that distinction is useful. The Witches’ Stone is not strongest when treated as proof of a precise execution method. It is strongest when read as evidence of how Morayshire remembers witch-hunting: through place, fear, pity and a vivid story that has outlived the paperwork.

## Why stones carry so much of the Forres story

Forres’s witch-and-Macbeth landscape works because stones seem to promise permanence. A play can feel airy, a rumour can feel slippery, but a stone beside a road appears to say: something happened here. That is why Sueno’s Stone, the Witches’ Stone and Macbeth’s Hillock form such a memorable cluster. They give the visitor a route through different layers of belief.

The layers are not the same:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **Sueno’s Stone** is the strongest archaeological object: an officially protected early medieval carved cross-slab of national importance, later absorbed into Macbeth legend.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.historicenvironment.scot/visit/all/suenos-stone/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: historicenvironment.scot">[Historic Environment Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">historicenvironment.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Historic Environment Scotland Sueno&#x27;s Stone &#124; Historic Scotland</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Historic Environment Scotland Sueno&#x27;s Stone &#124; Historic Scotland</span></span></span>
* **The Witches’ Stone** is the strongest memorial object: a local landmark whose plaque preserves a violent tradition, though historians question the documentary basis of the spiked-barrel story.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://her.aberdeenshire.gov.uk/Monument/MAB3401/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: her.aberdeenshire.gov.uk">[Aberdeen Historic Environment Record]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">her.aberdeenshire.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on aberdeenshire.gov.uk.</span></span></span>
* **Macbeth’s Hillock** is the strongest literary-tourist object: a named landscape associated with the witches of *Macbeth*, meaningful as local tradition but not secure as eleventh-century history.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.macbethshillock.co.uk/history" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: macbethshillock.co.uk">[macbethshillock]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">macbethshillock.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on macbethshillock.co.uk.</span></span></span>

Seen together, they explain why Forres stands out within Morayshire folklore. Many counties have witch memories; many Scottish places claim Macbeth associations; many northern landscapes have carved stones. Forres has all three pressed into a compact, walkable imaginative geography. That concentration makes the town feel unusually legible as a folklore place.

## Legend, trial history and tourist retelling

Modern Forres retells its witch traditions in a way that is both serious and marketable. Visitor pages point people to the Witches’ Stone, Macbeth’s Hillock and Sueno’s Stone; accommodation businesses use Macbeth’s witches as atmosphere; heritage pages present the Witches’ Stone as a grim reminder of persecution. This is not unusual or automatically disrespectful. Folklore survives partly because places keep finding new forms for old stories.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://forresheritage.co.uk/witches-stone/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: forresheritage.co.uk">[forresheritage.co.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">forresheritage.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on forresheritage.co.uk.</span></span></span>

The risk is flattening everything into one spooky tale. Shakespeare’s Weird Sisters were dramatic figures shaped by chronicle, theatre and early modern ideas of prophecy. Scotland’s accused witches were real people caught in legal, religious and social systems between 1563 and 1736. Sueno’s Stone belongs to a much older early medieval world of kingship, battle, religion and carved monumentality. When tourist retelling fuses them too tightly, Macbeth’s witches can accidentally swallow the memory of actual accused people, while the Pictish stone becomes only a prop in a Shakespeare story.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.rsc.org.uk/macbeth/about-the-play/dates-and-sources" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: rsc.org.uk">[rsc.org.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">rsc.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Royal Society of Chemistry</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Dates and Sources &#124; Macbeth &#124; Royal Shakespeare Company &#124; Royal Shakespeare Company...</span></span></span>

A better public telling lets the layers remain visible. It can still be atmospheric. It can still call Forres Morayshire’s witch town. But it should say clearly that the strongest evidence for Forres is not a single proven witch-burning at the stone. It is the survival of a place-based tradition where literature, memorial, antiquarian record, local pride and uneasy historical conscience overlap.

## Why Forres matters in Morayshire folklore

Forres matters because it shows how folklore is made from more than old tales. It is made from texts, monuments, plaques, maps, visitor routes, scholarship, local repetition and argument. The town’s witch reputation is not weakened by separating evidence from legend; it becomes richer. The reader can see how a Shakespearean heath, a Pictish battle stone and a roadside memorial have been drawn into one Morayshire story-world.

This also makes Forres a useful gateway to neighbouring Morayshire traditions. Burghead has its fire custom and Pictish survivals; Elgin has cathedral memory and darker historical tales; the Findhorn and Spey landscapes carry their own stories of danger, crossing and old belief. Forres, however, is the county’s clearest example of how famous literature can settle onto local ground and change the way a town remembers itself.

The final picture is not simple, and that is the point. Forres is not “Morayshire’s witch town” because every legend is literally true. It is Morayshire’s witch town because its stones have become meeting-points: between Macbeth and Moray, between archaeology and imagination, between real witch-hunt history and later memorial folklore, and between local place and national myth.
