## Introduction

St Fillan’s healing traditions in [Perthshire]({{ 'where-perthshires-strange-stories-meet/' | relative_url }}) centre on a simple but powerful idea: illness could be met through saintly objects, running water, ritual movement and local memory. The best-known examples are the eight healing stones kept at Killin, close to the Falls of Dochart, and the Holy Pool of Strathfillan near Tyndrum, where older accounts describe troubling rites for people thought to be mentally ill. These traditions matter because they show Perthshire folklore at its most layered: Christian saint-cult, river landscape, older ritual habits, parish record, antiquarian curiosity, and modern heritage all meeting in one small Highland district. The stories are vivid, but they need careful handling. They are not evidence that the stones or pool medically cured illness; they are evidence of how communities made sacred geography out of water, stone, suffering and hope. Historic Environment Scotland’s records place both the Killin stones and Strathfillan pool within the former county of Perthshire, even though the modern local authority is Stirling.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.trove.scot/place/24219" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: trove.scot">[Trove Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">trove.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">landSt Fillan&#x27;s Stones &#124; PlaceAlso known as St Fillan&#x27;s Ash Tree; ling Parish: Killin Former region: Location accurate to the nearest 10...</span></span></span>

## Where St Fillan’s healing landscape sits in Perthshire

St Fillan’s Perthshire cult is not attached to one isolated shrine. It links Killin, Glen Dochart, Strathfillan and Upper Strathearn through a chain of places where the saint’s name gathers around stones, water, relics and stories. For a folklore reader, the important point is that these are landscape traditions: the river, the mill, the pool, the ruined church, the saint’s “bed”, the bell and the stones all matter together.

Killin is the most accessible focus today. The former mill by the Bridge of Dochart, also known as St Fillan’s Mill or the Old Mill, stands on a traditional milling site associated with the saint. Canmore records that a succession of meal mills was said to have stood there, the earliest supposedly erected by St Fillan, and that the occupier in 1938 still observed the saint’s festival day by ceasing work.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://canmore.org.uk/site/24215/killin-dochart-mill" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: canmore.org.uk">[Canmore]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">canmore.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on canmore.org.uk.</span></span></span> The stones themselves are recorded separately as “St Fillan’s Stones”, with their location given at the north-west corner of Dochart Bridge, in Killin parish, former county of Perthshire.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.trove.scot/place/24219" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: trove.scot">[Trove Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">trove.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">landSt Fillan&#x27;s Stones &#124; PlaceAlso known as St Fillan&#x27;s Ash Tree; ling Parish: Killin Former region: Location accurate to the nearest 10...</span></span></span>

Strathfillan, farther west towards Tyndrum, gives the tradition a more disturbing and more explicitly therapeutic frame. The Holy Pool is recorded by Canmore as a natural pool at a sharp bend in the River Fillan, opposite Strathfillan Manse. Its archaeological record notes that it was famous for its connection with St Fillan and that, until the middle of the nineteenth century, people described as insane were bathed there.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://canmore.org.uk/site/23864/strathfillan-holy-pool" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: canmore.org.uk">[Canmore]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">canmore.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">strathfillan holy pool</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">strathfillan holy pool</span></span></span> Nearby, St Fillan’s Priory at Kirkton preserves the medieval church setting that helped turn a river pool into a remembered healing complex. The scheduled monument record describes the remains as a priory of Augustinian canons dedicated to St Fillan, with a foundation in 1317/18 and dissolution in 1607.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.trove.scot/designation/SM5585" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: trove.scot">[Trove Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">trove.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on trove.scot.</span></span></span>

The wider Perthshire setting matters because these places sit in the west of the historic county, an area now administered outside Perth and Kinross but still central to old Perthshire identity. This is exactly the kind of mismatch that folklore often exposes: stories follow glens, parishes, saints’ districts and remembered routes, not modern council borders.

## Killin’s body-shaped healing stones

The Killin stones are the gentler and more visitor-friendly side of the tradition. They are usually described as a set of eight water-worn stones, each linked to a part of the body or a kind of ailment. The basic practice was not complex: the appropriate stone was rubbed on, held near, or moved around the afflicted part. Modern heritage interpretation presents them as stones given healing power by St Fillan and still kept at the Old Mill beside the Falls of Dochart.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://killincdt.co.uk/project-oldmill" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: killincdt.co.uk">[killincdt.co.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">killincdt.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">project oldmill</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">project oldmill</span></span></span>

The most useful early archaeological summary comes from G. C. Cash’s 1912 account, preserved in the National Record of the Historic Environment. It states that in 1912 there were eight stones: seven water-worn pebbles and two larger socket stones, used for rubbing the bodies of invalids. It also notes that the Old Statistical Account of 1796 discussed the Holy Pool and bell but did not mention the Killin stones, which is important for judging the evidence. The stones are old as objects and old in local tradition, but the written trail for their exact healing use is clearer in nineteenth- and twentieth-century antiquarian recording than in the earliest parish account.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.trove.scot/place/24219" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: trove.scot">[Trove Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">trove.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">landSt Fillan&#x27;s Stones &#124; PlaceAlso known as St Fillan&#x27;s Ash Tree; ling Parish: Killin Former region: Location accurate to the nearest 10...</span></span></span>

Their power in folklore comes partly from resemblance. Britain Express, summarising the former Breadalbane Folklore Centre interpretation, describes the set as smooth stones shaped like the organs or body parts they were supposed to heal, with a liver-shaped stone for liver complaints as one example.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.britainexpress.com/scotland/Central-Scotland/museums/breadalbane-folklore-centre.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: britainexpress.com">[Britain Express]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">britainexpress.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on britainexpress.com.</span></span></span> This is often called sympathetic magic: the object works because it resembles, stands for, or touches the damaged part. In local saint-cult, however, the explanation becomes Christianised. The stones are not merely lucky pebbles; they are remembered as St Fillan’s stones, charged with authority by his holiness, his local presence and the repeated practice of the community.

The stones also had a seasonal life. The Trove record notes a custom, recorded by W. A. Gillies in 1938, in which the stones were taken out each year on 5 January and replaced on a fresh bed of straw, reeds and grass gathered from the river bank.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.trove.scot/place/24219" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: trove.scot">[Trove Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">trove.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">landSt Fillan&#x27;s Stones &#124; PlaceAlso known as St Fillan&#x27;s Ash Tree; ling Parish: Killin Former region: Location accurate to the nearest 10...</span></span></span> That detail is easy to miss, but it changes the meaning of the objects. They were not simply museum pieces. They were cared for, re-bedded and renewed, almost like household or shrine objects with an annual cycle. The straw and riverbank plants tied them back to place: not generic healing stones, but Killin stones, river stones, saint stones.

The modern Old Mill continues that community heritage framing. Killin and Ardeonaig Trust describes the Old Mill as home to the eight water-washed stones and says people still visit, hold and use them today.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://killincdt.co.uk/project-oldmill" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: killincdt.co.uk">[killincdt.co.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">killincdt.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">project oldmill</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">project oldmill</span></span></span> This is where the critique matters. As heritage, the stones are a rare survival of local ritual memory. As medicine, they should not be presented as a substitute for healthcare. Their contemporary value lies in touch, story, continuity and place-based identity, not in making unsupported medical claims.

## Holy pools, bells and uneasy cures

The Holy Pool tradition is more unsettling than the Killin stones because it centres on coercive treatment of people labelled “mad” or “insane” in older sources. The language in those sources belongs to earlier periods and should not be repeated uncritically as modern description. What the records preserve is a ritual cure tradition for severe mental distress or socially recognised mental disorder, using cold water, binding, vigil, saintly relics and an ordeal-like test.

The Old Statistical Account and later antiquarian accounts describe a ceremony in which the sufferer was dipped in St Fillan’s Pool, then taken to the chapel and left bound overnight. The University of Glasgow’s Saints in Scottish Place-Names database quotes the 1796 parish account’s disapproving description of the rite as a mixture of “Druidism and Popery”, with the bell set on the person’s head after a night in the chapel. It also notes that the bell had been locked up for some years to stop its use for “superstitious purposes”.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://saintsplaces.gla.ac.uk/place.php?id=1324652223" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: saintsplaces.gla.ac.uk">[saintsplaces.gla.ac.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">saintsplaces.gla.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on gla.ac.uk.</span></span></span>

The New Statistical Account gives a fuller nineteenth-century version. It says the ceremony was performed after sunset on the first day of the quarter, old style, and before sunrise the next morning. The person dipped in the pool was told to take three stones from the bottom, go three times round three cairns on the bank, and throw a stone at each. They were then taken to the ruins of St Fillan’s chapel, laid in a corner called St Fillan’s Bed, and left tied all night. If found loose in the morning, the cure was counted complete.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://stataccscot.ed.ac.uk/data/pdfs/account2/StAS.2.10.1076.P.Perth.Killin.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: stataccscot.ed.ac.uk">[stataccscot.ed.ac.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">stataccscot.ed.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">parish of killin</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">parish of killin</span></span></span>

Several things are happening here at once. The water marks a crossing into sacred danger; the three stones and three circuits give the rite a patterned, memorable form; the ruined chapel supplies saintly authority; the binding creates the test; and the bell or relic gives the ritual a material focus. The result is not a simple “holy well cure” but a whole sequence of actions staged across riverbank and church ruin.

The bell is especially important because it connects popular healing to early medieval ecclesiastical objects. A paper in the Proceedings of the Society of Antiquaries of Scotland on the ancient bell of St Fillan records the same broad rite: the dipped person took stones from the pool, walked round cairns, and was tied in St Fillan’s Bed, with release by morning taken as a successful cure.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://journals.socantscot.org/index.php/psas/article/download/5423/5394" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journals.socantscot.org">[Antiquaries of Scotland Journals]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journals.socantscot.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on socantscot.org.</span></span></span> Modern archaeological synthesis for Perth and Kinross notes that a bronze bell of St Fillan from Strathfillan is now in National Museums Scotland, giving the tradition a physical relic beyond the written accounts.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://scarf.scot/regional/pkarf/early-medieval/6-7-religion-and-ritual/6-7-5-other-religious-artefacts/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: scarf.scot">[Scarf]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">scarf.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on scarf.scot.</span></span></span>

The Holy Pool also has the classic folklore motif of lost power. Canmore records local information from 1962 that the pool was still remembered for bathing mentally ill people, but was said to have lost its power when a wild bull was thrown into it.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://canmore.org.uk/site/23864/strathfillan-holy-pool" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: canmore.org.uk">[Canmore]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">canmore.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">strathfillan holy pool</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">strathfillan holy pool</span></span></span> That ending is not just a colourful add-on. It is a way of explaining why an old sacred place no longer works, or why the community no longer practises what it remembers. Folklore often preserves abandoned rituals by giving their decline a story.

## How old and how well-attested is the tradition?

The safest answer is: St Fillan’s cult in Perthshire is medieval and well-attested, while the exact healing rites are best documented in early modern, eighteenth-, nineteenth- and twentieth-century records. The distinction matters.

There is strong medieval evidence for St Fillan’s institutional importance in Strathfillan. The Saints in Scottish Place-Names database records forms such as “capella Sancti Felani” in 1318 and “ecclesia de Strathfulane” in the reign of Robert I, while Historic Environment Scotland dates the priory foundation to 1317/18.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://saintsplaces.gla.ac.uk/place.php?id=1324644735" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: saintsplaces.gla.ac.uk">[saintsplaces.gla.ac.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">saintsplaces.gla.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">St Fillan&#x27;s Priory, Killin</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">St Fillan&#x27;s Priory, Killin</span></span></span> The saint’s relic-cult was also substantial. Joseph Anderson’s 1889 paper concerns the Quigrich or crozier and other relics of St Fillan in the possession of hereditary keepers, the Dewars of Glendochart, in 1549–50.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://journals.socantscot.org/index.php/psas/article/view/6317" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journals.socantscot.org">[Antiquaries of Scotland Journals]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journals.socantscot.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on socantscot.org.</span></span></span>

What is less certain is whether every detail of the healing stones and pool rituals can be projected back to St Fillan’s own lifetime. The popular date of 777 AD appears in modern heritage accounts for the giving of the stones, but it functions as tradition rather than as a securely documented event. Discover Highlands and Islands says the stones are “said to have been” given to the people of Killin by St Fillan in 777, which is careful wording and should remain careful in retelling.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://discoverhighlandsandislands.scot/en/story/heartlands-highland-perthshire-healing-stones" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: discoverhighlandsandislands.scot">[Discover Highlands and Islands]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">discoverhighlandsandislands.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Discover Highlands and Islands Heartlands of Highland Perthshire</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Discover Highlands and Islands Heartlands of Highland Perthshire</span></span></span>

For the stones, the record is uneven but still valuable. Trove’s archaeological note cites John Shearer in 1836 for the sacred ash tree, G. C. Cash in 1912 for the eight stones and their use on invalids, E. A. Elders in 1962 for the seat, tree and survival of the stones, and W. A. Gillies in 1938 for traditions including annual re-bedding.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.trove.scot/place/24219" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: trove.scot">[Trove Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">trove.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">landSt Fillan&#x27;s Stones &#124; PlaceAlso known as St Fillan&#x27;s Ash Tree; ling Parish: Killin Former region: Location accurate to the nearest 10...</span></span></span> That is a good antiquarian and local-history chain, but it is not the same as continuous written proof from the early medieval period.

For the pool, the written evidence is stronger for ritual practice because the Old and New Statistical Accounts describe it directly. Yet those sources are also shaped by Protestant disapproval, outsider curiosity and older medical assumptions. Their value lies in recording what was remembered and, in some cases, still being done or recently done. Their weakness is that they use loaded categories and may compress varied local practice into a single strange-sounding custom.

## Why the stones and water mattered to Perthshire identity

St Fillan’s healing traditions matter because they turn west Perthshire into a map of sacred treatment. Illness is not imagined as something dealt with only by a healer in a room. It is moved through a landscape: to a mill, a stone, a pool, a chapel, a saint’s bed, a bell, a riverbank cairn.

That makes the tradition especially Perthshire in texture. The Falls of Dochart give the Killin stones a dramatic water setting; the River Fillan gives the Holy Pool its force; the priory at Kirkton gives the landscape a medieval religious anchor; the Dewars of Glendochart connect relics to hereditary guardianship; and the survival of the stones at the Old Mill connects antiquarian folklore with living community heritage. The tradition is not just “a saint cured people”. It is a network of local objects and places that made suffering publicly legible.

There is also a social edge. The Holy Pool rites show how communities once handled people in mental crisis through ritual, restraint and ordeal. That deserves neither mockery nor romanticising. Families may have acted out of desperation and belief; the treatment itself could be frightening and harmful. A responsible folklore page can hold both truths: the rite belongs to Perthshire’s heritage, and it also reveals the risks of turning illness into a test of supernatural favour.

The Killin stones offer a different kind of continuity. Their annual re-bedding, public display and continued handling show how a healing tradition can be softened into heritage without disappearing. Instead of binding sufferers overnight, the modern emphasis falls on story, touch, craft, local memory and community ownership. The Old Mill’s own wording that “heritage is not static” captures this shift: the stones are preserved not only as relics of the past, but as objects through which Killin continues to tell a story about itself.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://killincdt.co.uk/project-oldmill" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: killincdt.co.uk">[killincdt.co.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">killincdt.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">project oldmill</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">project oldmill</span></span></span>

## How local heritage preserves the cult today

Today, St Fillan’s healing traditions are preserved through a mixture of museum-style interpretation, community stewardship, walking culture and digital folklore collecting. The Old Mill in Killin is central because it keeps the stones close to their traditional place rather than removing them entirely into a national museum context. Historic records place them in the mill in the twentieth century; current community interpretation still identifies the Old Mill as their home.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.trove.scot/place/24219" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: trove.scot">[Trove Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">trove.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">landSt Fillan&#x27;s Stones &#124; PlaceAlso known as St Fillan&#x27;s Ash Tree; ling Parish: Killin Former region: Location accurate to the nearest 10...</span></span></span>

Tobar an Dualchais, the national sound archive for Gaelic and Scots oral recordings, also helps preserve the story as spoken tradition. Its record for “The healing stones of St Fillan” describes the eight stones at the mill, notes the Breadalbane Folklore Centre context, and preserves oral discussion of the tradition rather than reducing it to a tourist panel.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.tobarandualchais.co.uk/track/18060?l=en" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: tobarandualchais.co.uk">[Tobar an Dualchais]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">tobarandualchais.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on tobarandualchais.co.uk.</span></span></span> This matters because saint-lore often lives in small variations: which stone cured what, when the stones were moved, who was allowed to handle them, and how seriously people took the cure.

The Holy Pool is preserved differently. It is not a comfortable visitor attraction in the same way as the stones. Canmore’s record treats it as a place of archaeological and traditional interest: a natural pool with no artificial works visible, known through map evidence, antiquarian references and local memory.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://canmore.org.uk/site/23864/strathfillan-holy-pool" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: canmore.org.uk">[Canmore]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">canmore.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">strathfillan holy pool</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">strathfillan holy pool</span></span></span> The Saints in Scottish Place-Names database gives it a place within the wider network of St Fillan names, connecting the pool to the priory, the former parish and the valley.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://saintsplaces.gla.ac.uk/place.php?id=1324644735" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: saintsplaces.gla.ac.uk">[saintsplaces.gla.ac.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">saintsplaces.gla.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">St Fillan&#x27;s Priory, Killin</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">St Fillan&#x27;s Priory, Killin</span></span></span>

The national relics complete the picture. The bell and crozier traditions show that St Fillan’s cult was not merely local superstition but part of a wider medieval culture of saintly objects, hereditary keepers and sacred authority. The Quigrich or crozier of St Fillan and the bronze bell now sit within museum and scholarly contexts, while Killin’s stones remain embedded in community place.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://journals.socantscot.org/index.php/psas/article/view/6317" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journals.socantscot.org">[Antiquaries of Scotland Journals]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journals.socantscot.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on socantscot.org.</span></span></span>

That split is useful for modern retelling. The national museum objects show the prestige of the cult; the Old Mill stones show its local intimacy; the Holy Pool shows its harder, more troubling medical folklore; and the Perthshire landscape holds them together. St Fillan’s stones and holy waters are therefore not just curiosities. They are one of the clearest examples in historic Perthshire of how saint-lore, illness, ritual objects and remembered place became inseparable.
