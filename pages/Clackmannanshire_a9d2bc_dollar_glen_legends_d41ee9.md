## Introduction

Dollar Glen feels haunted because its stories are unusually concentrated in the landscape itself. Above the town of Dollar in [Clackmannanshire]({{ 'where-do-clackmannanshires-legends-begin/' | relative_url }}), Castle Campbell stands between the Burn of Care and the Burn of Sorrow, below Gloom Hill and near old routes through the Ochils. Local tradition turns these names into a sorrowful castle legend: a banished maiden, a well she was allowed to visit, and a fairy hill where a piper loses a hundred years in a single enchanted night. Historic Environment Scotland confirms the castle’s earlier name as Castle Glume or Gloom and its dramatic position between steep ravines, while local and antiquarian sources preserve the maiden, well and fairy-castle tales that make this one of the richest story landscapes in the Wee County.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.historicenvironment.scot/visit/all/castle-campbell/history-and-stories/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: historicenvironment.scot">[Historic Environment Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">historicenvironment.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on historicenvironment.scot.</span></span></span>

## Why the glen gathers so many legends

Dollar Glen is not a wide, open valley where folklore has to work hard to create atmosphere. It is already theatrical. The National Trust for Scotland describes it as a place of deep gorges, rushing burns, woodland walks, waterfalls and Castle Campbell in the shadow of the Ochil Hills; the same page notes that the Burns of Care and Sorrow are among its defining features.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nts.org.uk/visit/places/dollar-glen" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nts.org.uk">[National Trust for Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nts.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Trust for Scotland Dollar Glen &#124; National Trust for Scotland</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Trust for Scotland Dollar Glen &#124; National Trust for Scotland</span></span></span>

That physical setting helps explain why the legends feel so rooted. The castle is set on a narrow ridge, with ravines falling away on either side and the Burn of Care and Burn of Sorrow running below. Historic Environment Scotland dates the stone fortress to the early 1400s, records that it was known as Castle Glume before it became Castle Campbell, and notes that the name was changed in 1489 after the Campbells acquired it as a Lowland seat of power.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.historicenvironment.scot/visit/all/castle-campbell/history-and-stories/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: historicenvironment.scot">[Historic Environment Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">historicenvironment.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on historicenvironment.scot.</span></span></span>

The important point for folklore is that the place-names did not stay as dry map labels. “Gloom”, “Care”, “Sorrow” and “Dollar” invited explanation. By the eighteenth century, Francis Grose was already describing the castle’s “dreary and solemn situation” in romance-like terms, linking the Castle of Gloom with the parish of Dolor, the Glen of Care and the Burn of Sorrow. Historic Environment Scotland’s designation record quotes this antiquarian passage and also records how picturesque walks were later developed through the glen, shaping how visitors encountered its named scenes.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://portal.historicenvironment.scot/apex/f?p=1505%3A300%3A%3A%3A%3A%3AVIEWTYPE%2CVIEWREF%3Adesignation%2CGDL00089" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: portal.historicenvironment.scot">[Historic Environment Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">portal.historicenvironment.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Historic Environment ScotlandCASTLE CAMPBELL (GDL00089)...</span></span></span>

## Castle Gloom and the sad place-names

The best-known legend says that a young royal woman was imprisoned at Castle Gloom because she fell in love with a man beneath her rank. In grief, she is said to have named the surrounding landscape after her misery: the castle became Gloom, the burns became Care and Sorrow, and the melancholy mood spread down through the glen towards Dollar. Clackmannanshire’s local history site gives this as the core Castle Gloom tradition and connects it directly with the Maiden’s Well story.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://clackmannanshire.scot/index.php/history/myths-and-legends/castle-gloom" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: clackmannanshire.scot">[clackmannanshire.scot]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">clackmannanshire.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Castle Gloom</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Castle Gloom</span></span></span>

This is a good example of how folklore explains names by turning them into a human drama. The official historical record is more cautious. Historic Environment Scotland says the place was originally Castle Glume, not necessarily “Gloom” in the modern emotional sense, and the Map of Stories retelling notes a possible derivation from a word meaning a chasm or gap, which would fit the castle’s deep ravine setting.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.historicenvironment.scot/visit/all/castle-campbell/history-and-stories/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: historicenvironment.scot">[Historic Environment Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">historicenvironment.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on historicenvironment.scot.</span></span></span>

The legend still matters even if it is not a literal origin story. It shows how local imagination read the landscape. A castle hemmed in by steep wooded gullies, with water sounding below and hills rising behind, became the perfect stage for a story about confinement, sorrow and longing. The later Campbell name belongs to documented political history; Castle Gloom belongs to the emotional geography of Dollar Glen.

There is also a useful distinction between different layers of tradition. The castle itself is strongly documented: it was a Campbell stronghold, associated with major figures including John Knox and Mary Queen of Scots, and was attacked during seventeenth-century conflict. The imprisoned maiden, by contrast, is a local explanatory legend: memorable, place-specific and widely retold, but not supported as a recoverable historical event.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.historicenvironment.scot/visit/all/castle-campbell/history-and-stories/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: historicenvironment.scot">[Historic Environment Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">historicenvironment.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on historicenvironment.scot.</span></span></span>

## Maiden’s Well and the glen route

The maiden’s story does not end at the castle. The route north from Castle Campbell through the Glen of Care and into Glen Quey is part of the folklore. Clackmannanshire’s local account says Cadger’s Gate, an old road used for driving livestock, followed the burn towards Glendevon; about a mile and a half from the castle, in a narrow pass between Hillfoot Hill and Whitewisp, lies a small deep spring known as Maiden’s Well. The imprisoned maiden was supposedly allowed to walk there and drink from it.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://clackmannanshire.scot/index.php/history/myths-and-legends/castle-gloom" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: clackmannanshire.scot">[clackmannanshire.scot]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">clackmannanshire.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Castle Gloom</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Castle Gloom</span></span></span>

This gives the legend a route, not just a site. Readers can imagine the movement from the castle’s confinement into the open hills, then to the spring at the edge of another glen. That matters because many Scottish fairy and well traditions are attached to thresholds: passes, burns, hillocks, springs and old roads. In Dollar Glen, the well sits between the castle legend and the fairy-hill tradition.

The older antiquarian trail is especially important here. Sir John Rhys’s 1901 collection *Celtic Folklore: Welsh and Manx* includes a Dollar-area tale passed to him by the Rev. Andrew Clark, who had heard it from the late sexton of Dollar. Clark described the old route between Dollar and Glendevon, the round hill called Maiden Castle, and, nearby, a spring bordered with flat stones in a tidy grassy place called Maiden’s Well.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/files/55025/55025-0.txt" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Project Gutenbergwww.gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Project Gutenbergwww.gutenberg.org</span></span></span>

That chain of transmission is not the same as a medieval record, but it is stronger than a vague modern ghost story. Rhys gives a named collector, a named intermediary, a local informant and a clear place description. It shows that the fairy-hill and well traditions were circulating in local memory by the late nineteenth century at the latest, and probably earlier through family telling.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/files/55025/55025-0.txt" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Project Gutenbergwww.gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Project Gutenbergwww.gutenberg.org</span></span></span>

## Fairy hills on the Ochil edge

The fairy element centres on Maiden Castle, a rounded hill near the pass between Glen Quey and the Glen of Care. Rhys’s source described it as a neat, low, round hill whose short grass and green ferns made it stand out; the path only became visible when the traveller came close. That detail is folklorically powerful: the hill appears to block the way, then gives passage, making it a natural place for stories about hidden entrances and otherworldly dwellings.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/files/55025/55025-0.txt" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Project Gutenbergwww.gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Project Gutenbergwww.gutenberg.org</span></span></span>

The key tale is the Lost Piper. A piper travelling from Glendevon to Dollar in the grey of evening passes Maiden Castle and at first sees only the hillside. Then he hears music, turns back, and sees a great castle blazing with lights. He is drawn inside, made to play for the dancers, and later released. When he returns home, no familiar faces remain: what seemed like a short visit to the fairy castle has lasted a hundred years.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/files/55025/55025-0.txt" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Project Gutenbergwww.gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Project Gutenbergwww.gutenberg.org</span></span></span>

This is one of the strongest Dollar Glen traditions because it combines a precise local route with a classic fairy-time motif. The piper is not wandering in an abstract fairyland. He is moving between Glendevon and Dollar, across named burns and past a named hill. The story turns the Ochil edge into a border between ordinary travel and enchanted time.

Modern retellings keep that structure. Clackmannanshire’s local version places the piper on the track between Glendevon and Dollar, hearing music near Maiden’s Well, seeing a bright castle where none should be, and discovering on his return that a century has passed. Discover Britain similarly presents Maiden Castle as a round hillock near the pass, with no ordinary castle known there but a fairy castle in local folklore.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://clackmannanshire.scot/index.php/history/myths-and-legends/castle-gloom" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: clackmannanshire.scot">[clackmannanshire.scot]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">clackmannanshire.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Castle Gloom</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Castle Gloom</span></span></span>

## How old and well-attested are these stories?

The Dollar Glen legends are best described as well-attested local folklore rather than provable ancient survivals. The castle’s historical framework is firm: Castle Campbell, formerly Castle Glume or Gloom, is documented from the fifteenth century, and Historic Environment Scotland records its setting, owners, name change and later history.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.historicenvironment.scot/visit/all/castle-campbell/history-and-stories/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: historicenvironment.scot">[Historic Environment Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">historicenvironment.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on historicenvironment.scot.</span></span></span>

The folklore is layered differently. The Lost Piper has a valuable antiquarian witness in Rhys’s 1901 text, with a named oral chain leading back through the Dollar sexton and his father. That does not prove the tale’s age beyond the nineteenth century, but it does show that it was not invented recently for tourism.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/files/55025/55025-0.txt" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Project Gutenbergwww.gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Project Gutenbergwww.gutenberg.org</span></span></span>

The imprisoned maiden and Maiden’s Well story is widely repeated in local and visitor-facing accounts, and Historic Environment Scotland itself acknowledges the tradition of a princess banished to Castle Gloom for loving below her station. The organisation is careful, however, to frame this as local tradition and says the origin of the Maiden Tree name is lost to history.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.historicenvironment.scot/visit/all/castle-campbell/history-and-stories/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: historicenvironment.scot">[Historic Environment Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">historicenvironment.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on historicenvironment.scot.</span></span></span>

That caution is important. The sadness of Castle Gloom may be a story created to explain evocative names, while “Glume” may originally have referred to the ravine-like setting rather than melancholy. The fairy hill may preserve an older class of belief about hollow hills, hidden halls and dangerous fairy hospitality, but the surviving evidence reaches us through antiquarian and later retellings, not through early medieval documentation.

## Why Dollar Glen matters to Clackmannanshire folklore

Dollar Glen matters because it gives Clackmannanshire a compact mythic landscape rather than a single isolated tale. Castle, burns, well, road, pass and hill all work together. A visitor can move through the story-world: up from Dollar, through the wooded ravines, past Castle Campbell, towards Maiden’s Well and the hill of Maiden Castle.

It also shows how the Wee County’s folklore often attaches to named physical features. The [Stone of Mannan]({{ 'mannan-stone/' | relative_url }}) anchors identity at Clackmannan; the Alloa witch records anchor belief in legal history; Dollar Glen anchors legend in topography. In this case the place itself does much of the storytelling. The names Care, Sorrow and Gloom make the landscape feel pre-haunted before a guide or book says anything at all.

The glen’s modern care also shapes its retelling. Dollar Glen is managed by the National Trust for Scotland, while Castle Campbell is managed by Historic Environment Scotland; the Trust highlights the woodland walks, waterfalls, rare habitats and SSSI status, while HES presents the castle’s architecture, Campbell history and local tradition.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nts.org.uk/visit/places/dollar-glen" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nts.org.uk">[National Trust for Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nts.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Trust for Scotland Dollar Glen &#124; National Trust for Scotland</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Trust for Scotland Dollar Glen &#124; National Trust for Scotland</span></span></span>

That combination keeps the folklore alive in a grounded way. Dollar Glen does not need exaggerated paranormal claims to be memorable. Its power comes from the meeting of credible place-history and old-style story: a real castle with a dark former name, real burns with sorrowful names, a real route into the hills, and a fairy tale about what might happen when music is heard from a hill where no castle stands.
