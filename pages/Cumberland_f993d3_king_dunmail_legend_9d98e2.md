## Introduction

King Dunmail is the fallen king whom local legend places beneath the great cairn at Dunmail Raise, the mountain pass between Grasmere and Thirlmere. The story says he was the last ruler of Cumbria or [Cumberland]({{ 'where-cumberland-turns-landscape-into/' | relative_url }}), defeated by King Edmund of England and Malcolm of Scotland, while his crown was hidden in Grisedale Tarn until the day his kingdom should need him again. The tale matters because it turns a visible roadside heap of stones into a memory-place for lost northern power: a border marker, a possible prehistoric cairn, a legendary grave and a symbol of the old Cumbrian kingdom all at once. Historic evidence does not prove that a king is buried there, but the legend is strongly attached to the pass, appears in antiquarian and literary retellings, and still shapes how many people read this stretch of Cumberland landscape.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://historicengland.org.uk/listing/the-list/list-entry/1011353" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: historicengland.org.uk">[historicengland.org.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">historicengland.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Historic EnglandRound cairn on Dunmail RaiseAccording to local tradition the cairn was built either as a tumulus for Dunmail, last king o...</span></span></span>

## The pass between Cumberland and Westmorland

Dunmail Raise is one of those places where folklore makes immediate sense on the ground. It sits on the route now followed by the A591, between Grasmere to the south and Thirlmere to the north, at a natural crossing through the Lake District fells. The pass has long worked as a threshold: water falling north drains towards the Solway side, while water falling south runs towards the Morecambe Bay side, and the road itself links the southern and northern Lake District by a comparatively low route through high country.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dunmail_Raise" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Dunmail Raise</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Dunmail Raise</span></span></span>

For historic-county folklore, its boundary role is crucial. Dunmail Raise lay on the old line between Cumberland and Westmorland, making it a natural place for a story about the edge of a kingdom. The Cumbria County History Trust, summarising the longer history of the area, notes Dunmail Raise as the boundary point between Cumberland and Westmorland in the later medieval county pattern.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cumbriacountyhistory.org.uk/thousand-years-cumbria-background" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cumbriacountyhistory.org.uk">[Cumbria County History Trust]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cumbriacountyhistory.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cumbriacountyhistory.org.uk.</span></span></span>

The cairn itself now looks oddly modern because the road divides around it. Historic England lists the site as the “Round cairn on Dunmail Raise” and records it as a scheduled monument. Its official description treats it as a round cairn but also notes the local tradition that it was either Dunmail’s tumulus or a marker for the battlefield of Dunmail and Edmund. That pairing is important: archaeology protects the mound as an ancient monument, while folklore explains why this particular mound has been remembered as a royal grave.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://historicengland.org.uk/listing/the-list/list-entry/1011353" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: historicengland.org.uk">[Historic England]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">historicengland.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Historic EnglandRound cairn on Dunmail RaiseAccording to local tradition the cairn was built either as a tumulus for Dunmail, last king o...</span></span></span>

There is also a useful caution here. A cairn can be prehistoric, altered by later roadworks, used as a boundary marker, and wrapped in medievalising legend without those layers cancelling one another out. A Pastscape-derived summary describes the feature as a Bronze Age round cairn on a stone-built platform, disturbed by nineteenth-century road works and later restored to something like its original form and position. That does not prove the Dunmail story, but it helps explain why an old stone monument in a boundary pass could attract a tale of battle and burial.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.megalithic.co.uk/article.php?sid=52995" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: megalithic.co.uk">[Megalithic Portal]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">megalithic.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on megalithic.co.uk.</span></span></span>

## The last king story and the hidden crown

The core legend is simple and memorable. Dunmail, often called the last king of Cumberland or Cumbria, is said to have been defeated in the pass by King Edmund and Malcolm, king of the Scots. After the battle, stones were heaped over his body, forming the cairn at Dunmail Raise. Some versions add a harsh royal aftermath: Dunmail’s sons are blinded, removing the possibility of a continuing dynasty.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://thewildpeak.wordpress.com/tag/king-malcolm/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: thewildpeak.wordpress.com">[The Wild Peak]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">thewildpeak.wordpress.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">king malcolm</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">king malcolm</span></span></span>

The best-known supernatural turn comes with the crown. In later retellings, Dunmail’s followers carry the crown away from the battlefield and cast it into Grisedale Tarn, high among the fells, so that no conqueror can claim it. The crown is not merely treasure; it stands for legitimate Cumbrian kingship. In some versions, ghostly warriors return each year, retrieve the crown from the tarn, bring it to the cairn and strike the stones, only to hear the sleeping king answer that the time has not yet come.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://esmeraldamac.wordpress.com/2011/02/15/dunmail-last-king-of-cumbria-945ce/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: esmeraldamac.wordpress.com">[Esmeralda&#x27;s Cumbrian History &amp; Folklore]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">esmeraldamac.wordpress.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">dunmail last king of cumbria 945ce</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">dunmail last king of cumbria 945ce</span></span></span>

That motif links Dunmail to a wider family of “king under the hill” stories, where a defeated or hidden ruler waits for a future national need. What makes the Cumberland version distinctive is its geography. The sleeping king is not placed in an abstract fairy hill but in a cairn on a real pass; the crown is not hidden in a vague otherworld but in a named tarn reachable by walkers; and the lost kingdom is not a fantasy realm but a memory of the Brittonic Cumbrian north.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.repository.cam.ac.uk/bitstreams/66af080f-349a-4463-ae0e-c628ef84203c/download" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: repository.cam.ac.uk">[Cambridge Repository]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">repository.cam.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Cambridge Repository The emergence of the Cumbrian kingdom</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cambridge Repository The emergence of the Cumbrian kingdom</span></span></span>

The story is often told today as if it were a neat historical episode from 945. The actual documentary record is much thinner. The Anglo-Saxon Chronicle says that in 945 King Edmund overran or ravaged Cumberland and granted it to Malcolm, king of the Scots, on condition that Malcolm became his ally by sea and land. That source gives the political shock behind the legend, but it does not describe Dunmail dying at the cairn, his crown being hidden, or his warriors returning from the dead.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://avalon.law.yale.edu/medieval/ang10.asp" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: avalon.law.yale.edu">[Avalon Project]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">avalon.law.yale.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on yale.edu.</span></span></span>

## Was there a real Dunmail?

The name Dunmail is usually connected with Dyfnwal, a name borne by rulers of Strathclyde, the Brittonic kingdom whose influence extended into what later writers called Cumbria. Modern scholarship on medieval Cumbria is cautious: the “Cumbrian” terminology appears to reflect the southward expansion and political transformation of Strathclyde rather than a tidy modern county-sized kingdom. Fiona Edmonds’ work on medieval Cumbria argues that terms such as “land of the Cumbrians” were prompted by the expansion of the Strathclyde kingdom beyond Clydesdale and were tied to a people or ruling dynasty.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.repository.cam.ac.uk/bitstreams/66af080f-349a-4463-ae0e-c628ef84203c/download" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: repository.cam.ac.uk">[Cambridge Repository]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">repository.cam.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Cambridge Repository The emergence of the Cumbrian kingdom</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cambridge Repository The emergence of the Cumbrian kingdom</span></span></span>

This helps explain why Dunmail is such a powerful figure in Cumberland folklore. He appears at the point where local legend, early medieval politics and later county identity overlap. The “lost kingdom” is not invented from nothing: there really was a northern Brittonic political world, and tenth-century sources really do record Edmund’s violent intervention in Cumberland. But the named battlefield, the burial beneath the cairn and the enchanted crown belong to tradition rather than secure chronicle history.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://avalon.law.yale.edu/medieval/ang10.asp" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: avalon.law.yale.edu">[Avalon Project]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">avalon.law.yale.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on yale.edu.</span></span></span>

A targeted study of Strathclyde’s southward expansion specifically notes Dunmail Raise as the most southerly proposed boundary point associated with Dyfnwal, while also warning that other suggested boundary lines rely on later and problematic texts. That is a good model for reading the legend: Dunmail Raise may preserve a real memory of political limits, but the exact story told around the cairn has been shaped by centuries of retelling.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.repository.cam.ac.uk/bitstreams/b580c610-873c-4748-a20d-d8ef4bb5d081/download" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: repository.cam.ac.uk">[Cambridge Repository]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">repository.cam.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cam.ac.uk.</span></span></span>

There is even uncertainty in the date attached to the cairn tradition. Historic England’s listing records the local tradition as linking the cairn with Dunmail and Edmund in 934, while the wider historical frame most often cited for Edmund’s action in Cumberland is 945. That mismatch is not a minor clerical nuisance; it is a reminder that local legend and chronicle history do not line up cleanly.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://historicengland.org.uk/listing/the-list/list-entry/1011353" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: historicengland.org.uk">[Historic England]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">historicengland.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Historic EnglandRound cairn on Dunmail RaiseAccording to local tradition the cairn was built either as a tumulus for Dunmail, last king o...</span></span></span>

## Who preserved the legend?

The Dunmail story was kept alive by a mixture of local tradition, antiquarian writing, guidebooks and Romantic literature. Eighteenth-century Lake District tourism was especially important because travellers wanted roads, views, ruins and stories to fit together. A dramatic cairn on a mountain pass, supposedly marking the grave of a lost king, was exactly the kind of feature that guidebook writers could use to make landscape feel historically charged. Thomas West’s influential *Guide to the Lakes* is one of the key antiquarian-tourist contexts in which Dunmail Raise was circulated to readers exploring Cumberland, Westmorland and Lancashire.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/details/guidetolakesincu00west" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on archive.org.</span></span></span>

The legend also entered poetry. In *The Waggoner*, William Wordsworth turns the cairn into a brief but forceful emblem of fallen rule: the travellers reach the pile of stones “heaped over brave King Dunmail’s bones”, and Dunmail is remembered as the “last king of rocky Cumberland”. Wordsworth’s lines did not invent the tradition, but they helped fix its mood for later readers: stony, dark, desolate and haunted by old power.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://teaching.shu.ac.uk/ds/sle/altered/selections/Romantics/waggoner.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: teaching.shu.ac.uk">[teaching.shu.ac.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">teaching.shu.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on shu.ac.uk.</span></span></span>

Antiquarian and literary preservation can make a legend feel older than the surviving evidence allows. The Dunmail tale probably contains older local associations, especially around the cairn, boundary and name, but many of the details familiar today come through written retellings. That does not make the story worthless as folklore. It means its value lies partly in showing how Cumberland’s past was interpreted by travellers, poets and local historians as well as by oral tradition.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.lakesguides.co.uk/html/lgaz/lk16634.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: lakesguides.co.uk">[lakesguides.co.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">lakesguides.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Dunmail Raise Stones</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Dunmail Raise Stones</span></span></span>

Modern retellings often emphasise the crown and the sleeping king because those details give the legend emotional lift. They turn a defeat into an unfinished story: Cumberland’s lost kingship is not gone for ever, only hidden below the stones and under the tarn-water. That is why the tale still works for visitors who may know little about Strathclyde, Edmund or tenth-century politics. The landscape gives them the evidence they can see, while the legend supplies the meaning.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://esmeraldamac.wordpress.com/2011/02/15/dunmail-last-king-of-cumbria-945ce/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: esmeraldamac.wordpress.com">[Esmeralda&#x27;s Cumbrian History &amp; Folklore]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">esmeraldamac.wordpress.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">dunmail last king of cumbria 945ce</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">dunmail last king of cumbria 945ce</span></span></span>

## Why border legends cling to roads and cairns

Dunmail Raise is a classic folklore site because it gathers several kinds of memory in one place. A cairn invites burial stories. A pass invites battle stories. A county boundary invites stories about rival powers. A road makes the site visible to generation after generation of travellers. In this case, all four come together, so the legend feels unusually well anchored even where the historical proof is uncertain.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://historicengland.org.uk/listing/the-list/list-entry/1011353" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: historicengland.org.uk">[Historic England]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">historicengland.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Historic EnglandRound cairn on Dunmail RaiseAccording to local tradition the cairn was built either as a tumulus for Dunmail, last king o...</span></span></span>

The cairn’s present setting strengthens rather than weakens the effect. Many ancient monuments sit apart from daily life; Dunmail Raise sits in the middle of a road corridor. Thousands pass it without stopping, yet its very awkwardness makes it memorable. It is not a polished visitor attraction with a single official story. It is a heap of stones that appears to have survived practical alteration, administrative boundary changes and modern traffic while still carrying a royal legend.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.megalithic.co.uk/article.php?sid=52995" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: megalithic.co.uk">[Megalithic Portal]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">megalithic.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on megalithic.co.uk.</span></span></span>

For Cumberland identity, the tale is especially potent because it tells a story of loss at the southern threshold of the historic county. Dunmail is not merely a warrior killed in battle; he is “the last king”, a figure through whom later generations could imagine a time before English, Scottish and county boundaries settled into their familiar forms. The legend gives the mountains a political memory: the fells become the last refuge of a northern kingdom rather than just scenery.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.repository.cam.ac.uk/bitstreams/66af080f-349a-4463-ae0e-c628ef84203c/download" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: repository.cam.ac.uk">[Cambridge Repository]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">repository.cam.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Cambridge Repository The emergence of the Cumbrian kingdom</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cambridge Repository The emergence of the Cumbrian kingdom</span></span></span>

It also sits naturally beside other Cumberland and Lake District traditions in which old stones, roads and high places are explained by dramatic human or supernatural events. [Long Meg]({{ 'long-meg/' | relative_url }}) and Her Daughters turns a prehistoric stone circle into a petrified coven; Dunmail Raise turns a cairn into a royal grave; other fell and tarn legends use the same imaginative habit, making landscape features into story-markers. The Dunmail legend belongs to that local way of reading the land, but with a sharper historical edge because it gestures towards the real early medieval struggle for the Cumbrian north.

## What the legend means today

Today, “King Dunmail” is best understood neither as plain history nor as pure invention. The safest reading is layered. At the base is a real pass and a protected cairn, probably ancient and certainly long visible in the landscape. Around it lies a remembered boundary between Cumberland and Westmorland, and possibly an echo of older territorial limits. Behind the story stands the historical fact that Edmund’s forces violently intervened in Cumberland in 945. Above that sits the legend: the slain king, the blinded heirs, the hidden crown, the yearly return and the voice from within the stones.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://historicengland.org.uk/listing/the-list/list-entry/1011353" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: historicengland.org.uk">[historicengland.org.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">historicengland.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Historic EnglandRound cairn on Dunmail RaiseAccording to local tradition the cairn was built either as a tumulus for Dunmail, last king o...</span></span></span>

That layered reading preserves the tale’s power without pretending that every detail is proven. A visitor looking at the cairn should not be told, as fact, that Dunmail’s body lies beneath it. Nor should the story be dismissed as a tourist fancy. Its endurance shows how Cumberland has remembered itself as border country: a place where kingdoms met, where roads crossed hard country, and where old defeats could be turned into a promise that something hidden might one day return.

The most memorable part of the legend is not the battle itself but the unresolved ending. Dunmail does not simply die; his crown is kept from his enemies. His warriors do not simply vanish; they return. The cairn does not simply mark a grave; it becomes a listening place, waiting for the answer that has not yet changed. That is why Dunmail Raise remains one of Cumberland’s most resonant legendary sites: a roadside cairn that makes the lost kingdom feel close enough to pass at speed, but old enough to remain just out of reach.
