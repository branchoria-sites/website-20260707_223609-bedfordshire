## Introduction

Dunbartonshire’s folklore is rooted in a landscape where water, rock and borderland history meet: [Dumbarton Rock]({{ 'dumbarton-rock/' | relative_url }}) above the Clyde, Loch Lomond and its islands, the Kilpatrick Hills, Luss, Old Kilpatrick, Clydebank, Kirkintilloch and the detached eastern part of the old county around Cumbernauld. The strongest traditions are not a single famous “county myth”, but a cluster of stories: Arthurian and Merlin associations at Dumbarton Rock, saintly legends and holy wells at Luss and Old Kilpatrick, witch-trial memory in Dumbarton, prehistoric rock art at Cochno, and modern fairy retellings around Loch Lomond.



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/Dunbartonshire_abd4f2-overview.webp" | relative_url }}" alt="Overview image for Where Dunbartonshire&#x27;s Legends Gather" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

For this project, Dunbartonshire is best understood as the historic county rather than the modern council areas. The old county was split into a main western part along the Clyde, Loch Lomond and the Lennox, plus a detached eastern part containing Kirkintilloch and Cumbernauld; today its territory is divided between West Dunbartonshire, East Dunbartonshire, Argyll and Bute, and North Lanarkshire.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://gazetteer.org.uk/search?place=Dunbartonshire&amp;type=em" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gazetteer.org.uk">[gazetteer.org.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gazetteer.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on gazetteer.org.uk.</span></span></span> That matters because the folklore does not follow present-day council lines: Luss is now in Argyll and Bute, Cumbernauld is now in North Lanarkshire, and Dumbarton itself anchors West Dunbartonshire, but all belong to the historic county frame.

## Why Dunbartonshire’s folklore starts with Dumbarton Rock

Dumbarton Rock is the county’s great mythic landmark. Historic Environment Scotland describes Dumbarton Castle as guarding the meeting point of the River Leven and the River Clyde, with a recorded history stretching back around 1,500 years; its earlier name, Alt Clut, means “Rock of the Clyde”, while the later Gaelic name Dun Breatann means “Fortress of the Britons”, from which Dumbarton takes its name.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.historicenvironment.scot/visit/all/dumbarton-castle/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: historicenvironment.scot">[Historic Environment Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">historicenvironment.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on historicenvironment.scot.</span></span></span> That combination of dramatic geology, ancient kingship and strategic river control gives the Rock a quality that easily invites legend.

The site’s history is dramatic enough without embellishment. Historic Environment Scotland records that Viking kings Olaf and Ivar of Dublin attacked in 870, carrying off treasure and captives in 200 longships after a four-month siege.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.historicenvironment.scot/visit/all/dumbarton-castle/history-and-stories/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: historicenvironment.scot">[Historic Environment Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">historicenvironment.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on historicenvironment.scot.</span></span></span> In folklore terms, this matters because Dumbarton Rock is not just a picturesque castle: it is remembered as a lost royal centre, a place where the Brittonic kingdom of Strathclyde stood before medieval Scotland took its later shape. The fall of such a place naturally becomes the kind of historical wound around which heroic and supernatural stories gather.

The Rock also sits at the edge of Arthurian tradition. Local and popular accounts connect Dumbarton with Rhydderch Hael, the sixth-century ruler of Strathclyde, and with stories of Merlin or Lailoken at the king’s court. A specialist discussion of the Merlin tradition notes that Lailoken, Kentigern and Geoffrey of Monmouth’s Merlin were all depicted as contemporaries of Rhydderch Hael, whose chief citadel lay at Dumbarton Rock.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://senchus.wordpress.com/2018/08/19/the-merlin-trail/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: senchus.wordpress.com">[Senchus]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">senchus.wordpress.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Senchus The Merlin Trail</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Senchus The Merlin Trail</span></span></span> The Dumbarton Castle Society presents the Arthurian link more cautiously, explaining that Dumbarton Rock is mentioned in connection with Arthur’s northern campaigns and that Merlin is said to have stayed at the court of Rhydderch Hael, while also acknowledging that the Arthur association is uncertain.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.dumbartoncastle.co.uk/king-arthur" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: dumbartoncastle.co.uk">[Dumbarton Castle Soc]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">dumbartoncastle.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on dumbartoncastle.co.uk.</span></span></span>

That uncertainty is important. The Merlin material belongs to medieval literary and legendary tradition, not to a securely documented local event. The historical Rhydderch seems to have been real, and Dumbarton was a real royal centre; Merlin’s stay there is a story built from the borderland world of Welsh, Scottish and Latin tradition.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://jemne.org/issues/2/ha2rh.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: jemne.org">[Early Medieval Journal]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">jemne.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on jemne.org.</span></span></span> For a folklore reader, the value is not that the wizard “really” walked the Rock, but that Dumbarton became one of the places where northern British history and Arthurian imagination overlap.

## Saints, wells and Christian legend on the Loch Lomond side

Dunbartonshire’s saintly folklore is strongest around Luss and Loch Lomond. The Loch Lomond and The Trossachs National Park authority describes Luss as a place associated with St Kessog, an Irish missionary said to have arrived around 1,500 years ago; legend says he was martyred, his body was embalmed with sweet herbs, and the herbs that grew around his grave gave Luss its name.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.lochlomond-trossachs.org/discover-the-park/towns-villages/luss/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: lochlomond-trossachs.org">[Loch Lomond National Park]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">lochlomond-trossachs.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Loch Lomond National Park Luss</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Loch Lomond National Park Luss</span></span></span> The National Churches Trust similarly presents Luss Parish Church as dedicated to St Kessog, who is said to have preached there and died in 520, and notes that he founded a monastery on nearby Inchtavannach, the “island of the monks”.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nationalchurchestrust.org/church/luss-parish-church-luss" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nationalchurchestrust.org">[National Churches Trust]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nationalchurchestrust.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nationalchurchestrust.org.</span></span></span>

This is a good example of how early Christian memory becomes folklore. The exact historical details of Kessog’s life are difficult to prove, but the tradition gives Luss a sacred biography: missionary arrival, martyrdom, holy burial, place-name explanation and island monasticism. The present church is Victorian, built in 1875, but the churchyard contains much older Christian material, including early medieval and medieval monuments.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.trove.scot/place/319269" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: trove.scot">[Trove Scot]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">trove.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on trove.scot.</span></span></span> The result is a living visitor landscape in which a parish church, an old burial ground, the loch shore and a saint’s story all reinforce one another.

Old Kilpatrick offers a different kind of sacred-water tradition. West Dunbartonshire Council records St Patrick’s Well, also known as the Trees Well, and explains that Old Kilpatrick once had several wells, probably dating back to Roman times, which supplied the Roman infantry and cavalry and later served local domestic and industrial needs until the mid-19th century.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.west-dunbarton.gov.uk/leisure-parks-events/museums-and-galleries/collections/buildings/monuments-and-memorials/st-patricks-well" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: west-dunbarton.gov.uk">[West Dunbartonshire Council]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">west-dunbarton.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on west-dunbarton.gov.uk.</span></span></span> A local antiquarian account cited by The Northern Antiquarian says the well stood beside a church dedicated to St Patrick and that its water had been used “from time immemorial” by villagers before it was closed because it was no longer fit for use.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.thenorthernantiquarian.org/2024/02/10/st-patricks-well-kilpatrick/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: thenorthernantiquarian.org">[thenorthernantiquarian.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">thenorthernantiquarian.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">St. Patrick&#x27;s Well, Old Kilpatrick, Dumbartonshire</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">St. Patrick&#x27;s Well, Old Kilpatrick, Dumbartonshire</span></span></span>

Holy wells in Scotland often sit between utility, devotion and folk healing. The intangible cultural heritage record for Scottish holy wells stresses that their meaning lies not only in the physical spring, but in the knowledge and practices surrounding sacred water.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ichscotland.org/wiki/living-traditions-surrounding-holy-wells-historic-springs-and-sacred-waters-scotland" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ichscotland.org">[ichscotland.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ichscotland.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on ichscotland.org.</span></span></span> In Dunbartonshire, St Patrick’s Well shows how a practical water source could also carry saintly association, parish memory and local identity. Its folklore is quieter than a ghost story, but it is deeply typical of Scottish local tradition: a place where water, faith, habit and community memory overlap.

## Witches, justice and memory in Dumbarton

Witchcraft in Dunbartonshire should be handled carefully. These are not cosy Halloween tales about magical women; they are records of accusation, fear and punishment in early modern Scotland. The University of Edinburgh’s Survey of Scottish Witchcraft was created to document people accused of witchcraft in Scotland between 1563 and 1736, using secular and ecclesiastical records to examine who was accused, how cases were investigated and what happened to the accused.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://datashare.ed.ac.uk/handle/10283/45" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: datashare.ed.ac.uk">[datashare.ed.ac.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">datashare.ed.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on ed.ac.uk.</span></span></span> The UK Data Service summary notes that nearly 4,000 accused people were identified in Scotland for the period when witchcraft was a secular crime.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.thearda.com/data-archive/religion-resource-single-item?resourceInternalName=SN4667" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: thearda.com">[ARDA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">thearda.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">religion resource single item</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">religion resource single item</span></span></span>

Dumbarton appears directly in this record. The Survey’s entry for Janet Boyd states that she confessed to the provost and bailies of Dumbarton and refers to a second commission in her trial record.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://witches.hca.ed.ac.uk/trial/T/LA/508" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: witches.hca.ed.ac.uk">[witches.hca.ed.ac.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">witches.hca.ed.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on ed.ac.uk.</span></span></span> That small database entry is more valuable than a dramatic retelling because it anchors the subject in evidence: local magistrates, named people, formal procedure and the machinery of accusation.

Modern Dunbartonshire has begun to revisit this history as public memory rather than spooky entertainment. West Dunbartonshire Council’s 2023–24 Crime and Punishment exhibition at the Heritage Centre in Dumbarton Library included the 17th-century witchcraft trials alongside prisoners of war in Dumbarton Castle and Dumbarton Prison.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.west-dunbarton.gov.uk/leisure-parks-events/museums-and-galleries/exhibitions/crime-punishment-exhibition" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: west-dunbarton.gov.uk">[West Dunbartonshire Council]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">west-dunbarton.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on west-dunbarton.gov.uk.</span></span></span> More recent local reporting has also discussed efforts to honour women persecuted for witchcraft in West Dunbartonshire.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.glasgowlive.co.uk/news/persecuted-witches-west-dunbartonshire-honoured-32377725" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: glasgowlive.co.uk">[Glasgow Live]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">glasgowlive.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">persecuted witches west dunbartonshire honoured 32377725</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">persecuted witches west dunbartonshire honoured 32377725</span></span></span> The shift is significant: older folklore often preserved witch stories as warnings or curiosities, while present-day retellings increasingly emphasise injustice, gender, fear and remembrance.

The county’s witchcraft material also connects naturally to wider Scottish witch-trial history. Dunbartonshire was part of the Lowland legal and ecclesiastical world in which many Scottish prosecutions occurred, yet its local evidence is scattered across records rather than concentrated in one nationally famous case like North Berwick or Paisley. That makes it especially important not to inflate the record. The real story is not that Dunbartonshire was uniquely “witch-haunted”, but that ordinary burgh and parish life here participated in a wider Scottish culture of witch-belief, accusation and judicial violence.

## Fairy tradition: old belief, neighbouring lore and modern retelling

Dunbartonshire’s fairy material is less neatly documented than its saints, stones and witch records, but the county sits beside one of Scotland’s best-known fairy landscapes: the Loch Lomond and Trossachs world of fairy hills, wooded knowes and the Reverend Robert Kirk. The National Park authority describes the Doon Hill and Fairy Knowe walk near Aberfoyle as a route steeped in mystery, with an ancient pine said to be a doorway to the underground palace of the Fairy Queen, where Kirk’s spirit was trapped after he revealed fairy secrets in 1691.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.lochlomond-trossachs.org/things-to-do/walking/hillwalking/doon-hill-and-fairy-knowe/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: lochlomond-trossachs.org">[Loch Lomond National Park]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">lochlomond-trossachs.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Loch Lomond National Park Doon Hill and Fairy Knowe</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Loch Lomond National Park Doon Hill and Fairy Knowe</span></span></span>

Strictly speaking, Doon Hill is not the core of historic Dunbartonshire. Its relevance is interpretive rather than territorial: it shows the kind of fairy belief circulating in the wider Loch Lomond and Trossachs cultural zone, just beyond the county’s old boundaries. Dunbartonshire’s Loch Lomond side shares that same tourist and storytelling region, so modern readers often encounter “Loch Lomond folklore” as a blended landscape rather than a county-by-county archive.

Within historic Dunbartonshire, Luss now hosts a clearly modern fairy attraction: the Loch Lomond Faerie Trail. Its own description presents a family woodland story about Rhu the Faerie, the “Heart of the Loch”, clues, hideaways and magical friends.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.lochlomondfaerietrail.com/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: lochlomondfaerietrail.com">[lochlomondfaerietrail.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">lochlomondfaerietrail.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on lochlomondfaerietrail.com.</span></span></span> This is not old oral tradition in the same sense as saint legends or witch records. It is a modern imaginative retelling that uses fairy motifs to make the lochside landscape playful and accessible for children and visitors.

That distinction is useful rather than dismissive. Old fairy belief in Scotland could be dangerous, eerie and morally serious; modern fairy trails are usually gentle, ecological and family-friendly. Dunbartonshire’s folklore page should therefore treat fairy material in two layers: the older regional tradition of fairy hills and hidden people, and the newer visitor culture that turns those motifs into trails, puzzles and outdoor storytelling.

## The Whangie and the Devil’s cut in the Kilpatrick Hills

The Whangie, in the Kilpatrick Hills, is one of the best landscape legends associated with the Dunbartonshire edge. It is a long, narrow rock cleft on Auchineden Hill, popular with walkers because it feels like a hidden passage sliced into the hill. Local folklore says the Devil made it with his tail while flying over the hill after a gathering of witches and warlocks.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://westendermagazine.com/articles/the-whangie-with-pocket-mountains/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: westendermagazine.com">[westendermagazine.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">westendermagazine.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Whangie</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Whangie</span></span></span>

The geological explanation is less demonic but just as interesting. Accounts of the Kilpatrick Hills describe the Whangie as a separated slice of hillside forming a chasm roughly 100 metres long; older explanations invoked glacial plucking, while more recent geological interpretation points to a landslide process.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kilpatrick_Hills" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Kilpatrick Hills</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Kilpatrick Hills</span></span></span> The name itself is often connected with the Scots word “whang”, meaning a slice, which neatly matches the appearance of the place.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.thetimes.co.uk/article/walk-of-the-week-the-whangie-kilpatrick-hills-stirling-w9lhsxwkh" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: thetimes.co.uk">[The Times]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">thetimes.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on thetimes.co.uk.</span></span></span>

This is classic landscape folklore: an unusual feature demands a story. The Devil’s tail explains the cut in memorable human terms, while geology explains the physical process. Neither cancels the other as culture. The legend tells us how local imagination made sense of a strange and dramatic place; the science tells us how the hill actually split. Together they make the Whangie one of the county’s most readable examples of mythic landscape.

## Cochno Stone: prehistoric mystery without easy answers

The Cochno Stone near Faifley is one of Dunbartonshire’s most important ancient sites, and it has become a modern mystery in its own right. Historic Environment Scotland describes it as a rock outcrop carrying prehistoric abstract motifs, probably up to 5,000 years old, on a south-facing slope overlooking the Clyde.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://portal.historicenvironment.scot/apex/f?p=1505%3A300%3A%3A%3A%3A%3AVIEWTYPE%2CVIEWREF%3Adesignation%2CSM716" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: portal.historicenvironment.scot">[Historic Environment Scotland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">portal.historicenvironment.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on historicenvironment.scot.</span></span></span> The Scottish Archaeological Research Framework calls it one of the most extensive Neolithic rock-art sites in Britain, with more than 100 cupmarks and cup-and-ring marks across almost 100 square metres.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://scarf.scot/thematic/future-thinking-on-carved-stones-in-scotland/future-thinking-on-carved-stones-in-scotland-case-studies/case-study-the-cochno-stone-the-contemporary-archaeology-of-rock-art/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: scarf.scot">[scarf.scot]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">scarf.scot</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on scarf.scot.</span></span></span>

The stone’s modern biography is almost as striking as its ancient carving. The Factum Foundation, working with the University of Glasgow, notes that the panel is about 8 by 13 metres and was reburied in 1965 to protect it from vandalism before being investigated again in the 2010s.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.factumfoundation.org/our-projects/digitisation/the-cochno-stone/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: factumfoundation.org">[Factum Foundation]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">factumfoundation.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">the cochno stone</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">the cochno stone</span></span></span> Archaeologist Kenneth Brophy’s study describes the Cochno Stone as one of the most extensive and highly decorated prehistoric rock-art outcrops in Britain, first rediscovered in the late 19th century and re-exposed in 2015 after decades underground.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://eprints.gla.ac.uk/158755/1/158755.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: eprints.gla.ac.uk">[Enlighten Publications]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">eprints.gla.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Enlighten Publicationsthe story of the Cochno Stone, West Dunbartonshire</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Enlighten Publicationsthe story of the Cochno Stone, West Dunbartonshire</span></span></span>

For folklore readers, the key point is restraint. Cup-and-ring marks invite speculation: maps of the stars, ritual diagrams, territorial markers, sacred symbols, pathways for the dead. Some of those ideas may be imaginative and useful as interpretations, but the original meaning is not known. Cochno is therefore not a “decoded” myth but a place where prehistoric art, antiquarian fascination, vandalism, conservation and modern wonder all meet. Its mystery is real precisely because we should not pretend to solve it too easily.

## Loch Lomond, song and the “low road” of memory

Loch Lomond lies partly within the historic county’s cultural world, and its best-known tradition is the song usually called “The Bonnie Banks o’ Loch Lomond”. The Friends of Loch Lomond and The Trossachs explain that the “high road” and “low road” allusions are often interpreted as the main road of the living and the road of death, by which the spirit of a dead soldier returns to Scotland, while also stressing that the lack of a definitive songwriter allows several interpretations.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.lochlomondtrossachs.org.uk/loch-lomond-song" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: lochlomondtrossachs.org.uk">[Friends of Loch Lomond &amp; the Trossachs]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">lochlomondtrossachs.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on lochlomondtrossachs.org.uk.</span></span></span>

That uncertainty is part of the song’s power. It is often linked to the Jacobite rising of 1745, and many retellings imagine two captured soldiers, one released and one condemned.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Bonnie_Banks_o%27_Loch_Lomond" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Bonnie Banks o&#x27; Loch Lomond</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Bonnie Banks o&#x27; Loch Lomond</span></span></span> Whether or not that origin story can be pinned down, the song turns Loch Lomond into a place of longing, exile and impossible return. In folklore terms, the “low road” is especially suggestive because it sounds like an underworld route: faster than the living road, but only because it belongs to death.

The song also shows how tradition keeps changing. It has moved from folk repertoire into choral arrangements, popular performance, Scottish sporting culture and modern recordings.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Bonnie_Banks_o%27_Loch_Lomond" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Bonnie Banks o&#x27; Loch Lomond</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Bonnie Banks o&#x27; Loch Lomond</span></span></span> That makes it one of the most widely recognised pieces of Dunbartonshire-adjacent folklore, even though many singers and listeners know it simply as a Scottish song rather than a county tradition.

## Old county, modern retellings

Dunbartonshire’s folklore is easy to misread if modern boundaries are treated as fixed. The historic county includes places now administered by several councils, and its stories belong to older geographies: the Lennox, Strathclyde, the Clyde, Loch Lomond, the Kilpatrick Hills, parish churches, castles, estates and old roads. The Gazetteer for British Place Names describes Dunbartonshire as a historic county split into two parts, with the larger part along the north shore of the Clyde and northwards between Loch Long and Loch Lomond, while the eastern detached part contains places such as Cumbernauld and Kirkintilloch.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://gazetteer.org.uk/search?place=Dunbartonshire&amp;type=em" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gazetteer.org.uk">[Gazetteer]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gazetteer.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on gazetteer.org.uk.</span></span></span>

This helps explain the county’s mixed folklore character. It is partly Highland-edge and lochside, partly Clyde burgh and industrial, partly early Christian, partly Brittonic and Arthurian, partly Lowland legal record. Dumbarton Rock looks west to the Clyde and back to Alt Clut; Luss looks north along Loch Lomond; Clydebank and Faifley bring prehistoric rock art into a modern urban fringe; Cumbernauld and Kirkintilloch remind us that the historic county has a detached eastern story as well.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://abcounties.com/counties/county-profiles/dunbartonshire/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: abcounties.com">[Association of British Counties]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">abcounties.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on abcounties.com.</span></span></span>

Modern tourism has softened some traditions and revived others. Fairy belief becomes a family trail at Luss; the Whangie becomes a scenic walk with a Devil story; St Kessog becomes pilgrimage and heritage; Dumbarton’s witch-trial past becomes museum interpretation and memorial discussion.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.lochlomondfaerietrail.com/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: lochlomondfaerietrail.com">[lochlomondfaerietrail.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">lochlomondfaerietrail.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on lochlomondfaerietrail.com.</span></span></span> The best way to read Dunbartonshire folklore is therefore not as a catalogue of monsters, but as a set of local memories attached to powerful places.

## What to remember about Dunbartonshire folklore

Dunbartonshire’s strongest folklore is landscape-led. Dumbarton Rock carries the memory of ancient kings, Viking siege, Merlin associations and Arthurian literary afterlife. Luss and Old Kilpatrick preserve saintly and sacred-water traditions. The Whangie turns a geological cleft into the mark of the Devil. Cochno Stone offers deep prehistoric mystery without a secure translation. The witchcraft records show how fear and law shaped real lives in 17th-century Dumbarton. Loch Lomond’s song tradition adds longing, death and return to the county’s wider imaginative map.

The county’s folklore is also unusually good at showing the difference between kinds of tradition. Some material is historically documented, such as witch-trial records and castle history. Some is medieval literary legend, such as Merlin at Dumbarton. Some is saintly local tradition, such as Kessog at Luss. Some is antiquarian and archaeological mystery, such as Cochno. Some is modern retelling, such as the Loch Lomond Faerie Trail. Read together, these stories make Dunbartonshire a county of thresholds: between Highlands and Lowlands, river and rock, saint and fairy, history and legend, old county identity and modern visitor imagination.
