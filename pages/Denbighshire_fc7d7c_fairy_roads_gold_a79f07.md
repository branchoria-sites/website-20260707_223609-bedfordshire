## Introduction

[Denbighshire]({{ 'where-denbighshires-legends-still/' | relative_url }})’s fairy-road and hidden-gold traditions are not about a single mapped “fairy road” in the modern tourist sense. They are about how North Welsh stories turn ordinary routes across fields, stiles, moorland ridges and market roads into dangerous crossings between everyday life and the fairy world. In the uplands around Bodfari, Pentrefoelas, Cerrig-y-drudion and the Denbigh Moors, fairies appear at the point where people are moving through open country: children playing near a stile, a weaver crossing the Garn towards Bala, workers walking long distances, or travellers returning from market.



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/Denbighshire_fc7d7c_fairy_roads_gold_a79f07-overview.webp" | relative_url }}" alt="Overview image for Fairy Roads" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

The best-attested examples come through the Victorian cleric and antiquary Elias Owen, whose *Welsh Folk-Lore* was based on material gathered across North Wales and published in 1896 after winning the 1887 National Eisteddfod prize. The National Library of Wales identifies Owen as a cleric, antiquary and diligent collector of local tradition, and his own preface says much of his material came from older Welsh-speaking informants encountered during school-inspection journeys in the Diocese of St Asaph.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.library.wales/discover-learn/digital-exhibitions/books/history-books/welsh-folk-lore-a-collection-of-the-folk-tales-and-legends-of-north-wales" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: library.wales">[National Library of Wales]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">library.wales</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on library.wales.</span></span></span>

## Why Denbighshire’s fairies lure travellers

The lure in these Denbighshire upland stories is usually simple: music, movement, beauty, money or the promise of sudden rescue from poverty. The danger is just as plain. A person who follows, speaks too freely, delays, boasts, looks where they should not, or fails to understand the rules loses the gift or narrowly escapes harm. These are not distant palace tales. They belong to hedges, gates, moorland crossings, bare ridges and secluded valleys.

That is why “fairy roads” is a useful phrase for this subtopic, even where the sources do not label a named lane as such. The road is the mechanism. In the Cae Caled story, the stile marks the boundary between the children’s ordinary play space and the frightening field encounter. In the chest-of-gold story, the upland route from Cerniogau Mawr towards Bala becomes a path of temptation. In the fairy-money tales Owen groups nearby, the place where money appears is a repeated walking route: a worker’s daily passage, a hearth crossed at night, a green spot beside a gate.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/20096/pg20096-images.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Project Gutenberg Welsh Folk-Lore</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Project Gutenberg Welsh Folk-Lore</span></span></span>

The landscape helps explain the pattern. Mynydd Hiraethog, also known as the Denbigh Moors, is described by Heneb as a large upland area between the Conwy and Clwyd valleys, much of it around 400 to 500 metres above Ordnance Datum, with valleys cutting into an otherwise bleak rolling moorland. Modern Denbighshire Countryside Service similarly presents the Hiraethog Trail as a route through rolling heather moor, deep forest, quiet lakes, public footpaths and quiet lanes linking Pentrefoelas, Cerrigydrudion, Llanfihangel Glyn Myfyr and Llanrhaeadr.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://heneb.org.uk/hcla/mynydd-hiraethog/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: heneb.org.uk">[Heneb]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">heneb.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Heneb Mynydd Hiraethog</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Heneb Mynydd Hiraethog</span></span></span>

## Bodfari’s elf dancers at Cae Caled

The most vivid Denbighshire fairy-road episode is the Cae Caled encounter at Bodfari. Owen prints it from the account of Dr Edward Williams, born at Glan Clwyd, Bodfari, in 1750, who remembered an incident from 1757. Williams and three other children were playing in a field called Cae Caled near a hedge and a stile when they saw a group of small, red-clad beings dancing in the middle of the field. The children fled towards the stile; one of the beings ran after them and stretched towards Williams, but did not cross after him.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/20096/pg20096-images.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Project Gutenberg Welsh Folk-Lore</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Project Gutenberg Welsh Folk-Lore</span></span></span>

The power of the story lies in its ordinary setting. The children are not wandering through an enchanted forest. They are in a named field, near a house, by a hedge, close to a stile, in daylight around midsummer. The frightening part is that the supernatural interrupts the familiar. The stile becomes a threshold: on one side is the field where the dancers appear; on the other is the route back to house, family and adult explanation. When the adults return to investigate, they find no trace that explains the event.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/20096/pg20096-images.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Project Gutenberg Welsh Folk-Lore</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Project Gutenberg Welsh Folk-Lore</span></span></span>

Owen is careful about classification. He notes that Wirt Sikes, in *British Goblins*, treated the Cae Caled beings as mine fairies or “knockers”, but Owen rejects that label because Williams called them “Beings” rather than mine spirits. Owen elsewhere distinguishes unseen mine knockers from fairies who, in popular belief, could be seen by mortal eyes.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/20096/pg20096-images.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Project Gutenberg Welsh Folk-Lore</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Project Gutenberg Welsh Folk-Lore</span></span></span>

This matters because it keeps the Bodfari tale rooted in field folklore rather than mining folklore. It is not a story of underground ore, warning knocks or industrial luck. It is a story of visible, dancing, pursuing figures in a rural parish landscape. Curious Clwyd’s modern retelling preserves the same emphasis, locating Cae Caled as part of a larger field now known as Cae Ffynnon between Glan Clwyd Isa and Glan Clwyd Ganol, off the Aberwheeler to Llandyrnog road.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.mythslegendsodditiesnorth-east-wales.co.uk/bodfari-goblins" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: mythslegendsodditiesnorth-east-wales.co.uk">[Curious Clwyd]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">mythslegendsodditiesnorth-east-wales.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Curious Clwyd Bodfari Goblins &#124; Curious Clwyd</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Curious Clwyd Bodfari Goblins &#124; Curious Clwyd</span></span></span>

## The weaver, the Garn and fairy gold

The clearest hidden-gold tale in this upland cluster is Owen’s “The Fairies and their Chest of Gold”. Owen says he received it from the Rev. Owen Jones, Vicar of Pentrefoelas. The central figure is David the weaver, who lived at Llurig near Cerniogau Mawr, between Pentrefoelas and Cerrig-y-drudion. One day, while crossing the hill towards Bala, David meets two fairies on top of the Garn. They promise to show him a chest filled with gold and tell him the gold will be his.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/20096/pg20096-images.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Project Gutenberg Welsh Folk-Lore</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Project Gutenberg Welsh Folk-Lore</span></span></span>

The route is important. David is not digging for treasure. He is travelling over a bleak mountain route, and the fairies divert him from his intended journey. They lead him for miles across bare upland ground to a secluded glen beneath the mountain, where the treasure appears. His poverty makes the offer believable within the story: he is “in want of money”, so the lure works because it answers a real human pressure.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/20096/pg20096-images.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Project Gutenberg Welsh Folk-Lore</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Project Gutenberg Welsh Folk-Lore</span></span></span>

David knows enough fairy lore to understand that he must claim the treasure immediately. Since the chest is too heavy to carry, he thrusts his walking stick into the gold as a sign of ownership and hurries home. His caution is also traditional: he warns his wife not to tell anyone, because disclosure will offend the fairies and cost them the gift. She breaks the rule by telling a neighbour, Samuel the miller. When David and his wife oversleep and return after sunrise, the chest and the stick have vanished. Soon afterwards Samuel is seen to have come into money, leaving the implication that he rose early and took the treasure first.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/20096/pg20096-images.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Project Gutenberg Welsh Folk-Lore</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Project Gutenberg Welsh Folk-Lore</span></span></span>

The story is not merely “fairies give gold, people lose gold”. It is a compact moral drama about timing, secrecy and social envy. The fairies lure David with wealth, but the real failure happens back in the human village: boasting, delay and neighbourly competition undo the promise. The upland path opens the fairy opportunity; domestic talk destroys it.

## How upland paths shape fairy danger

These Denbighshire stories make most sense when read as tales of movement through marginal ground. Pentrefoelas sits on the historic county map of Denbighshire even though it is now administered by Conwy, and Wikishire describes it as a village and parish on the A5 between Betws-y-coed and Cerrigydrudion, with a large parish including part of Mynydd Hiraethog, Llyn Alwen and Alwen Reservoir.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://wikishire.co.uk/wiki/Pentrefoelas" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wikishire.co.uk">[Wikishire]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wikishire.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Wikishire Pentrefoelas</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wikishire Pentrefoelas</span></span></span>

Cerniogau Mawr also matters as a travel landmark. Curious Travellers notes that the Cernioge Inn, near today’s A5, became an important stopping place on the Holyhead road, midway between Betws-y-Coed and Corwen, with travellers using it for accommodation and horse-changing in the coaching period. That later coaching history is not the origin of David the weaver’s fairy tale, but it shows why this upland belt was remembered through movement, stopping places and exposed journeys.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://curioustravellers.ac.uk/the-cernioge-inn-a-forgotten-spot-on-the-welsh-tour/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: curioustravellers.ac.uk">[Curious Travellers]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">curioustravellers.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on curioustravellers.ac.uk.</span></span></span>

The mechanism is repeated across the tales:

* **A person is away from settled supervision.** The children are in the field before adults arrive; David is alone on the Garn; the poor worker in Owen’s nearby silver-coin tale walks several miles to work and finds money by a green spot beside a gate.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/20096/pg20096-images.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Project Gutenberg Welsh Folk-Lore</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Project Gutenberg Welsh Folk-Lore</span></span></span>
* **The fairy world appears through a small interruption.** Dancers in a field, two guides on a ridge, a coin on the sward, a palace by a mountain lake, or money on a hearth are all sudden changes in familiar space.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/20096/pg20096-images.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Project Gutenberg Welsh Folk-Lore</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Project Gutenberg Welsh Folk-Lore</span></span></span>
* **The gift has a rule.** Do not delay; do not tell; do not pry; do not assume fairy wealth behaves like ordinary wealth. Owen explicitly frames fairy riches as generous but capricious, rewarding some mortals while withdrawing the gift when the rules are broken.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/20096/pg20096-images.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Project Gutenberg Welsh Folk-Lore</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Project Gutenberg Welsh Folk-Lore</span></span></span>
* **The route does not stay neutral.** The path home, the stile, the ridge and the gate become testing points. The traveller has to decide whether to follow, flee, keep silent, return quickly or involve others.

This is why the Denbighshire uplands are not just a backdrop. Their remoteness, long routes, ridges, moorland hollows and parish edges provide the story machinery. A road through such a landscape is never just a line between two villages; in folklore it is also a place where judgement, temptation and danger can appear without warning.

## Hidden gold and the rules of fairy wealth

Owen’s fairy-gift chapter places David’s chest of gold alongside several related North Welsh money tales, and the comparison sharpens the Denbighshire material. In one, a poor shoemaker walking to work finds a silver coin every day on a round green patch near a gate on Tan-y-Coed farm; when he tells his wife where the money comes from, the coins stop. In another, a tidy woman receives a bright new shilling from fairies who enjoy her clean hearth, but the reward ends after she tells a neighbour.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/20096/pg20096-images.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Project Gutenberg Welsh Folk-Lore</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Project Gutenberg Welsh Folk-Lore</span></span></span>

The shared rule is secrecy. Fairy money is not treated like wages, inheritance or ordinary trade. It is conditional, private and easily lost. The rule creates a useful tension for the storyteller: the gift improves a poor person’s life, but improvement itself attracts questions. Once neighbours notice the new coins or hear the boast, the magic collapses.

David’s story adds a sharper upland twist. The treasure is not a repeated coin but a whole chest, and the loss is not simply that the fairies withdraw it. A human neighbour may have taken advantage of the broken secrecy. That makes the tale darker and more socially pointed. Fairy danger and human opportunism work together.

There is also a common anti-greed pattern, but it should not be overstated. David is not punished simply for wanting money. Owen says he is poor, and the fairies’ offer initially appears generous. The problem is not desire alone; it is failure to handle a fairy gift according to fairy rules. In Denbighshire terms, the mountain gives a chance, but the village tests whether the chance can be kept.

## Old oral tradition, antiquarian print and modern retelling

The main evidence for these traditions is strong by folklore standards, but it is not the same as modern recorded oral history. Owen was a serious collector for his period, and the Dictionary of Welsh Biography records his career as a cleric, antiquary and author whose best-known work included *Welsh Folk-Lore*. It also notes his role as diocesan inspector of schools in St Asaph, a position that helped him encounter local traditions across North Wales.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://biography.wales/article/s-OWEN-ELI-1833" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: biography.wales">[Dictionary of Welsh Biography]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">biography.wales</span><span class="citation-popover-title">s OWEN ELI 1833</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">s OWEN ELI 1833</span></span></span>

Owen’s own preface is especially useful because it tells readers how the material was gathered. He says he sought out aged inhabitants, often with local clergy, wrote traditions down on scraps of paper and later transferred them to notebooks. He also says most of his information from the people was spoken in Welsh and that he aimed to translate it literally, without embellishment.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/20096/pg20096-images.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Project Gutenberg Welsh Folk-Lore</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Project Gutenberg Welsh Folk-Lore</span></span></span>

That does not make every detail immune from doubt. Victorian collectors shaped their books through selection, translation, comparison and moral commentary. The gendered aside in the David story about women’s inability to keep silent, for example, is part of Owen’s period voice and should be read critically rather than as neutral folklore fact. What remains valuable is the cluster of names, places, routes and informant links: Bodfari, Cae Caled, Glan Clwyd, Pentrefoelas, Cerniogau Mawr, Cerrig-y-drudion, the Garn and Bala.

Modern retellings tend to make the stories more visitor-friendly. Curious Clwyd, for example, presents the Cae Caled encounter as one of North East Wales’s more unsettling fairy episodes and gives a present-day location note. Denbighshire Countryside Service promotes Hiraethog as a walking landscape of moor, forest, lakes and quiet lanes. These modern frames are not the same as old oral belief, but they show how the stories still fit the way people experience the county: on foot, by road, across open upland space.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.mythslegendsodditiesnorth-east-wales.co.uk/bodfari-goblins" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: mythslegendsodditiesnorth-east-wales.co.uk">[Curious Clwyd]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">mythslegendsodditiesnorth-east-wales.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Curious Clwyd Bodfari Goblins &#124; Curious Clwyd</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Curious Clwyd Bodfari Goblins &#124; Curious Clwyd</span></span></span>

## Why this tradition matters in Denbighshire folklore

Fairy roads and hidden gold matter because they show Denbighshire folklore working at ground level. The county’s fairy tradition is not only about grand otherworlds, named fairy queens or literary romance. It is about what can happen when a familiar path becomes strange. A field near Bodfari becomes a place of pursuit. A route over the Garn becomes a road to treasure. A gate, hearth or green patch becomes a point where poverty and luck meet.

The historic county frame is important here. Wikishire places Pentrefoelas and Cerrigydrudion within Denbighshire’s historic geography, even though modern local-government boundaries have shifted. The wider county landscape also includes high mountains, the Clwydian Range, the Denbigh Moors and Mynydd Hiraethog, so these upland tales belong naturally within Denbighshire’s older mental map rather than only within present council lines.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://wikishire.co.uk/wiki/Denbighshire" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wikishire.co.uk">[Wikishire]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wikishire.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Wikishire Denbighshire</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wikishire Denbighshire</span></span></span>

The stories also connect Denbighshire to wider Welsh fairy tradition without losing their local character. Sikes’s *British Goblins* helped popularise Welsh fairy categories for English-language readers, including mountain fairies who lure travellers on lonely roads, but Owen’s Denbighshire examples are more precise and more grounded. The Bodfari dancers are not a generic fairy type; they are remembered in a named field. David’s gold is not abstract fairy treasure; it is found by a weaver from Llurig, near Cerniogau Mawr, on a route over the Garn.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://publicdomainreview.org/collection/british-goblins-welsh-folk-lore-fairy-mythology-legends-and-traditions-1880/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: publicdomainreview.org">[The Public Domain Review]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">publicdomainreview.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">british goblins welsh folk lore fairy mythology legends and traditions 1880</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">british goblins welsh folk lore fairy mythology legends and traditions 1880</span></span></span>

That specificity is the lasting appeal. Denbighshire’s upland fairy lore asks a question that still works for modern readers and walkers: what changes when a lonely path is treated not merely as scenery, but as a threshold? In these tales, the answer is that the landscape becomes morally alert. It watches the traveller’s courage, poverty, curiosity, secrecy and timing — and it gives or takes away accordingly.
