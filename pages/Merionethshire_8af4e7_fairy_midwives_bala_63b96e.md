## Introduction

The fairy-midwife tale around Bala is one of [Merionethshire]({{ 'where-merionethshire-turns-landscape/' | relative_url }})’s sharpest little stories about living beside the hidden folk. In the version recorded by Elias Owen, a midwife from Llanuwchllyn is called to Coed y Garth, identified as Siambra Duon, the home of the Tylwyth Teg, to help at a fairy birth. She is warned not to touch her eyes with the baby’s washing water, but does so by accident. Later, in Bala, she recognises the fairy husband and asks after his household. He realises she can see him, asks which eye has the sight, and takes that sight from her in the harshest possible way.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/files/20096/20096-h/20096-h.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on gutenberg.org.</span></span></span>



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/Merionethshire_8af4e7_fairy_midwives_bala_63b96e-overview.webp" | relative_url }}" alt="Overview image for Fairy Midwives" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

The story matters because it is not just “a fairy tale” in the loose modern sense. It is a local Merionethshire tradition tied to named places at the south-western end of Llyn Tegid, with Llanuwchllyn standing near the lake and Bala close enough for the final encounter to feel like an everyday meeting in town.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.genuki.org.uk/big/wal/MER/Llanuwchllyn" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: genuki.org.uk">[GenUKI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">genuki.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on genuki.org.uk.</span></span></span> It shows fairies not as decorative sprites, but as secretive neighbours: powerful, domestic, grateful for skilled human help, yet dangerous when the boundary between their world and ours is crossed.

## The Llanuwchllyn Journey to Coed y Garth

In Owen’s account, the human centre of the tale is a working woman, a midwife living at Llanuwchllyn. That detail is important. Llanuwchllyn is a village and parish in historic Merionethshire, near the southern end of [Bala Lake]({{ 'bala-lake/' | relative_url }}), and modern descriptions still place it by Llyn Tegid rather than treating it as an abstract fairy setting.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.genuki.org.uk/big/wal/MER/Llanuwchllyn" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: genuki.org.uk">[GenUKI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">genuki.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on genuki.org.uk.</span></span></span> The tale’s geography therefore runs along a real local line: from a lakeside community, to a hidden fairy dwelling at Coed y Garth or Siambra Duon, and then back into the ordinary social space of Bala.

The fairy summons is practical rather than romantic. A fairy household needs a human midwife for childbirth. That makes the hidden folk oddly familiar: they marry, bear children, keep homes, and need help at moments of danger. But the location makes them unfamiliar again. Coed y Garth is not presented as a public fairy palace open to human curiosity; it is a concealed place, reachable because the midwife is called, not because she goes looking for enchantment. Owen’s wording identifies it as the home of the Tylwyth Teg, the Welsh “fair folk” or fairy people, and that phrase carries a traditional politeness: fair names for beings one does not want to offend.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/files/20096/20096-h/20096-h.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on gutenberg.org.</span></span></span>

This makes the Bala story feel different from grander Welsh myth. It is not about kings, giants or saints, but about a professional errand. The midwife is not a chosen heroine. She is someone whose ordinary expertise gives her temporary access to a hidden household. That is why the tale works so well as local folklore: it brings the supernatural into a recognisable rural economy of favours, journeys, births and neighbourly conversation.

The final meeting at Bala is just as important as the secret journey. The midwife does not lose her fairy sight inside the otherworld. She loses it afterwards, in the ordinary world, because she recognises a fairy man in public and speaks too freely. Owen says she asks how things are at Siambra Duon: how the wife is, and how the little family is.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/files/20096/20096-h/20096-h.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on gutenberg.org.</span></span></span> The question is kind, but it proves she can see what she should not see. In this tradition, goodwill is not enough to protect a human who has accidentally gained forbidden knowledge.

## Second Sight, Secrecy and Punishment

The mechanism of the tale is simple and memorable: fairy washing water touches the midwife’s eye, and that eye gains the power to see the hidden folk. Owen’s version uses water from washing the baby, while many wider fairy-midwife tales use ointment. The difference matters because it keeps the Llanuwchllyn version close to the birth scene itself. The dangerous substance is not a magic object brought in for spectacle; it is part of the intimate work of caring for a newborn.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/files/20096/20096-h/20096-h.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on gutenberg.org.</span></span></span>

The warning is also precise. The fairies tell the midwife not to touch her eyes with the baby’s washing water. She breaks the rule unintentionally, which gives the tale a hard moral edge. This is not a story about deliberate theft of fairy power. It is about the risks that come with crossing into a hidden household at all. Once a human has entered that space, even an accidental gesture can change what she is able to see.

The punishment is severe. When the fairy husband asks which eye she sees him with, she points to her left eye. Owen’s printed text says he plucks that eye out, while also noting that other versions soften or vary the ending: in some, the woman is deprived only of supernatural sight, and in others she becomes blind in one eye.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/files/20096/20096-h/20096-h.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on gutenberg.org.</span></span></span> That variation is useful. It suggests that the core of the tradition is not gore for its own sake, but the removal of illicit sight. The eye is the problem because the eye has crossed the boundary.

The story therefore turns “second sight” into a liability. In some traditions, the ability to see hidden beings can sound like a gift. Here it is more like evidence against the human. The midwife’s eye allows her to recognise a fairy in Bala, but recognition is exactly what the fairies cannot permit. Their safety depends on remaining unseen among ordinary people.

This is why the tale has such staying power. It sets up a memorable bargain without stating it as a contract: humans may help the hidden folk, and may even be rewarded or allowed to return safely, but they must not expose them. The midwife’s kindness gives her access; her sight makes her dangerous; her speech gives her away.

## How the Tale Fits Wider Fairy-Midwife Traditions

The Llanuwchllyn story belongs to a much wider family of fairy-midwife and fairy-ointment tales found across Britain and Europe. Folklore indexes commonly group these under the “midwife for the elves” pattern, including Aarne-Thompson-Uther type 476 variants and migratory legends in which a human nurse or midwife is called to serve supernatural beings.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://sites.pitt.edu/~dash/midwife.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sites.pitt.edu">[University of Pittsburgh]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sites.pitt.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on pitt.edu.</span></span></span> The usual pattern is recognisable: a human woman is summoned to a birth, some substance gives her fairy sight, she later reveals that she can see a fairy, and she loses the enchanted eye.

What makes the Bala-area version distinctive is the local compression. Some versions dwell on a false palace that becomes a cave, or on fairy glamour falling away after ointment is applied. In the Llanuwchllyn account, Owen gives a brisker and more place-bound narrative: the call to Coed y Garth, the warning, the accidental touch, the later meeting in Bala, and the punishment.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/files/20096/20096-h/20096-h.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on gutenberg.org.</span></span></span> The effect is not a long wonder-tale but a local caution: this happened to a midwife from here, who went there, and later met him in town.

John Rhys recorded related Welsh material, including a Corwrion version in which a midwife is taken to what appears to be a splendid palace and, after using ointment, finds herself sitting on a tuft of rushes rather than in grandeur. Owen explicitly points readers to Rhys’s variant when discussing the midwife motif.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gutenberg.org/files/20096/20096-h/20096-h.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gutenberg.org">[Project Gutenberg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gutenberg.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on gutenberg.org.</span></span></span> That contrast helps explain the range of the tradition. Some Welsh versions stress glamour: fairy splendour is an illusion. The Llanuwchllyn version stresses visibility: the fairy man is present in the ordinary world, but should remain unseen.

The same wider pattern appears in English, Irish, German and Scandinavian examples. D. L. Ashliman’s comparative collection brings together variants under the “Midwife for the Elves” heading, while later folklore summaries describe the recurring plot of a midwife accidentally gaining fairy sight and being blinded or deprived of that power once she reveals it.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://sites.pitt.edu/~dash/midwife.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sites.pitt.edu">[University of Pittsburgh]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sites.pitt.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on pitt.edu.</span></span></span> The Bala tale is therefore both local and international: a Merionethshire expression of a story-shape that travelled widely through oral tradition.

That wider context should not flatten the local version. Folklore often works by fitting a known pattern to a named landscape. Around Bala, the hidden household is not merely “under a hill” or “in fairyland”; it is Coed y Garth, Siambra Duon, and the social aftershock happens at Bala. Those names are what make the story belong to Merionethshire rather than to a general fairy anthology.

## Why Bala’s Landscape Suits Hidden Folk

The Bala and Llanuwchllyn landscape gives the tale a natural home. Llyn Tegid is the largest natural lake in Wales, and modern visitor material still presents the Bala-Llanuwchllyn route as a lakeside journey with mountain views towards Aran Fawddwy, Cadair Idris and Arenig Fawr.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.visitsnowdonia.info/bala-llanuwchllyn" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: visitsnowdonia.info">[Visit Snowdonia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">visitsnowdonia.info</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on visitsnowdonia.info.</span></span></span> It is a landscape of water, woodland, slopes and old routes: exactly the sort of terrain in which hidden households, fairy roads and sudden encounters can feel imaginatively plausible.

Local fairy tradition also sits beside other Bala legends rather than replacing them. Llyn Tegid is better known in many modern retellings for drowned-court stories, Taliesin and the lake creature nicknamed Teggie; Literature Wales’s Land of Legends material, for example, treats Llyn Tegid as a major watery folklore site.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.landoflegends.wales/theme/watery-worlds" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: landoflegends.wales">[Land of Legends]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">landoflegends.wales</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on landoflegends.wales.</span></span></span> The midwife tale is quieter, but it adds a different kind of supernatural texture. Instead of something beneath the lake, it gives the district hidden neighbours near the lake.

Modern heritage and tourism tend to foreground scenery, outdoor activity and broad mythic atmosphere. Eryri National Park’s folklore pages describe the region as a landscape where mountains, rivers and lakes are thick with stories, while Visit Wales presents Bala as culturally rich and surrounded by mountains.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://eryri.gov.wales/discover/history-and-heritage/mythology-and-folklore/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: eryri.gov.wales">[Eryri National Park]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">eryri.gov.wales</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on gov.wales.</span></span></span> The midwife story fits that public-facing landscape well, but it also resists being turned into a cosy fairy postcard. Its fairies are domestic, but not harmless. They can be grateful enough to call a human specialist, yet ruthless enough to enforce invisibility.

That tension is part of the county’s folklore identity. Merionethshire stories often attach danger to beauty: a lake may have a drowned world beneath it, a mountain may grant poetry or madness, and a fairy household may need a midwife but punish her for seeing too much. The Llanuwchllyn tale belongs precisely in that tradition of beautiful places with strict unseen rules.

## What the Midwife Story Tells Us About the Fairies

The hidden folk around Bala are not portrayed as tiny winged ornaments. They are a parallel community. They have husbands, wives, babies, homes and social rules. They can enter the human world and pass through Bala unnoticed. They can also draw a human into their own space when they need her skill. That makes them close enough to resemble neighbours, but separate enough to be dangerous.

The tale’s most revealing features are practical:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **They need human help.** The fairy birth requires a human midwife, suggesting dependence as well as power.
* **They control access.** The midwife is summoned; she does not discover the place by her own mastery.
* **They guard visibility.** The problem is not that she helped, but that she can later see and identify them.
* **They enforce silence through fear.** The punishment teaches that fairy knowledge must not be carried casually into public life.

</div>

This is why the story feels so compact and effective. It does not need a long explanation of fairy society. A single episode shows the rules: service may be rewarded or tolerated, but sight and speech are dangerous. The hidden folk can live beside human communities only if the boundary remains intact.

For a modern reader, the tale is also a reminder of how local folklore preserves social anxieties in memorable form. Childbirth was dangerous. Midwives held intimate knowledge. Rural neighbours relied on one another, yet also guarded secrets. The fairy frame turns those human realities into a supernatural encounter. Around Bala, the midwife who saw the fairies becomes a story about care, trust, accidental knowledge and the cost of saying aloud what others cannot see.
