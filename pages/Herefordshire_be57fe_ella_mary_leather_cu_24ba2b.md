## Introduction

Ella Mary Leather is the reason so much of old [Herefordshire]({{ 'why-herefordshires-legends-cling-to/' | relative_url }}) can still be heard in its own voice. Working mainly from Weobley in the early 1900s, she collected rural songs, carols, charms, witchcraft beliefs, seasonal rites and orchard customs from singers and informants whose memories reached back into the 19th century. Her 1912 book, *The Folk-Lore of Herefordshire*, was not just a colourful miscellany: contemporary reviewers treated it as an unusually complete county collection, and the English Folk Dance and Song Society still presents it as one of the important texts of English folklore.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/stream/folklore23folkuoft/folklore23folkuoft_djvu.txt" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Internet Archive Full text of &quot;Folklore</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Internet Archive Full text of &quot;Folklore</span></span></span>



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/Herefordshire_be57fe_ella_mary_leather_cu_24ba2b-overview.webp" | relative_url }}" alt="Overview image for Leather" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

What makes Leather’s work so valuable is its mixture of music, magic and place. Herefordshire appears not as a county of abstract “old beliefs”, but as a lived landscape of cottages, workhouses, gypsy encampments, orchards, cowhouses, wheatfields, Christmas carols, healing charms and remembered local speech. Her material matters because it catches customs at the point where they were already becoming fragile, but had not yet become museum pieces.

## Collecting around Weobley

Leather was born at Bidney in the parish of Dilwyn in 1874 and, after her marriage to Francis Leather in 1893, lived in Weobley, a small Herefordshire town that became the centre of her collecting world. The English Folk Dance and Song Society records that she first contributed “The folk-lore of the shire” to *Memorials of Old Herefordshire* in 1905, then published Herefordshire folktales in 1907, before bringing out *The Folk-Lore of Herefordshire* in 1912.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.efdss.org/learning/resources/beginners-guides/35-english-folk-collectors/2444-efdss-ella-mary-leather" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: efdss.org">[English Folk Dance and Song Society]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">efdss.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on efdss.org.</span></span></span>

That Weobley base mattered. Leather was not simply gathering picturesque legends from printed antiquarian books. Modern descriptions of her work stress that she collected from people around her own community: cottage dwellers, workhouse residents and gypsy families, as well as older local informants who could remember customs no longer widely practised. A recent review of the Logaston Press reprint notes that many informants were credited and quoted closely enough to preserve the feel of an older generation passing on knowledge before it vanished.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://rakinglight.co.uk/book-reviews/the-folk-lore-of-herefordshire-a-classic-county-folklore-collection/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: rakinglight.co.uk">[rakinglight.co.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">rakinglight.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on rakinglight.co.uk.</span></span></span>

Leather’s own limitations also shaped the record. She was not a highly trained musician, and both Leominster Museum and EFDSS note that she found it difficult to write down tunes accurately. In 1906, Ralph Vaughan Williams helped her obtain a phonograph, an early recording machine, and this changed the quality of what she could preserve: songs could be captured as sound first, then transcribed with expert help.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://leominstermuseum.org.uk/2024-project-sharing-songs-and-stories/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: leominstermuseum.org.uk">[Leominster Museum]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">leominstermuseum.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">2024 project sharing songs and stories</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">2024 project sharing songs and stories</span></span></span>

The partnership with Vaughan Williams has sometimes overshadowed Leather herself, but it should be read the other way round too. Leather knew the county, the singers, the families and the places; Vaughan Williams brought musical transcription and national reach. EFDSS notes that Sharp and Vaughan Williams made expeditions to Weobley and accompanied Leather to local gypsy encampments, including the 1912 visit to Monkland where Vaughan Williams collected a version of “The Unquiet Grave” from the gypsy tenor Alfred Price Jones.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.efdss.org/learning/resources/beginners-guides/35-english-folk-collectors/2444-efdss-ella-mary-leather" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: efdss.org">[English Folk Dance and Song Society]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">efdss.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on efdss.org.</span></span></span>

## Songs, carols, charms and witchcraft lore

Leather’s work is often remembered through song, but *The Folk-Lore of Herefordshire* was far broader than a songbook. HathiTrust’s catalogue describes the 1912 volume as *The folk-lore of Herefordshire, collected from oral and printed sources*, published in Hereford by Jakeman and Carver, and notes that it “contains music”.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://catalog.hathitrust.org/Record/001276501" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: catalog.hathitrust.org">[HathiTrust]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">catalog.hathitrust.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on hathitrust.org.</span></span></span> The 1912 review in the journal *Folk-Lore* listed its range as natural objects, tree and plant superstitions, animal life, supernatural phenomena, witchcraft, diviners, magic, leechcraft, festivals, ceremonial customs, games, local customs, folk-tales, carols, ballads, songs, legends, riddles, rhymes, proverbs and more.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/stream/folklore23folkuoft/folklore23folkuoft_djvu.txt" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Internet Archive Full text of &quot;Folklore</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Internet Archive Full text of &quot;Folklore</span></span></span>

That breadth is important because Herefordshire’s “old voices” were not divided into tidy modern categories. A singer might know a carol, a charm against sickness, a tale about witchcraft and a custom for midwinter cattle or crops. Leather preserved this mixed world, where Christian carol, household cure, supernatural warning and agricultural rite could sit side by side.

Her songs and carols are among the most visible survivals. EFDSS states that *The Folk-Lore of Herefordshire* included the lyrics and music of 23 traditional carols, ballads and songs, while the later collaboration with Vaughan Williams produced *Twelve Traditional Carols from Herefordshire* in 1920.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.efdss.org/learning/resources/beginners-guides/35-english-folk-collectors/2444-efdss-ella-mary-leather" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: efdss.org">[English Folk Dance and Song Society]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">efdss.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on efdss.org.</span></span></span> The Ralph Vaughan Williams Society describes that 1920 publication as a joint work by Leather and Vaughan Williams, with each carol arranged twice: once for unaccompanied SATB choir and once for voice and piano.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.rvwsociety.com/product/carols-from-herefordshire/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: rvwsociety.com">[Ralph Vaughan Williams Society]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">rvwsociety.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Ralph Vaughan Williams Society Carols from Herefordshire</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Ralph Vaughan Williams Society Carols from Herefordshire</span></span></span>

These carols are not all cosy Christmas-card pieces. The Vaughan Williams Society points out that their words do not always follow biblical canon and are “by no means all about Christmas”, while MusicWeb’s discussion of the 2024 Albion recording notes the presence of apocryphal material and “native moralising” alongside Christmas themes.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.rvwsociety.com/product/carols-from-herefordshire/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: rvwsociety.com">[Ralph Vaughan Williams Society]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">rvwsociety.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Ralph Vaughan Williams Society Carols from Herefordshire</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Ralph Vaughan Williams Society Carols from Herefordshire</span></span></span> That is exactly why they matter for folklore: they show how biblical story, local singing tradition and rural imagination could be blended in performance.

Leather’s charms and magical material are equally revealing. A modern review of *The Folk-Lore of Herefordshire* highlights examples such as a pneumonia cure using a warm sheep’s lung, a stable charm made from seven grey mare’s hairs to keep elves from riding horses, and a wart cure involving stolen beef rubbed nine times on the affected place.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://rakinglight.co.uk/book-reviews/the-folk-lore-of-herefordshire-a-classic-county-folklore-collection/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: rakinglight.co.uk">[rakinglight.co.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">rakinglight.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on rakinglight.co.uk.</span></span></span> These are not presented here as medicine or as instructions to follow. Their value is historical: they show how people tried to manage illness, livestock anxiety and household misfortune before modern healthcare and veterinary knowledge were equally available or trusted.

The witchcraft material sits in the same practical world. Leather’s Herefordshire is not a stage set of pointy hats and broomsticks, but a county where suspicion, illness, bad luck, animals, neighbours and local reputation could become entangled. The 1912 *Folk-Lore* review notes that in Herefordshire “evil magicians” of both sexes were called witches, while the wise man of some counties appeared locally as a “clever man” or “conjurer”.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/stream/folklore23folkuoft/folklore23folkuoft_djvu.txt" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Internet Archive Full text of &quot;Folklore</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Internet Archive Full text of &quot;Folklore</span></span></span> That distinction matters: Herefordshire belief culture included feared harmful magic, but also specialists imagined as having knowledge useful against harm.

## Wassailing and the revival of rural custom

Leather’s most memorable seasonal material is tied to Herefordshire’s agricultural identity: cider, wheat, cattle, orchards and midwinter blessing. The 1912 *Folk-Lore* review singled out the county’s Christmas customs as especially individual. It described a Twelfth Day wassailing custom in which people lit twelve small fires and one large fire in a field of growing wheat, drank cider, then visited the cowhouse, toasted the oxen in ale, placed a holed plum cake on the horn of the first ox, and read omens from the way it fell.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/stream/folklore23folkuoft/folklore23folkuoft_djvu.txt" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Internet Archive Full text of &quot;Folklore</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Internet Archive Full text of &quot;Folklore</span></span></span>

The same review distinguished this from another Herefordshire New Year custom, once widespread and still surviving in parts at the time Leather collected it. In that rite, a bonfire was lit in the first-sown wheatfield; the old hawthorn “bush” from the farmhouse kitchen was burned, a new one was cut and scorched, cider was poured over it, and blazing straw was carried over the ridges to drive away evil, protect wheat from smut or banish “the old ’un”. The men then stood round the fire and slowly shouted “holloa old cider” three times, bowing at every syllable.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/stream/folklore23folkuoft/folklore23folkuoft_djvu.txt" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Internet Archive Full text of &quot;Folklore</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Internet Archive Full text of &quot;Folklore</span></span></span>

This is a useful reminder that “wassailing” in Herefordshire was not only about apple trees, though modern readers often meet it that way. Leather’s material links midwinter drink, crop protection, livestock blessing, fire, noise, omen-reading and household renewal. It belongs to the working year of farms, not simply to festive entertainment.

Modern Herefordshire wassailing has understandably leaned into the orchard image, because cider and apple trees remain central to the county’s public identity. A 2024 Tradfolk account of a Herefordshire wassail describes a January gathering with fires, singing, cider, noise to banish blight and pestilence, and cider poured at the roots of trees. The same article explicitly links the custom back to Leather’s record of the ox toast and Twelfth cake.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://tradfolk.co/customs/wassailing/well-met-in-muddy-lanes-what-it-means-to-wassail-on-twelfth-night/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: tradfolk.co">[Tradfolk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">tradfolk.co</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Well-met in Muddy Lanes: What it means to Wassail on Twelfth Night</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Well-met in Muddy Lanes: What it means to Wassail on Twelfth Night</span></span></span>

The revival is not a perfect survival of an unchanged ancient rite. It is better understood as a modern performance drawing on older recorded fragments, local agriculture and a desire to reconnect custom with place. That does not make it fake. It means Leather’s notes have become part of the machinery by which Herefordshire remembers itself.

## Why Leather still shapes Herefordshire folklore

Leather’s importance lies partly in timing. She was collecting at the moment when older rural customs were still within living memory, but modern change was making them harder to sustain. EFDSS says she was conscious that the modern world would cause many old country customs of her childhood to die out, and Leominster Museum’s 2024 anniversary project puts the point more directly: by recording people who remembered local songs and stories, Leather and Vaughan Williams saved Herefordshire traditions that might otherwise have been lost.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.efdss.org/learning/resources/beginners-guides/35-english-folk-collectors/2444-efdss-ella-mary-leather" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: efdss.org">[English Folk Dance and Song Society]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">efdss.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on efdss.org.</span></span></span>

Her importance also lies in method. The 1912 *Folk-Lore* review praised *The Folk-Lore of Herefordshire* as the most complete account of any English county folklore then published, and said Leather had been “peculiarly successful” in recovering traditional songs and music with Vaughan Williams’s help in reducing phonographic records to writing.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/stream/folklore23folkuoft/folklore23folkuoft_djvu.txt" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Internet Archive Full text of &quot;Folklore</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Internet Archive Full text of &quot;Folklore</span></span></span> For a county folklore project, that makes her work more than a useful source: it is one of the foundations on which later readers, singers, museums and local historians build.

There are limits to remember. Leather was a collector of her period, and the voices she preserved passed through her choices, her social position, her spelling, her editorial judgement and sometimes through the musical arrangements of better-known male collaborators. MusicWeb notes that the 1920 carol editors openly aimed at singable preservation rather than purely scientific publication, keeping much of the original text while correcting grammar, filling gaps and amending perceived corruptions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://musicwebinternational.com/2024/10/carols-from-herefordshire-albion-records/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: musicwebinternational.com">[MusicWeb International]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">musicwebinternational.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Music Web International Carols from Herefordshire (Albion Records</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Music Web International Carols from Herefordshire (Albion Records</span></span></span> That makes the carols living repertoire, but not untouched field recordings in printed form.

The modern afterlife of Leather’s work shows how strongly it still belongs to Herefordshire. Leominster Museum marked the 150th anniversary of her birth in 2024 with displays, activities, musical events, a town trail, school workshops and performances centred on the songs and stories she collected.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://leominstermuseum.org.uk/2024-project-sharing-songs-and-stories/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: leominstermuseum.org.uk">[Leominster Museum]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">leominstermuseum.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">2024 project sharing songs and stories</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">2024 project sharing songs and stories</span></span></span> The Stitched Atlas, a contemporary community textile project, has also used Leather’s published book and her VWML notebook as primary sources for exploring rural Herefordshire places through songs, tunes, dances and stories.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://thestitchedatlas.uk/blog-2/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: thestitchedatlas.uk">[The Stitched Atlas]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">thestitchedatlas.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Stitched Atlas Latest blog posts – The Stitched Atlas</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Stitched Atlas Latest blog posts – The Stitched Atlas</span></span></span>

That is the clearest answer to why Ella Mary Leather still matters. She did not merely write down quaint survivals. She preserved a working archive of Herefordshire memory: the words people sang, the charms they trusted, the fears they named, the cattle and crops they blessed, and the local customs later generations could revive, question, perform and reinterpret.
