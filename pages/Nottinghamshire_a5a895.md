## Introduction

Nottinghamshire folklore is unusually dominated by one figure: [Robin Hood]({{ 'robin-hood/' | relative_url }}). Yet the county’s legendary landscape is wider and stranger than the outlaw alone. It includes Sherwood’s medieval forest memory, saintly and healing wells, Plough Monday plays, witchcraft accusations and cunning folk, a rare black dog tale on Crow Lane, and smaller traditions attached to springs, rivers, caves and haunted houses. The strongest traditions are not all equally old. Robin Hood is rooted in late medieval ballad culture and later civic identity; many holy-well stories survive through antiquarian recording and local heritage work; ghost stories often belong to modern retelling and tourism. The county’s folklore matters because it turns working landscapes — forest, road, river, village green, chapel spring and market town — into places where law, danger, healing, seasonal custom and memory can still be read in story form.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nottinghamshire.gov.uk/culture-leisure/country-parks/history-of-sherwood-forest-robin-hood-and-major-oak" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nottinghamshire.gov.uk">[nottinghamshire.gov.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nottinghamshire.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nottinghamshire.gov.uk.</span></span></span>



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/Nottinghamshire_a5a895-overview.webp" | relative_url }}" alt="Overview image for Where Nottinghamshire Turns History Into..." loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

For this project, “Nottinghamshire” is best understood as the historic county: the inland Midland shire between Derbyshire, Lincolnshire, Yorkshire and Leicestershire, with Nottingham as its county town. The modern local-government picture is more complicated, especially because Nottingham city is administratively separate from Nottinghamshire County Council, but the folklore of the county does not stop at council boundaries. The project’s historic-county map frame is supported by the Wikimedia Commons historic-county map for Nottinghamshire, which marks the historic county rather than only present-day administrative areas.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://wikishire.co.uk/wiki/Nottinghamshire" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wikishire.co.uk">[wikishire.co.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wikishire.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wikishire.co.uk.</span></span></span>

## Why Robin Hood became Nottinghamshire’s defining legend

Robin Hood is not simply “a Nottinghamshire character” in the way a documented landowner or sheriff would be. He is a legendary outlaw built from medieval ballads, later plays, pageantry, antiquarian enthusiasm, tourism, children’s books, cinema, television and local pride. That layered history is exactly why he matters. Nottinghamshire’s most famous folklore is not a single fixed tale, but a long argument over forest, law, fairness and who gets to claim the greenwood.

The county’s own landscape gives the legend its force. Nottinghamshire County Council’s account of Sherwood explains that “forest” in the Middle Ages was a legal term: a royal hunting jurisdiction protected by special laws over timber and game. In the 1200s, the period popularly associated with Robin Hood, Sherwood covered about 100,000 acres, around a fifth of Nottinghamshire, and the Great North Way ran through it, making it a plausible imaginative setting for outlaws, travellers and royal authority colliding on the road.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nottinghamshire.gov.uk/culture-leisure/country-parks/history-of-sherwood-forest-robin-hood-and-major-oak" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nottinghamshire.gov.uk">[Nottinghamshire County Council]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nottinghamshire.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nottinghamshire.gov.uk.</span></span></span>

That does not mean Sherwood was an untouched, endless wilderness. The same county history stresses that medieval Sherwood included oak and birch woodland, sandy heath, rough grassland, deer parks, managed woodland industries and forest settlements. This matters because the Robin Hood stories work best when Sherwood is understood as a lived, regulated, exploited landscape rather than a vague fairy-tale forest. It was a place of work, grazing, timber, law enforcement, monastic estates and aristocratic power.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nottinghamshire.gov.uk/culture-leisure/country-parks/history-of-sherwood-forest-robin-hood-and-major-oak" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nottinghamshire.gov.uk">[Nottinghamshire County Council]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nottinghamshire.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nottinghamshire.gov.uk.</span></span></span>

Sherwood Forest’s modern visitor interpretation is careful about the question of origins. It notes that the legend rests on a small body of ballads, including *A Lyttell Gest of Robyn Hode*, usually placed in the mid-15th century, and that Little John, Will Scarlet and the Sheriff of Nottingham are already part of that early legendary world. It also makes clear that the historical identity of Robin Hood has been debated for centuries, with several candidates proposed but no settled answer.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://visitsherwood.co.uk/about-robin-hood/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: visitsherwood.co.uk">[Sherwood Forest - Where legends grow]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">visitsherwood.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Sherwood Forest</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Sherwood Forest</span></span></span>

What Nottinghamshire has, then, is not proof of one historical Robin. It has something more durable for folklore: a landscape that repeatedly makes the story feel locally true. Nottingham Castle supplies the Sheriff’s seat of power; Sherwood supplies the outlaw refuge; Edwinstowe supplies the wedding tradition of Robin and Maid Marian; and the Major Oak supplies the vivid, photographable “hideout” that generations of visitors have used to picture the tale. Sherwood Forest’s own interpretation describes the Major Oak as rumoured to have been Robin’s hiding place and St Mary’s, Edwinstowe, as the church where Robin and Marian were said to have married.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://visitsherwood.co.uk/about-robin-hood/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: visitsherwood.co.uk">[Sherwood Forest - Where legends grow]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">visitsherwood.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Sherwood Forest</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Sherwood Forest</span></span></span>

The legend is still changing. In 2026, the Major Oak was widely reported to have died after failing to produce leaves, but public reaction showed how powerfully a tree can become folklore’s physical anchor. The Guardian recorded readers describing childhood visits, school trips and the feeling that the tree connected them directly with Robin Hood, Maid Marian and Nottinghamshire’s past. Even as a dead or dying ancient tree, it remains a cultural landmark because people continue to attach story, memory and local identity to it.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.theguardian.com/environment/2026/jun/24/major-oak-sherwood-forest-readers-remember" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theguardian.com">[The Guardian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theguardian.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on theguardian.com.</span></span></span>

## Sherwood is a mythic landscape, not just a backdrop

Sherwood’s folklore works because the forest is both real and symbolic. It is a place you can visit, but also a moral landscape where the rules of ordinary society are suspended. In the Robin Hood tradition, the forest is where unjust power can be mocked, where disguise and archery defeat rank, and where loyalty matters more than status. That is why Nottinghamshire’s best-known legend has travelled so well beyond the county: it turns a local royal forest into a national story about justice and freedom.

The historical forest also explains why the Sheriff matters. A sheriff in the Robin Hood stories is not merely a pantomime villain. He represents law, taxation, capture and the machinery of authority. Nottingham Castle’s Robin Hood interpretation summarises the familiar pattern: Robin is the heroic outlaw, the Sheriff is his enemy, and Sherwood Forest is the setting where much of the early action takes place. Historic England’s listing for the Robin Hood statues at Nottingham Castle likewise records the continuing association between Robin, the Merry Men, Sherwood and Nottingham Castle, and notes that Nottinghamshire people continue to claim Robin Hood as a symbol of the county.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nottinghamcastle.org.uk/the-legend-of-robin-hood/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nottinghamcastle.org.uk">[Nottingham Castle]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nottinghamcastle.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Nottingham Castle The Legend of Robin Hood</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Nottingham Castle The Legend of Robin Hood</span></span></span>

That symbolism has been institutionalised. The Robin Hood Festival, guided walks, character-led interpretation, archery activities and visitor-centre retail at Sherwood are not “ancient folklore” in themselves, but they are modern folklore-making: organised ways of letting each generation re-enact the legend. Sherwood’s visitor body openly frames its work as bringing Robin, his followers and his stories to life through events and activities.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://visitsherwood.co.uk/about-robin-hood/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: visitsherwood.co.uk">[Sherwood Forest - Where legends grow]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">visitsherwood.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Sherwood Forest</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Sherwood Forest</span></span></span>

This is a useful distinction for readers. The early ballads are the older textual tradition. The named trees, statues, trails, festivals and digital attractions are later forms of public storytelling. Both matter, but they should not be confused. Nottinghamshire’s Robin Hood culture is strongest when treated as a long chain of retelling rather than a solved historical puzzle.

## Wells, healing water and fairy-haunted springs

Nottinghamshire’s water folklore is quieter than Sherwood’s outlaw legend, but it gives the county some of its most revealing local traditions. [Holy wells]({{ 'holy-wells-e8a1cf/' | relative_url }}) often sit at the meeting point of religion, healing, place-name memory and everyday need. A spring might begin as a practical water source, gain a saint’s name, attract stories of cures, become neglected after religious change, and later be rediscovered by antiquarians or local historians.

A survey of Nottinghamshire holy wells and healing springs argues that the county has many recorded or possible water sites, but also warns that the evidence varies sharply. It notes that Nottinghamshire does not appear to have as much explicit ritual or folk-custom material around wells as some neighbouring counties, and that some identifications may be uncertain, duplicated or late. That caution is important: not every old spring with a saint’s name can safely be treated as a continuously used sacred site.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.academia.edu/6249688/Holy_wells_and_healing_springs_of_Nottinghamshire" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: academia.edu">[Academia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">academia.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">PDF) Holy wells and healing springs of Nottinghamshire</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">PDF) Holy wells and healing springs of Nottinghamshire</span></span></span>

St Ann’s Well in Nottingham is the county’s most famous healing-water tradition. It was also known as Robin Hood’s Well, showing how two powerful strands of Nottinghamshire folklore — saintly water and the outlaw legend — could overlap. Local-history accounts describe the well as a popular healing and visiting place, later affected by enclosure, urban development, the railway and uncertainty over its exact site. The well was reportedly filled or hidden by railway works in the late 19th century, and later attempts to identify the site have been debated.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://nottinghamhiddenhistoryteam.wordpress.com/2013/01/21/st-anns-well/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nottinghamhiddenhistoryteam.wordpress.com">[wordpress.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nottinghamhiddenhistoryteam.wordpress.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Nottingham Hidden History Team St Ann&#x27;s Well</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Nottingham Hidden History Team St Ann&#x27;s Well</span></span></span>

St Catherine’s Well at Newark offers a more archaeological example. Nottinghamshire’s Historic Environment Record lists it as a medieval holy well, gives its grid reference in Newark, and records the tradition that it was famous for healing leprosy. It also states that St Catherine’s chapel, once built over the well, has disappeared, and that the visible survival is now a moss-covered, concrete-walled landscaped pond in a private ornamental garden. This is a good example of how folklore can outlast the physical setting that once supported it.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://her.nottinghamshire.gov.uk/Monument/MNT14671/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: her.nottinghamshire.gov.uk">[her.nottinghamshire.gov.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">her.nottinghamshire.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nottinghamshire.gov.uk.</span></span></span>

Watnall Holy Well adds a small but memorable cure legend. Local accounts describe a plaque near the well telling of a bedridden boy whose recovery followed water being drawn from the well by a priest. The Northern Antiquarian notes this as a local piece of folklore recorded at the site, while the Nottingham Hidden History Team places the well in woodland near Trough Lane, close to modern retail development. Its power as a story comes partly from that contrast: a sacred spring surviving in memory beside an ordinary modern landscape.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://megalithix.wordpress.com/2014/09/13/holy-well-watnall/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: megalithix.wordpress.com">[The Northern Antiquarian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">megalithix.wordpress.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wordpress.com.</span></span></span>

Fairy material is much thinner in Nottinghamshire than in some western and northern counties, but it is not absent. One Nottinghamshire well tradition tells of fairies visible at Midsummer and a farmer who dug up a fairy ring near a well, after which the water became unavailable to him until he restored the ring. The story is a classic piece of moralised fairy folklore: disturb the otherworld’s place, and the everyday resource you rely on is withdrawn.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://insearchofholywellsandhealingsprings.wordpress.com/tag/nottinghamshire/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: insearchofholywellsandhealingsprings.wordpress.com">[holyandhealingwells]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">insearchofholywellsandhealingsprings.wordpress.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wordpress.com.</span></span></span>

## Witches, possession and useful magic

Nottinghamshire’s witchcraft history is better treated as belief culture and social history than as a parade of spooky tales. Early modern witch accusations grew out of fear, illness, religious tension, neighbourly conflict and attempts to explain misfortune. The local stories matter because they show how supernatural belief operated in ordinary communities, not just in famous national witch-hunt centres.

University of Nottingham Manuscripts and Special Collections gives a particularly useful East Midlands frame. It notes that powers ascribed to witches included spoiling food, flying, causing illness in people and livestock, and making crops fail. But it also stresses a crucial distinction: in Lincolnshire and Nottinghamshire, cunning men and women could be consulted for healing, folk medicine and divination, and were not always seen in the same way as harmful witches.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://sauonremweblog.blob.core.windows.net/%24web/The_Witches_of_East_Mids_-_Manuscripts_and_Special.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sauonremweblog.blob.core.windows.net">[sauonremweblog.blob.core.windows.net]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sauonremweblog.blob.core.windows.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Witches of East Mids</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Witches of East Mids</span></span></span>

That distinction changes how the reader should understand “witchcraft” in the county. A person accused of witchcraft might be feared as a source of harm, while another person using charms, divination or folk cures might be treated as useful until suspicion turned against them. The same University of Nottingham source gives the example of Anne Cook of Mattersey, presented in 1622 for soothsaying and for claiming to foretell people’s deaths. That is not a fairy-tale witch on a broomstick; it is a local woman whose alleged speech about death was unsettling enough to become a church matter.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://sauonremweblog.blob.core.windows.net/%24web/The_Witches_of_East_Mids_-_Manuscripts_and_Special.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sauonremweblog.blob.core.windows.net">[sauonremweblog.blob.core.windows.net]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sauonremweblog.blob.core.windows.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Witches of East Mids</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Witches of East Mids</span></span></span>

Nottingham itself also has stories of witchcraft, possession and fraudulent exorcism. LeftLion’s history of witchcraft in Nottingham highlights that, although Matthew Hopkins’ 17th-century witch-hunts are better known in East Anglia, Nottingham had its own high-profile incidents involving witchcraft, possession and exorcism. This local urban setting is important: supernatural belief was not confined to remote villages or forest edges. It could become part of city religious life, public controversy and courtroom culture.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://leftlion.co.uk/features/2020/10/witchcraft-in-nottingham-history/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: leftlion.co.uk">[LeftLion]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">leftlion.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Left Lion Out of Time: A History of Witchcraft in Nottingham</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Left Lion Out of Time: A History of Witchcraft in Nottingham</span></span></span>

Modern retellings of Nottinghamshire witchcraft often blur together accused witches, cunning folk, protective magic, Halloween entertainment and heritage tourism. A grounded county page should keep them separate. Trial records and church presentments are evidence of belief, accusation and authority. Cunning-folk traditions show how people sought help. Ghost tours and seasonal events show how later audiences turn fear into entertainment.

## Plough Monday and village performance

Nottinghamshire folklore is not only about supernatural beings. It also includes seasonal customs, especially the winter and early-year village performances associated with Plough Monday. These customs belonged to agricultural communities and often involved disguise, visiting houses, collecting money and performing rough comic drama.

The Master Mummers archive records a substantial Nottinghamshire tradition of Plough Bullock plays and related customs. Mansfield, Selston, East Retford, Lowdham, Shelford and other places appear in the indexed material. The Mansfield material includes versions of a Plough Monday or Plough Bullocks play with characters such as St George, Bold Slasher, the Doctor, Beelzebub, Molly Mop, Mickey Bent, Polly Flinders and the Rake. One Mansfield note says the custom was performed regularly up to about 1875, then declined, with later revival in the 1920s.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://mastermummers.org/notts/notts-mansfield.php" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: mastermummers.org">[Master Mummers]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">mastermummers.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on mastermummers.org.</span></span></span>

These plays are valuable because they show Nottinghamshire folklore as performance rather than just belief. A Plough Monday play is not a legend told about the past; it is an action carried from door to door. Its stock figures — hero, opponent, comic doctor, devilish or grotesque characters, cross-dressed or disorderly figures — belong to a wider English mumming tradition, but local names, routes, memories and performance details make each county version distinctive.

The custom also carried a social edge. In some accounts, performers expected a contribution, and refusal could bring nuisance or symbolic retribution. Master Mummers’ Lowdham material describes performers with paper finery and red-ochred faces, while other Nottinghamshire references mention plough trailing, Plough Bullockers, Morris dancers and a “Bessy”. Such details place the custom in the world of labourers, winter festivity and licensed disorder after the Christmas season.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.mastermummers.org/notts/notts-lowdham.php" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: mastermummers.org">[Master Mummers]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">mastermummers.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on mastermummers.org.</span></span></span>

For Nottinghamshire identity, these plays matter because they balance the county’s famous forest myth with village custom. Robin Hood belongs to international popular culture. Plough Monday belongs to local lanes, farm labour, house-visiting and remembered performance. Both are folklore, but they work at different scales.

## Black dogs, boggarts and smaller uncanny creatures

Compared with East Anglia’s Black Shuck or Yorkshire’s Barghest, Nottinghamshire’s black-dog folklore is sparse. That scarcity is itself interesting. It suggests that not every famous English folklore motif is evenly distributed, and that county folklore should not be padded by importing neighbouring material.

The clearest Nottinghamshire example is the Crow Lane black dog near South Muskham and Bathley. The At the Edge black-dog archive records a 1952 Nottingham County Library manuscript preserving the testimony of Mrs Smalley, then about 75. Her grandfather, born in 1804, was said to have seen a black dog trotting beside his pony and trap on Crow Lane between Southwell and Bathley; later, around 1915, her son Sidney reportedly saw the same dog while returning from dances in Newark by motorcycle.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.indigogroup.co.uk/edge/bdogfl.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: indigogroup.co.uk">[indigogroup.co.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">indigogroup.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">At the Edge archive: Black dogs folklore</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">At the Edge archive: Black dogs folklore</span></span></span>

That story has several classic black-dog features: a lane, night travel, repetition across generations, and an animal that cannot be treated as ordinary. Yet it lacks some of the more dramatic elements found elsewhere, such as fiery eyes, church attacks or explicit death omens. In Nottinghamshire, the black dog is more like a persistent road apparition than a fully developed hellhound.

Boggart material also appears in Nottinghamshire water folklore. The holy-wells survey mentions a Thurgarton boggart said to live in a dumble, with its booming voice later explained as a bittern. That is an excellent example of folklore and natural history sitting side by side: the story preserves a frightening local presence, while the explanation points to the uncanny soundscape of wetlands and wooded hollows.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.academia.edu/6249688/Holy_wells_and_healing_springs_of_Nottinghamshire" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: academia.edu">[Academia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">academia.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">PDF) Holy wells and healing springs of Nottinghamshire</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">PDF) Holy wells and healing springs of Nottinghamshire</span></span></span>

The same survey mentions the Clifton family’s harbinger fish, said to appear at times of death, and Girton’s bottomless Horsepool. These are not major county legends on the scale of Robin Hood, but they show how local families, pools and riverine places generated small, place-bound supernatural traditions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.academia.edu/6249688/Holy_wells_and_healing_springs_of_Nottinghamshire" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: academia.edu">[Academia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">academia.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">PDF) Holy wells and healing springs of Nottinghamshire</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">PDF) Holy wells and healing springs of Nottinghamshire</span></span></span>

## Caves, ghosts and modern haunted Nottinghamshire

Nottinghamshire’s ghost culture is especially strong around built heritage: halls, abbeys, caves, pubs and old city spaces. These stories are often less securely “old oral tradition” than Robin Hood ballads or Plough Monday records, but they are still part of modern folklore because they shape how people visit and talk about places.

Nottingham’s caves are central to this modern uncanny identity. European Heritage Days describes Nottingham as the “City of Caves” and notes that it has the UK’s largest network of caves, with more than 800 beneath the city streets. Such a landscape almost invites ghost stories, escape-room narratives and hidden-history tours, even when specific supernatural claims are late or promotional.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.europeanheritagedays.com/Story/Beneath-Our-Feet-the-story-of-Nottinghams-caves" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: europeanheritagedays.com">[European Heritage Days]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">europeanheritagedays.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on europeanheritagedays.com.</span></span></span>

Tourism sources now market “Haunted Nottinghamshire” through places such as Newstead Abbey, Clifton Hall, Kelham Hall, Nottingham caves and historic pubs. Visit Nottinghamshire’s haunted guide is explicit that this is a visitor-facing spooky itinerary, not a critical folklore archive. That does not make it worthless; it simply places the material in the category of modern retelling, heritage entertainment and paranormal tourism.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.visit-nottinghamshire.co.uk/blog/read/2022/09/haunted-nottinghamshire-b4860" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: visit-nottinghamshire.co.uk">[Visit Nottinghamshire]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">visit-nottinghamshire.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Visit Nottinghamshire Haunted Nottinghamshire</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Visit Nottinghamshire Haunted Nottinghamshire</span></span></span>

Clifton Hall is a good example of the modern haunted-house pattern. Local and paranormal sources describe a reputation for hauntings stretching back at least to its use as a school, with reports of apparitions, voices, banging and other disturbances. These accounts should be read as contemporary ghost tradition rather than verified history. Their value lies in showing how a prestigious house can gather stories of unease, especially when its architecture, age and changing uses make it feel socially and emotionally charged.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.hauntedrooms.co.uk/clifton-hall-nottingham" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: hauntedrooms.co.uk">[Haunted Rooms®]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">hauntedrooms.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on hauntedrooms.co.uk.</span></span></span>

Newstead Abbey, strongly associated with Lord Byron, has also acquired ghost traditions, including stories of a White Lady, a Goblin Friar and Byron’s dog Boatswain. These tales mix literary tourism, monastic memory, aristocratic ruin and the general British habit of attaching ghosts to old houses. They are memorable, but they are best presented as reported traditions and visitor lore rather than as evidence of a continuous medieval haunting.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.belvoir.co.uk/guides/nottingham-central/most-haunted-buildings-in-nottinghamshire-have-you-visited-any-of-these-places/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: belvoir.co.uk">[Belvoir]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">belvoir.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Belvoir Most Haunted Buildings in Nottinghamshire – Have you</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Belvoir Most Haunted Buildings in Nottinghamshire – Have you</span></span></span>

## Rivers, roads and the folklore of movement

Nottinghamshire’s folklore often attaches to routes and thresholds: forest roads, river crossings, lanes, wells, cave entrances and village visiting circuits. This is not accidental. Folklore thrives where people feel exposed, where one kind of space turns into another, or where ordinary rules are temporarily loosened.

Sherwood’s Great North Way gave the Robin Hood legend a travel corridor: merchants, pilgrims, officials and thieves could all be imagined moving through royal forest territory. Crow Lane gave the black dog a narrow repeated route. Plough Monday performers moved through villages, crossing household thresholds in disguise. Holy wells drew people out to the edge of town or parish for healing water. Nottingham’s caves invite descent below the ordinary street.

The River Trent adds a different kind of threshold. The holy-wells survey mentions the Aegir, the tidal bore of the Trent, as one of the county’s famed water features, with a name linked to a Norse sea figure. In folklore terms, the Aegir is not a personified Nottinghamshire fairy or monster so much as a dramatic natural event given mythic language. It shows how a real river phenomenon can become part of a county’s legendary vocabulary.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.academia.edu/6249688/Holy_wells_and_healing_springs_of_Nottinghamshire" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: academia.edu">[Academia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">academia.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">PDF) Holy wells and healing springs of Nottinghamshire</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">PDF) Holy wells and healing springs of Nottinghamshire</span></span></span>

This route-based pattern helps explain why Nottinghamshire folklore feels different from counties dominated by stone circles, giants or fairy hills. Its strongest stories are tied to movement through managed landscapes: the outlaw in the forest, the traveller on the lane, the villager visited by plough players, the sick person sent to the well, the modern visitor descending into caves.

## What is old tradition, and what is later invention?

A good Nottinghamshire folklore page needs to be honest about layers of evidence. The county’s stories are not all the same kind of thing.

Robin Hood has deep medieval roots in ballad tradition, but many familiar details are later. Sherwood Forest interpretation points to the mid-15th-century *Lyttell Gest* and early Merry Men figures, while also acknowledging centuries of debate over who Robin might have been. Later elements such as particular tourist sites, pageants, statues and interactive attractions belong to modern public folklore and heritage interpretation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://visitsherwood.co.uk/about-robin-hood/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: visitsherwood.co.uk">[Sherwood Forest - Where legends grow]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">visitsherwood.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Sherwood Forest</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Sherwood Forest</span></span></span>

Holy wells often depend on antiquarian, archaeological and local-history evidence. St Catherine’s Well at Newark has a Historic Environment Record entry and medieval classification, but the visible remains are limited and the chapel is gone. The wider wells survey repeatedly urges caution, noting uncertain sites, possible duplications and limited ritual evidence compared with some neighbouring counties.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://her.nottinghamshire.gov.uk/Monument/MNT14671/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: her.nottinghamshire.gov.uk">[her.nottinghamshire.gov.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">her.nottinghamshire.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nottinghamshire.gov.uk.</span></span></span>

Witchcraft material is strongest where it rests on church, manuscript or legal records. The University of Nottingham’s example of Anne Cook of Mattersey is valuable because it points to a dated churchwarden presentment, not just a later ghost story. By contrast, many modern witch-themed events and Halloween narratives are creative retellings inspired by early modern fears rather than direct continuations of old practice.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://sauonremweblog.blob.core.windows.net/%24web/The_Witches_of_East_Mids_-_Manuscripts_and_Special.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sauonremweblog.blob.core.windows.net">[sauonremweblog.blob.core.windows.net]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sauonremweblog.blob.core.windows.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Witches of East Mids</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Witches of East Mids</span></span></span>

Plough Monday plays are well supported by collectors, local memories and performance texts, although many surviving accounts come from the 19th and 20th centuries, often after customs had declined or were being revived. Their evidence is therefore partly documentary and partly revivalist, but still much firmer than many generic haunted-house claims.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://mastermummers.org/notts/notts-mansfield.php" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: mastermummers.org">[Master Mummers]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">mastermummers.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on mastermummers.org.</span></span></span>

Ghost stories and haunted attractions are the most fluid category. They may preserve older motifs, but they are often shaped by tourism, Halloween programming, local newspapers, paranormal groups and the atmosphere of historic buildings. That does not exclude them from folklore; it simply means they should be labelled as modern haunted tradition when the older evidence is thin.

## Nottinghamshire’s place in the wider UK folklore map

Nottinghamshire sits in the East Midlands, and its folklore naturally overlaps with Derbyshire, Lincolnshire, Leicestershire and Yorkshire. Plough plays cross county borders. Black dog lore is far stronger in neighbouring regions but has a small Nottinghamshire presence. Holy-well traditions invite comparison with Derbyshire and Lincolnshire, where well dressing and water customs are more prominent. Robin Hood crosses even more borders, with rival claims and related sites beyond Nottinghamshire, but Sherwood remains the county’s central claim.

The historic-county frame is useful here because older stories rarely match modern council lines. Nottingham city’s administrative separation does not remove it from the county’s legendary geography. Likewise, Sherwood’s older extent, the Great North Road corridor, river traditions and neighbouring county motifs all show that folklore travels by route, dialect, estate, parish, market and performance circuit as much as by modern boundary.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://wikishire.co.uk/wiki/Nottinghamshire" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wikishire.co.uk">[wikishire.co.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wikishire.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wikishire.co.uk.</span></span></span>

What makes Nottinghamshire distinctive is the balance between one world-famous legend and many smaller local survivals. Some counties are rich in fairy hills or megalithic giant tales; Nottinghamshire is richer in outlaw forest identity, village performance, healing springs, cunning-folk anxieties and modern haunted heritage. Its folklore is less about a single supernatural race or monster than about contested spaces: forest law, city caves, dangerous lanes, parish wells and houses that remember too much.

## Why the county’s folklore still matters

Nottinghamshire folklore remains alive because it gives people ways to feel the county’s past in ordinary places. Sherwood is not just woodland; it is a stage for justice and defiance. Nottingham Castle is not just a monument; it is the Sheriff’s imagined seat. A lost well in St Ann’s is not just an urban-history puzzle; it is a memory of healing water, pilgrimage, Robin Hood naming and neighbourhood identity. A Plough Monday text is not just an old script; it is evidence of villagers turning the agricultural year into performance.

The county’s legends also teach caution. They show how easily old tradition, antiquarian speculation, civic branding and modern tourism can be mistaken for one another. The strongest reading does not flatten those layers. It asks what kind of source stands behind each story, where the tale is located, who preserved it, and what the story does for the people who keep repeating it.

Nottinghamshire’s folklore is therefore best understood as a living map: Sherwood and Robin Hood at its centre, with wells, witches, plough plays, black dogs, boggarts, caves, ghosts and river lore forming smaller but important markings around the county. The result is not a generic “spooky county” identity, but a distinctive Midland folklore shaped by forest law, road travel, healing water, village custom, urban memory and the enduring appeal of the outlaw in the greenwood.

    if (cfg.mkrid) params.push('mkrid=' + encodeURIComponent(cfg.mkrid));
    if (cfg.campaign_id) params.push('campid=' + encodeURIComponent(cfg.campaign_id));
    params.push('mkevt=1');
    params.push('mkcid=1');
    params.push('toolid=' + encodeURIComponent(cfg.tool_id || '10001'));
    if (reference) params.push('customid=' + encodeURIComponent(reference).slice(0, 256));
    return url + joiner + params.join('&');
  }
  function domainLabel(marketId) {
    var cfg = markets[marketId] || markets[defaultMarket] || {};
    return cfg.domain_label || 'eBay';
  }
  function flagClass(marketId) {
    return 'fr-ebay-market-flag fr-ebay-market-flag--' + String(marketId || '').toLowerCase().replace(/_/g, '-').replace(/[^a-z0-9-]+/g, '');
  }
  function closeMenu(section) {
    var menu = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-menu]');
    var trigger = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-trigger]');
    var picker = section.querySelector('.fr-ebay-market-picker');
    if (menu) menu.hidden = true;
    if (trigger) trigger.setAttribute('aria-expanded', 'false');
    if (picker) picker.classList.remove('fr-ebay-market-picker--open');
  }
  function availableMarkets(section) {
    var select = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-select]');
    if (select) {
      return Array.prototype.slice.call(select.options)
        .map(function (option) { return option.value; })
        .filter(function (marketId) { return !!markets[marketId]; });
    }
    return Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]'))
      .map(function (button) { return button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button'); })
      .filter(function (marketId) { return !!markets[marketId]; });
  }
  function applyMarket(section, marketId, persist) {
    var available = availableMarkets(section);
    if (available.indexOf(marketId)< 0) marketId = available[0] || defaultMarket;
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-localized-link]')).forEach(function (link) {
      var query = link.getAttribute('data-ebay-query') || '';
      var reference = link.getAttribute('data-ebay-reference') || '';
      link.setAttribute('href', buildUrl(query, reference, marketId));
    });
    var select = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-select]');
    if (select && available.indexOf(marketId) >= 0) select.value = marketId;
    var selectedText = marketId;
    if (select && select.selectedIndex >= 0) selectedText = select.options[select.selectedIndex].text;
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-selected-market-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = selectedText;
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-selected-market-flag], [data-ebay-trigger-market-flag]')).forEach(function (flag) {
      flag.className = flagClass(marketId);
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-trigger-market-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = selectedText;
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-domain-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = domainLabel(marketId);
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-option]')).forEach(function (button) {
      button.setAttribute('aria-selected', button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-option') === marketId ? 'true' : 'false');
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      var active = button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button') === marketId;
      button.setAttribute('aria-pressed', active ? 'true' : 'false');
    });
    section.setAttribute('data-ebay-selected-market', marketId);
    if (persist) {
      try { if (window.localStorage) localStorage.setItem('phoenix-ebay-market', marketId); } catch (err) {}
    }
    closeMenu(section);
  }
  sections.forEach(function (section) {
    if (section.getAttribute('data-ebay-localized-init') === '1') return;
    section.setAttribute('data-ebay-localized-init', '1');
    var available = availableMarkets(section);
    var marketId = defaultMarket;
    if (window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation && window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation.inferMarket) {
      marketId = window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation.inferMarket(available, {
        storageKey: 'phoenix-ebay-market',
        defaultMarket: defaultMarket
      });
    } else if (available.indexOf(defaultMarket)< 0) {
      marketId = available[0] || defaultMarket;
    }
    var select = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-select]');
    if (select) {
      select.addEventListener('change', function () {
        applyMarket(section, select.value, true);
      });
    }
    var trigger = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-trigger]');
    var menu = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-menu]');
    if (trigger && menu) {
      trigger.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
        event.stopPropagation();
        var expanded = trigger.getAttribute('aria-expanded') === 'true';
        menu.hidden = expanded;
        trigger.setAttribute('aria-expanded', expanded ? 'false' : 'true');
        var picker = trigger.closest('.fr-ebay-market-picker');
        if (picker) picker.classList.toggle('fr-ebay-market-picker--open', !expanded);
      });
      Array.prototype.slice.call(menu.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-option]')).forEach(function (button) {
        button.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
          event.stopPropagation();
          applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-option'), true);
        });
      });
      document.addEventListener('click', function () { closeMenu(section); });
    }
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      button.addEventListener('click', function () {
        applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button'), true);
      });
    });
    applyMarket(section, marketId, false);
  });
})();
